title: Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal
---
Adopted in 1989 and entering into force in 1992, the treaty was created in response to high-profile scandals during the 1980s, where "toxic ships" sailed from Europe and North America to dump hazardous chemical waste in Africa and Latin America due to cheaper disposal costs.

The convention regulates global waste management through three legally binding strategic goals:

- **Reduction of Hazardous Waste:** Countries must minimize the generation of toxic waste at its source, encouraging eco-friendly manufacturing and domestic recycling infrastructure.

- **Restriction of Transboundary Movements:** Hazardous waste cannot be exported unless the exporting country lacks the technical capacity or facilities to dispose of it safely. Any export requires the Prior Informed Consent (PIC) of the importing country—meaning a nation must be formally notified and explicitly agree in writing before toxic cargo can enter its borders.

- **Strict Control over Disposal:** It mandates that hazardous and other regulated wastes must be managed and disposed of in an environmentally sound manner, preventing leakage into soil, oceans, and groundwater.

The treaty covers a massive spectrum of materials, classified into hazardous wastes (based on toxicity, flammability, or corrosiveness) and "other wastes" that require special consideration:

- **Chemical and Medical Waste:** Industrial chemical products, heavy metals (like mercury and lead), and clinical or pharmaceutical waste from hospitals.

- **E-Waste (Electronic Waste):** Outdated computers, smartphones, televisions, and circuit boards containing dangerous components.

- **Household and Plastic Waste:** In 2019, a historic amendment added **plastic waste** to the convention's strict regulations, legally restricting the global dumping of non-recyclable plastic scrap.


As global trade routes adapt, the Basel Convention has implemented massive legislative upgrades to combat illegal smuggling and the global climate crisis:

- **The Basel Ban Amendment Realization:** This crucial amendment has reached comprehensive implementation across signatory states, completely banning developed countries (OECD, EU, and Liechtenstein) from exporting any hazardous wastes to developing nations for _any_ reason, including recycling.

- **E-Waste Tracking Upgrades:** New global guidelines implemented in **January 2025** have plugged legal loopholes regarding electronic waste. Previously, companies smuggled obsolete electronics under the guise of "second-hand goods for reuse." The current rules require all electronic shipments—functional or not—to pass rigorous Prior Informed Consent (PIC) validation before leaving port.

- **The Global South Enforcement Initiatives:** Throughout 2025 and 2026, the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) and Interpol have launched joint maritime port tracking programs. These initiatives utilize advanced digital manifest tracking to intercept hundreds of illegal containers carrying contaminated plastics and hazardous lithium-ion batteries back to their western countries of origin.
