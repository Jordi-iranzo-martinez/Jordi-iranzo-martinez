
Aquifex (from Latin *aqua* "water" and *facere* "to make," meaning "water-maker") is a genus of hyperthermophilic, rod-shaped bacteria in the phylum Aquificota (formerly Aquificae).

Thriving at temperatures between 85 °C and 95 °C near hydrothermal vents and volcanic hot springs, it represents one of the most heat-tolerant and earliest-diverging lineages within the domain [[Bacteria]].

Cells are Gram-negative, non-spore-forming rods measuring 2 to 6 μm in length and about 0.5 μm in diameter. They are motile via flagella and frequently form large multicellular aggregates of up to 100 individual cells.

Unlike many hyperthermophiles that belong to the domain Archaea, Aquifex is a true bacterium. It inhabits marine hydrothermal systems, shallow submarine vents, and terrestrial hot springs—first isolated off northern Iceland and northern Sicily.

Aquifex species are strict autotrophs that fix carbon dioxide via the reductive citric acid (reverse TCA) cycle. They derive energy from inorganic electron donors such as molecular hydrogen (H₂), elemental sulfur (S⁰), or thiosulfate (S₂O₃²⁻).

When oxidizing hydrogen with oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor, their respiration produces liquid water (H₂O), inspiring the genus name.

Phylogenetic analyses place Aquifex near the base of the bacterial tree, making it a key model organism for studying early life on Earth and the origin of thermophily.

Its heat-stable enzymes—particularly hydrogenases and DNA-modifying enzymes—are actively studied for hydrogen fuel cells, biocatalysis, and molecular biology applications.
