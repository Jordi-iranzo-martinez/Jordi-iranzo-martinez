
Green sulfur bacteria (phylum Chlorobiota, family Chlorobiaceae) are a group of strictly anaerobic, photoautotrophic bacteria that carry out anoxygenic photosynthesis using reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors. Unlike plants and cyanobacteria, they do not use water and never produce oxygen during photosynthesis.

They are Gram-negative bacteria shaped as spheres, ovals, or curved rods. Almost all species are nonmotile, though some possess gas vesicles to adjust their depth in the water column, and one genus (_Chloroherpeton_) moves by gliding.

They possess unique, sac-like light-harvesting organelles called chlorosomes attached to their cytoplasmic membrane. Packed with up to 250,000 molecules of bacteriochlorophyll (_c_, _d_, or _e_) and carotenoids, these structures are the largest and most efficient light-harvesting antennas known in nature.

When oxidizing hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), they deposit globules of elemental sulfur outside their cells, which can be oxidized further to sulfate if sulfide runs out.

They rely primarily on hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), elemental sulfur, or thiosulfate to drive electron transport, though certain species can also use molecular hydrogen (H₂) or ferrous iron (Fe²⁺).

Light energy captured by the chlorosomes funnels through the Fenna-Matthews-Olson (FMO) protein complex into a Type-I reaction center (P840). Instead of the Calvin cycle used by plants, they fix carbon dioxide using the reverse tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle.

Many species are diazotrophic, meaning they can convert atmospheric nitrogen gas (N₂) into biologically usable ammonia.

Because their chlorosomes capture photons with extreme efficiency, green sulfur bacteria thrive at the deepest limits of the photic zone where almost no light penetrates.

They inhabit anoxic, sulfide-rich zones such as the bottom layers of stratified lakes (chemoclines), meromictic water bodies like the Black Sea, sulfur hot springs, salt marsh sediments, and deep-sea hydrothermal vents.

In nature, they drive the anaerobic carbon, sulfur, and nitrogen cycles. In biotechnology, their high tolerance for toxic sulfide makes them useful for desulfurizing biogas and industrial wastewater.
