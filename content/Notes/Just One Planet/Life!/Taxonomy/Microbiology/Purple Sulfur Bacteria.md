
Purple sulfur bacteria (order Chromatiales, within the class Gammaproteobacteria) are a group of anaerobic or microaerophilic phototrophic bacteria that perform anoxygenic photosynthesis using reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors. 

They get their distinctive purple, red, brown, or orange hues from high concentrations of carotenoid pigments alongside bacteriochlorophyll a or b.

They are Gram-negative bacteria shaped as rods, spheres, spirals, or vibrios. Many species are motile by means of polar flagella, allowing them to swim toward optimal light and sulfide gradients.
  
They house their photosynthetic apparatus inside extensive infoldings of the cytoplasmic membrane (forming vesicles, tubules, or lamellae).

When oxidizing hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), most species (family *Chromatiaceae*) store refractile globules of elemental sulfur inside their cells, whereas a smaller group (family *Ectothiorhodospiraceae*) deposits sulfur outside.

They use hydrogen sulfide, elemental sulfur, thiosulfate, or molecular hydrogen as electron donors. Unlike green sulfur bacteria, they fix carbon dioxide through the Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle.

While many are strict anaerobes, several species are tolerant of low oxygen levels and can switch to chemolithotrophic or chemoheterotrophic growth in the dark when oxygen is present.

They use a Type-II reaction center (P870) similar to Photosystem II in plants, requiring reverse electron flow to generate NADH for carbon fixation.

Because they lack chlorosomes and need higher light intensity than green sulfur bacteria, they sit directly above green sulfur bacteria in stratified water columns—where sunlight still reaches anoxic, sulfide-rich layers.

They frequently form dense, visible pink or purple blooms in stagnant ponds, salt marshes, sulfur springs, lagoons, and meromictic lakes.

By oxidizing toxic hydrogen sulfide into non-toxic elemental sulfur and sulfate without consuming oxygen, they act as natural biological filters in aquatic sediments and wastewater treatment lagoons.
