
Archaea (singular: archaeon) are single-celled, prokaryotic microorganisms that constitute one of the three primary domains of life on Earth, alongside Bacteria and Eukarya. 

Although they look superficially similar to bacteria under a microscope because they lack a cell nucleus, archaea have a fundamentally distinct evolutionary history and unique biochemistry—in many ways sharing closer molecular similarities with eukaryotes (including humans) than with bacteria.

Unlike bacteria and eukaryotes (whose cell membranes are built from fatty acids linked to glycerol by ester bonds), archaeal membranes are made of branched isoprenoid chains linked by ether bonds. This structure provides exceptional stability against extreme temperatures, acidity, and salinity.

Archaea completely lack peptidoglycan, the polymer that forms bacterial cell walls. Instead, their walls are typically composed of a proteinaceous surface layer (**S-layer**) or pseudopeptidoglycan.

While archaea have a single circular chromosome like bacteria, the molecular machinery they use to transcribe DNA into RNA and translate RNA into proteins closely resembles that of eukaryotes, including the use of histone proteins to package DNA in many species.

Ecological Diversity and Extremophiles:

- **Extremophiles**
	- **Halophiles** live in hypersaline waters like the Dead Sea and Great Salt Lake.
	- **Thermophiles and hyperthermophiles** thrive in boiling hot springs and deep-sea hydrothermal vents at temperatures above 80–120 °C (176–248 °F).
	- **Acidophiles and alkaliphiles** survive in extreme pH environments, such as volcanic pools or soda lakes.

- **Mesophiles**: modern genomic research has revealed that archaea are not restricted to extremes; they are abundant in ordinary oceans, soils, wetlands, and even inside the human gut and skin microbiome (mesophiles)

- **Methanogens**: a major group of anaerobic archaea produces methane gas as a metabolic byproduct (methanogens). Found in swamps, rice paddies, wastewater digesters, and the digestive tracts of ruminants (like cows) and humans, thhey play a critical role in the global carbon cycle.

Until 1977, archaea were classified as unusual bacteria ("archaebacteria"). Microbiologist Carl Woese and George Fox used ribosomal RNA sequencing to prove they formed a completely separate branch of life.

Current evolutionary evidence (specifically around the Asgard archaea superphylum) strongly supports the theory that eukaryotic cells originated from an ancient symbiosis between an archaeal host cell and a bacterium that evolved into the mitochondrion.
