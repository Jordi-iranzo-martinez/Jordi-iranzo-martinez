
Chytridiomycetes is a class of fungi belonging to the phylum Chytridiomycetes (colloquially known as chytrids). They represent one of the most primitive lineages of fungi and are unique for being the only true fungi that produce zoospores—motile reproductive cells equipped with a single, whip-like posterior flagellum that allows them to swim through water.

While many chytrids are harmless decomposers, certain parasitic species within this class have caused catastrophic environmental impacts, making them a major focus of modern evolutionary and conservation biology.

- **Microscopic Structure:** Most species are microscopic. Their bodies (thalli) can be unicellular or consist of simple, root-like structures called rhizoids used to anchor themselves and absorb nutrients.

- **Flagellated Zoospores:** This is their defining evolutionary trait. Unlike higher fungi (like mushrooms or molds) which rely on wind or animals to disperse non-motile spores, chytrids require a fluid film or open water body to swim and reproduce.

- **Chitinous Cell Walls:** Despite their primitive lifestyle and aquatic nature, they possess cell walls made of chitin, proving they are true fungi rather than protists.

- **Nutritional Roles:** They can be saprotrophic (breaking down tough organic materials like chitin, keratin, and cellulose) or parasitic (infecting plants, algae, invertebrates, and vertebrates).
