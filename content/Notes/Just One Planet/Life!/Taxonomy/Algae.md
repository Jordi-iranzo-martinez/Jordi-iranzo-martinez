
Algae (singular: alga) are a diverse, informal group of predominantly aquatic, photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that range from microscopic single-celled phytoplankton to giant multicellular seaweeds like kelp. 

Unlike land plants, algae lack true roots, stems, leaves, vascular tissues, and enclosed reproductive structures, yet they perform roughly half of all photosynthesis on Earth, generating a massive share of the planet's oxygen and forming the base of aquatic food webs.

Because "algae" is a polyphyletic term spanning several distinct evolutionary lineages, scientists classify them primarily by their photosynthetic pigments, cell wall chemistry, and storage products:

- **Green algae** ([[Chlorophyta]] and [[Charophyta]]). Containing chlorophyll a and b, green algae are the closest evolutionary relatives of land plants. They include single-celled species (like Chlamydomonas), colonial forms (Volvox), filamentous freshwater species (like Spirogyra), and marine seaweeds (such as sea lettuce, Ulva).

- **Red algae** ([[Rhodophyta]]). Colored red or purplish by phycoerythrin pigments—which absorb blue light that penetrates deep water—red algae live mostly in marine environments and include species harvested for nori (used in sushi), agar, and carrageenan.

- **Brown algae** ([[Phaeophyceae]]). Exclusively multicellular and mostly marine, brown algae owe their olive-brown hue to the pigment fucoxanthin. They include massive giant kelp (Macrocystis), which forms underwater forests, as well as Sargassum and rockweeds (Fucus).

- **[[Diatoms]]** (Bacillariophyta). Microscopic, single-celled algae encased in intricate, glass-like cell walls made of silica (frustules). Abundant in both oceans and freshwaters, diatoms alone produce an estimated 20–25% of Earth's oxygen.

- **[[Dinoflagellates]]** (Dinophyceae). Flagellated single-celled plankton famous for bioluminescence, coral symbiosis (zooxanthellae), and harmful algal blooms ("red tides").

*(Cyanobacteria, often called "blue-green algae," are photosynthetic bacteria rather than true eukaryotic algae, though they share similar ecological niches.)*

Marine phytoplankton and macroalgae absorb huge quantities of atmospheric carbon dioxide and produce approximately 50% to 70% of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.

Algae are widely used in human nutrition (spirulina, chlorella, kelp, nori), food thickeners (alginates, agar, carrageenan), agricultural fertilizers, cosmetics, wastewater treatment, and renewable biofuel research.

When agricultural runoff or warm temperatures cause excessive nutrient buildup (eutrophication), rapid algal overgrowth can deplete dissolved oxygen as the algae die and decompose, creating aquatic "dead zones" or releasing potent toxins (Harmful algal blooms: HABs)
