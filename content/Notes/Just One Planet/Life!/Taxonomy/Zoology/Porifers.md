
Porifers (from Latin *porus* "poro" and *ferre* "to bear") -commonly known as sponges-, is a phylum of basal, multicellular aquatic animals characterized by porous bodies, a cellular level of organization without true tissues or organs, and a specialized water-canal system for filter feeding.

Unlike more complex animals, sponges lack true tissues, organs, a digestive tract, a nervous system, and muscles. Their cells remain relatively independent and totipotent (able to change function), embedded in a gelatinous matrix called the mesohyl.

Water enters the body through numerous microscopic inhalant pores (ostia), passes through internal canals and chambers, and exits through one or more large exhalant openings (oscula)

Flagellated cells lining the internal chambers beat continuously to generate water currents, trapping bacteria, plankton, and dissolved organic matter for intracellular digestion.

Structural support is provided by microscopic crystalline spicules (composed of calcium carbonate or silica) and/or a flexible network of collagenous protein fibers called spongin

 They are mostly hermaphroditic, reproducing sexually (releasing sperm into the water to fertilize eggs, producing free-swimming larvae) as well as asexually through budding, fragmentation, or dormant survival pods (gemmules) in freshwater species.
