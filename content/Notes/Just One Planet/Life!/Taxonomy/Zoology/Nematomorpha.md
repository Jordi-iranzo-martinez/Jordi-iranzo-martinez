
Nematomorpha (commonly known as horsehair worms, hairsnakes, or Gordian worms) is a phylum of parasitic, thread-like animals superficially resembling nematodes (roundworms).

*Their common name "Gordian worm" refers to their habit of tangling themselves into tight, complex knots resembling the legendary Gordian knot, particularly when mating in water.*

Comprising around 350 to 2,000 estimated species, their larvae are internal parasitoids of arthropods—such as crickets, grasshoppers, beetles, mantises, cockroaches, and crustaceans—while their adults are free-living in freshwater or marine environments.

Adult horsehair worms are extraordinarily thin—typically only 1 to 3 millimeters (0.04–0.12 in) in diameter—yet commonly grow 10 to 50 centimeters (4–20 in) long, with some tropical species exceeding 2 meters (6.5 ft).

*Their common name "Gordian worm" refers to their habit of tangling themselves into tight, complex knots resembling the legendary Gordian knot, particularly when mating in water.*

Unlike nematodes, nematomorphs lack a functional digestive tract as adults (their mouth and gut are degenerated or non-functional). During their parasitic larval stage inside a host, they absorb nutrients directly through their body wall (_cuticle_), and as free-living adults they do not eat at all.

Free-living adults mate in ponds, streams, puddles, or wet soil, laying millions of eggs in gelatinous strings. Microscopic larvae hatch and either encyst on streamside vegetation or are eaten by aquatic insect larvae (such as mayflies or mosquitoes), which act as intermediate hosts when they emerge onto land and are eaten by larger terrestrial insects.

Once inside a definitive host (such as a cricket or praying mantis), the larva grows coiled tightly within the insect's body cavity, eventually occupying almost its entire interior while carefully avoiding vital organs so the host stays alive.

When the worm reaches adulthood and needs to return to water to reproduce, it alters the host's central nervous system—producing proteins that mimic the insect's own neurotransmitters—compelling a normally terrestrial insect to seek out light and jump or walk into water, where the adult worm bursts out.

Despite historical folklore claiming horsehair worms spontaneously generated from horse tail hairs in water troughs, they cannot infect humans, dogs, cats, livestock, or plants.

By driving terrestrial insects into streams and rivers, nematomorphs inadvertently provide a major seasonal food source for freshwater fish such as trout and salmon.
