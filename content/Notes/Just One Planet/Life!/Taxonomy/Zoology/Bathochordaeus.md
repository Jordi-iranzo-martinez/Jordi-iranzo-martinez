
Bathochordaeus is a genus of free-swimming marine tunicates commonly known as giant larvaceans.

Belonging to the phylum [[Chordata]], these deep-sea animals are famous for secreting intricate, transparent mucus houses over a meter wide to filter food from the water.

 While most larvaceans measure less than 1 cm (0.4 inches), Bathochordaeus species grow between 3 and 10 cm (1.2 to 4 inches) long. They consist of a small trunk containing internal organs and a long, muscular tail stiffened by a notochord (a primitive precursor to a backbone).

    - ***Bathochordaeus charon*** First described in 1900 and rediscovered more than a century later.

    - ***Bathochordaeus stygius**** A widespread midwater species.

    - ***Bathochordaeus mcnutti***. Discovered in 2017 and distinguished by a bright blue margin along its tail.

Specialized cells on the animal's head secrete a complex, balloon-like structure of mucus that can exceed 1 meter (3.3 feet) in diameter. It contains two nested filters: a coarse outer mesh that blocks debris too large to eat, and a fine inner filter that funnels microscopic food particles straight into the animal's mouth.

Suspended inside the center of its mucus mansion, the larvacean beats its tail rhythmically to pump huge volumes of seawater through the filters, feeding on marine snow, bacteria, algae, and organic detritus.

Because the delicate filters clog quickly—often within a single day—the larvacean regularly abandons its mucus house, swims out through an escape hatch, and inflates a brand-new one within hours.

Giant larvaceans filter water faster than almost any other zooplankton. When they discard their heavy, food-packed mucus houses, those structures sink rapidly to the ocean floor. This process transports massive amounts of carbon from upper waters into the deep sea, playing a vital role in regulating Earth's carbon cycle.

*[[Plankton]], [[Ocean]], [[Deep Ocean]]*

Sinking mucus houses provide a major feast for benthic (seafloor) organisms. Researchers have also discovered that these discarded houses trap and carry significant quantities of microplastics from midwater depths down to the seabed.
