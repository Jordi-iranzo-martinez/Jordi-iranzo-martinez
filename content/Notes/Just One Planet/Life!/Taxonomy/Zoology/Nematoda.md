
Nematoda (commonly known as nematodes or roundworms) is one of the largest and most abundant animal phyla on Earth.

Comprising tens of thousands of described species—and potentially millions yet undiscovered—these unsegmented, cylindrical worms inhabit almost every ecosystem on the planet, from deep ocean trenches and polar ice to soil and inside other living organisms.

Unlike earthworms (which belong to the segmented phylum Annelida), nematodes have smooth, thread-like bodies tapered at both ends with no external segments or appendages.

Their outer body is covered by a flexible, non-cellular collagen cuticle that protects them from harsh environments and must be shed (molted) as they grow, placing them in the superphylum Ecdysozoa alongside insects and crustaceans.

They possess a simple, tube-within-a-tube body plan featuring a mouth at one end and an anus at the other, suspended inside a fluid-filled cavity called a pseudocoelom.

Because they have only longitudinal muscles and no circular muscles, nematodes cannot crawl smoothly; instead, they move with a characteristic thrashing, whip-like S-shaped motion.

The vast majority of nematodes are microscopic (often under 1 millimeter long) and live freely in soil or aquatic sediments, feeding on bacteria, fungi, algae, and organic matter to recycle vital nutrients.

Some plant-parasitic species (like root-knot nematodes) attack crop roots and cause major agricultural damage. Conversely, predatory nematodes are used in organic farming as biological pest control against harmful insects.

Several well-known species infect animals and humans, including _Ascaris lumbricoides_ (giant intestinal roundworm), pinworms (_Enterobius_), hookworms, and _Dirofilaria immitis_ (dog heartworm).

A tiny, free-living soil nematode named C. elegans was the first multicellular organism to have its entire genome sequenced and its complete nervous system mapped, making it indispensable in genetics, aging, and neurobiology research.
