
Bryophyta (commonly known as mosses) is a division of small, non-vascular land plants that lack true roots, stems, leaves, and vascular tissues (xylem and phloem).

With over 12,000 known species, they are among the earliest lineages of plants to colonize land and play a crucial role in regulating moisture and preventing soil erosion across global ecosystems.

Because they lack specialized water-conducting tubes, mosses absorb water and nutrients directly through their thin, leaf-like surfaces. This restricts them to a small, low-growing size—typically 1 to 10 cm (0.4 to 4 inches) tall—and keeps them largely dependent on damp, shaded environments.

Mosses anchor themselves to soil, rocks, or tree bark using hair-like filaments called rhizoids. Unlike true roots, rhizoids serve purely for attachment rather than absorbing water or nutrients from deep underground.

Unlike flowering plants or ferns, the familiar green, leafy moss carpet is the haploid gametophyte generation. After fertilization, it produces a tall, slender stalk (seta) topped by a spore-bearing capsule (the diploid sporophyte), which relies directly on the green gametophyte for nutrition.

Mosses reproduce via spores rather than seeds or flowers. Male reproductive organs release flagellated sperm that must swim through a thin film of rainwater or dew to reach the female egg.

Mosses cannot actively regulate their internal water content. Instead, many species can dry out completely during droughts—entering a state of dormancy—and resume photosynthesis within minutes of being rehydrated by rain.

As pioneer species, mosses colonize bare rock and degraded soil, trapping windblown dust and breaking down stone surfaces to create fertile soil for larger plants. Their dense mats act like sponges that soak up heavy rainfall and prevent runoff.

Peat mosses (genus Sphagnum) form vast northern peatlands and bogs that store roughly 30% of the world's soil carbon—more than all the world's rainforests combined—making them vital allies in regulating Earth's climate.
