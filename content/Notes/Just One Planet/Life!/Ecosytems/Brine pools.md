
Brine pools (also known as underwater lakes or deep-sea brine lakes) are dense, hypersaline bodies of water that collect in depressions on the ocean floor. Because their salinity is typically three to eight times greater than the surrounding seawater, the heavy brine does not mix with the ocean above it—creating a distinct surface boundary complete with visible "shorelines," ripples, and waves beneath the sea.

[[Extreme halophiles]]

Millions of years ago, shallow seas evaporated, leaving behind thick layers of salt buried beneath seafloor sediments. When tectonic shifts or mud volcanoes expose these salt deposits, seawater dissolves the salt, forming a super-dense brine that sinks into seafloor basins.

Brine pools are most famously found in the Gulf of [[Mexico]] (including the well-known _"Jacuzzi of Despair"_), the [[Red Sea]], the [[Mediterranean Sea]], and along the [[Antarctica]]'s shelf.

The brine inside these pools contains virtually no dissolved oxygen (anoxic) and is often saturated with toxic hydrogen sulfide and methane. Fish, crabs, or deep-sea eels that accidentally swim into a brine pool quickly suffer toxic shock, suffocate, and become pickled—preserved intact on the pool floor for years.

Despite the lethal interior, the "shores" of brine pools host dense, thriving communities of extremophile bacteria and specialized seep mussels (Bathymodiolus). 

These mussels harbor symbiotic bacteria in their gills that convert dissolved methane and hydrogen sulfide into energy through chemosynthesis—sustaining entire ecosystems in pitch-black depths without sunlight.
