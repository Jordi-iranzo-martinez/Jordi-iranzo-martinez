
Kelp refers to large, fast-growing marine brown algae (seaweeds) belonging to the order Laminariales. 

*[[Algae]]*

Despite looking like towering underwater plants, kelp are actually protists (stramenopiles) that form dense, cathedral-like underwater ecosystems known as kelp forests along cool, nutrient-rich coastlines worldwide.

- **Plant-Like Structure Without True Roots.** Instead of roots, stems, and leaves, kelp consists of three main parts:
    - **Holdfast**: A root-like tangle of fibers that anchors the kelp tightly to rocky seafloors without absorbing nutrients.
    - **Stipe**: A flexible, stem-like stalk that supports the organism in strong ocean currents.
    - **Blades and Pneumatocysts**: Leaf-like fronds that absorb sunlight and nutrients directly from seawater, often kept afloat near the sunlit surface by gas-filled bladders called pneumatocysts.

- **Fastest-Growing Marine Organism.** Under ideal conditions with cold, nutrient-rich upwelling water and abundant sunlight, giant kelp (_Macrocystis pyrifera_) can grow up to 60 cm (2 feet) in a single day, reaching towering heights of 45 meters (150 feet) or more.

Kelp forests thrive in temperate and polar coastal waters (such as the [[Pacific]] coast of [[North America]] and [[South America]], [[South Africa]], southern [[Australia]], and northern [[Europe]]), providing food and shelter for thousands of marine species—including sea otters, seals, sea lions, rockfish, abalone, crabs, and migrating whales.

Dense kelp canopies absorb wave energy, protecting shorelines from storm erosion. Through rapid photosynthesis, kelp also absorbs large amounts of dissolved carbon dioxide, contributing to marine blue carbon sequestration and reducing localized ocean acidification.

Kelp forests are famously vulnerable to overgrazing by sea urchins. When keystone predators like sea otters or sunflower sea stars disappear, urchin populations explode and mow down lush forests into barren rocky seafloors known as "urchin barrens."
