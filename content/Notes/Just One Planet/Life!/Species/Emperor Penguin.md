The emperor penguin (*Aptenodytes forsteri*) is the tallest and heaviest of all living penguin species and is endemic to [[Antarctica]]. 

*[[Birds]]*

Famous for being the only animal to breed during the harsh Antarctic winter, it relies on extreme physiological adaptations and cooperative social behaviors to survive temperatures that drop below −50 °C (−58 °F).

Adults stand 110 to 130 cm (3.6 to 4.3 feet) tall and weigh between 22 and 45 kg (48 to 100 lbs) depending on the season. Both sexes look similar, featuring a black head and back, a white belly, a pale yellow upper breast, and bright yellow-orange ear patches.

They possess four layers of scale-like, waterproof feathers—the highest feather density of any bird—alongside a thick layer of subdermal blubber and specialized countercurrent blood vessels in their flippers and feet to minimize heat loss.

Emperor penguins dive deeper and longer than any other bird, capable of reaching depths of over 535 to 565 meters (1,750+ feet) and staying submerged for up to 20 minutes while hunting Antarctic silverfish, krill, and squid.

Unlike all other penguins, emperor penguins breed on stable, land-fast sea ice during the darkest, coldest months of the Antarctic winter (starting in May–June):

After laying a single egg, the female transfers it carefully to the male and returns to the open ocean to feed. The male balances the egg on top of his feet beneath a warm flap of feathered skin (the brood pouch) for 65 to 75 days without eating, losing up to half his body weight.

To survive blizzards and winds exceeding 150 km/h, thousands of males pack tightly together into shifting huddles. Birds on the freezing outer edge slowly rotate toward the warm interior (where temperatures can exceed 35 °C) so every penguin shares the shelter.

When the female returns in spring, she regurgitates food for the newly hatched chick while the exhausted male finally heads to sea to feed. Older chicks gather in protective groups called *crèches* before moulting their grey down into waterproof adult plumage by December.
