The grizzly bear (*Ursus arctos horribilis*), also known as the North American brown bear or silvertip bear, is a large subspecies of brown bear inhabiting western and northern [[North America]]. 

Famous for its muscular shoulder hump, dish-shaped facial profile, and silver-tipped fur, it is one of North America's most iconic apex predators.

*[[Mammalia]]*

Adult males typically weigh between 180 and 360 kilograms (400 to 800 pounds), while females weigh between 130 and 200 kilograms (290 to 440 pounds). Coastal brown bears (which feed heavily on salmon) can grow even larger, exceeding 450 kilograms (1,000 pounds).

Unlike American black bears, grizzlies have a prominent hump of muscle between their shoulders that powers their front limbs for digging, along with long, slightly curved front claws measuring 5 to 10 centimeters (2 to 4 inches).

Their fur ranges in color from blond and light brown to nearly black, often featuring silver- or white-tipped guard hairs that give them their "grizzled" appearance.

Despite their bulk, grizzly bears can sprint at speeds of up to 56 km/h (35 mph) over short distances.

Once roaming across much of western North America from [[Alaska]] to [[Mexico]], grizzlies today live primarily in Alaska, western [[Canada]] (British Columbia, Alberta, Yukon, Northwest Territories), and isolated ecosystems in the northern United States (such as Yellowstone and Glacier National Parks).

Although classified as carnivores, up to 80% to 90% of an inland grizzly's diet consists of plants, including berries, roots, tubers, grasses, and nuts (such as whitebark pine seeds). They also eat insects (like army cutworm moths), rodents, carrion, newborn elk or moose calves, and spawning salmon where available.

In autumn, grizzlies enter a period of intense feeding called hyperphagia to build up fat reserves before digging dens on mountain slopes, where they hibernate for 5 to 7 months without eating, drinking, urinating, or defecating.

Grizzlies are generally solitary outside of mating season, rich feeding sites (like salmon streams), and mothers raising cubs—which stay with their mother for two to three years.
