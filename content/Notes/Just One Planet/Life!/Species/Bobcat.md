
The bobcat (*Lynx rufus*) is a medium-sized wild cat native to North America, ranging from southern [[Canada]] across most of the contiguous United States and down into Mexico.

*Named for its short, "bobbed" tail, it is the most abundant and adaptable wild feline in North America—roughly twice the size of an average domestic house cat.*

[[Mammalia|Mammalia]]

Adults typically measure 65 to 105 cm (26 to 41 inches) in body length, stand about 50 cm (20 inches) tall at the shoulder, and weigh between 6 and 14 kg (13 to 30 lbs).

Their dense fur varies from gray-brown to reddish-brown (which gives them their species name, _rufus_), patterned with dark spots and bars. They feature distinctive tufts of fur framing their cheeks (a facial ruff) and short black tufts on the tips of their pointed ears.

Their stubby tail—measuring only 10 to 18 cm (4 to 7 inches)—has dark bands on top, a black tip, and a bright white underside, which flashes as a visual signal when walking or running.

Bobcats thrive in remarkably diverse habitats, including dense forests, deserts, swamps, mountains, and even suburban greenbelts on the edges of major cities.

Mostly active at dawn and dusk (crepuscular), bobcats rely on acute eyesight and hearing to stalk prey before launching a powerful pounce up to 3 meters (10 feet) in a single bound. Their diet consists mainly of rabbits and hares, alongside rodents, squirrels, birds, and occasionally small deer.

Bobcats live alone outside of the late-winter mating season, marking their home ranges with claw marks, urine, and scat. Females raise litters of 1 to 6 kittens in secluded dens inside hollow logs, rock crevices, or dense brush.

While closely related to the Canada lynx (_Lynx canadensis_), bobcats are generally smaller with shorter legs, smaller paws (less suited for deep snow), shorter ear tufts, and a tail tip that is black on top and white underneath (whereas a Canada lynx's tail tip is completely black all the way around).
