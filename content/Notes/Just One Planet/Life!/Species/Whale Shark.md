The whale shark (*Rhincodon typus*) is a slow-moving, filter-feeding carpet shark and the largest known extant fish species on Earth. Despite its massive size and name, it is a docile pelagic shark rather than a whale, feeding primarily on plankton and small nekton across tropical and warm-temperate oceans.

[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Taxonomy/Zoology/Fishes|Fishes]]

Confirmed adult individuals commonly reach 10 to 12 meters (33–39 feet) in length and weigh around 15 to 20 tonnes, with exceptional verified specimens exceeding 18 meters (59 feet).

Its dark bluish-gray dorsal skin is covered in a distinctive checkerboard pattern of pale yellow or white spots and stripes. Like human fingerprints, each shark's spot pattern is unique and is used by marine biologists for photo-identification tracking.

Features a broad, flattened head with a wide terminal mouth (up to 1.5 meters across) containing over 300 rows of tiny vestigial teeth that play no role in feeding, flanked by five massive pairs of gill slits.

One of only three known filter-feeding shark species (alongside the basking shark and megamouth shark), it feeds via ram filtration and active suction, straining zooplankton, krill, fish eggs, crab larvae, and small schooling fish through specialized spongy filter pads inside its gill arches.

Inhabits open tropical and subtropical waters worldwide (typically between 30°N and 35°S), migrating thousands of kilometers to seasonal feeding aggregations (such as Ningaloo Reef in Australia, Isla Mujeres in Mexico, and the Galápagos Islands).

Whale sharks are ovoviviparous—embryos hatch from eggs inside the mother's uterus and are born live at roughly 55–64 cm (22–25 in) long (a single pregnant female studied in 1995 carried over 300 pups). They mature slowly (around 25–30 years) and are estimated to live 80 to 130 years.

Listed as Endangered on the [[IUCN]] Red List and included in CITES Appendix II and the Convention on Migratory Species (CMS). Major threats include vessel strikes in busy shipping lanes, accidental bycatch in fishing nets, marine plastic pollution, and unregulated tourism pressure.
