Coelacanths (order Coelacanthiformes) are an ancient order of lobe-finned fish (Sarcopterygii) once believed to have gone extinct alongside the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago, until a living specimen was famously rediscovered off the coast of [[South Africa]] in 1938. 

Often referred to as a "Lazarus taxon" (and colloquially a "living fossil"), they represent one of the closest living fish relatives to terrestrial tetrapods.

While over 100 fossil species are known from the Devonian through the Cretaceous periods, only two living species (both in the genus Latimeria) survive today:

- **West Indian Ocean Coelacanth** (_Latimeria chalumnae_). Rediscovered in December 1938 by South African museum curator **Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer** from a trawler catch off the Chalumna River. It has deep metallic blue scales flecked with white spots and inhabits steep volcanic slopes and submarine caves along East Africa (Comoros, South Africa, Tanzania, Mozambique, and Madagascar).

- **Indonesian Coelacanth** (_Latimeria menadoensis_). Discovered in **1997** in a fish market in Manado Tua, Sulawesi, Indonesia. It features a distinct brownish coloration and lives in deep waters off Sulawesi and Papua.

Unlike modern ray-finned fish, coelacanths have paired pectoral and pelvic fins supported by internal bones and muscles that move in an alternating, tetrapod-like gait, plus an extra epicaudal lobe on their three-lobed tail.

They possess a unique hinged joint in the skull that allows the upper jaw to lift upward while feeding, and a thick, oil-filled hollow notochord instead of a solid bony vertebral column (their name derives from Greek koilos "hollow" and akantha "spine").

An electrosensory organ located in the snout that detects weak electrical fields generated by prey in dark deep-sea environments.

Coelacanths live at depths of 150–700 meters (500–2,300 feet), grow up to 2 meters (6.5 feet) long, and can live up to 100 years. They are ovoviviparous with one of the longest known gestation periods among vertebrates—estimated at around 5 years—giving birth to fully formed pups.

With an estimated global population of fewer than 500–1,000 individuals, the West Indian Ocean coelacanth is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List and protected under CITES Appendix I, primarily threatened by accidental bycatch in deep-set gillnets. L. menadoensis is listed as Vulnerable.
