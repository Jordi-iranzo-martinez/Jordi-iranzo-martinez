The terrestrial hermit crab (family Coenobitidae) is an amphibious [[Crustacea]]n that lives its adult life on land but remains biologically tethered to the sea. 

Found along tropical coastlines worldwide, these highly social scavengers are famous for their unique lifestyle of reclaiming abandoned snail shells to protect their soft, vulnerable bodies.

[[Coast Ecosystem]]

Unlike true crabs, hermit crabs do not grow their own hard protective carapaces over their abdomens. Instead, their bodies are highly specialized to occupy foreign architecture:

Their tail end is soft, asymmetric, and spirally twisted to curl tightly inside the interior chambers of empty gastropod (marine snail) shells. They lock themselves inside using specialized hooked appendages at the tip of their tail.

As a hermit crab grows, its current shell becomes too tight. It must constantly search for a larger model.

When several crabs meet around an empty shell, they often perform a fascinating social ritual known as a vacancy chain. The crabs line up in order of size, from largest to smallest. When the largest crab moves into the new, grand shell, the next crab in line quickly slips into the abandoned shell, triggering a rapid domino-effect housing upgrade for the entire group.
