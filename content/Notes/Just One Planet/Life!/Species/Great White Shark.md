The great white shark (*Carcharodon carcharias*) is the world's largest known predatory fish and one of the ocean's most recognizable apex predators. Renowned for its torpedo-shaped body, rows of serrated teeth, and ambush hunting tactics, it inhabits cool and temperate coastal waters around the globe.

*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Taxonomy/Zoology/Fishes|Fishes]]*

*They get their name from their stark white underbelly, which blends into the bright ocean surface when viewed from below. Their slate-grey or brownish back helps them disappear against the dark seafloor when viewed from above.*

Adult great whites typically measure 4.5 to 6 meters (15 to 20 feet) in length and weigh between 680 and 1,100 kg (1,500 to 2,400 lbs), with females growing significantly larger than males. They are slow-growing and long-lived, capable of reaching 70 years or more.

Unlike most fish, great whites are regionally endothermic—they possess a specialized network of blood vessels (rete mirabile) that retains heat generated by their swimming muscles. This keeps their body temperature warmer than the surrounding water, allowing them to hunt effectively in chilly seas.

Their jaws hold multiple rows of triangular, coarsely serrated teeth that continuously replace themselves throughout their lives. They also possess acute hearing, an extraordinary sense of smell, and specialized electroreceptors in their snout (ampullae of Lorenzini) that detect the faint electrical fields produced by moving prey.

Great whites live in coastal and offshore waters across the globe, with major populations off the coasts of [[California]] and [[Mexico]], the northeastern [[United States of America]], [[South Africa]], southern [[Australia]], [[New Zealand]], and the [[Mediterranean Sea]]. They also undertake vast transoceanic migrations across open oceans.

As apex predators, great white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias) play a vital role in maintaining the health, balance, and resilience of marine ecosystems around the world. Removing them triggers cascading effects that can destabilize entire coastal food webs.

- Preventing Overgrazing
- Maintaining Biodiversity
- Changing Prey Behavior
- Protecting Seagrass and Kelp Forests
- Culling the Weak and Sick
- Preventing Disease Outbreaks
- Scavenging and Decomposition
- Connecting Distant Habitats

Juvenile great whites feed mainly on fish, squid, and rays. As they mature, their diet shifts toward energy-rich marine mammals, including seals, sea lions, dolphins, and whale carcasses.



Hunting primarily from below, a great white will surge upward at speeds reaching 56 km/h (35 mph) to strike unsuspecting prey near the surface, sometimes launching its entire body completely out of the water in a dramatic leap known as breaching.

*Despite being responsible for more recorded unprovoked shark bite incidents than any other species, great whites do not actively hunt humans. Most incidents are cases of mistaken identity or exploratory "test bites" in murky water, and fatalities are exceptionally rare.*
