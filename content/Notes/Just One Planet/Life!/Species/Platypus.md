The platypus (*Ornithorhynchus anatinus*), sometimes referred to as the duck-billed platypus, is a semiaquatic, egg-laying mammal endemic to eastern [[Australia]] and [[Tasmania]]. 

Alongside the four species of echidna, it is one of the only surviving members of the ancient mammalian order Monotremata.

*[[Mammalia|Mammalia]]*

When European naturalists first examined a preserved specimen in 1799, they suspected it was a hoax stitched together from different animals. It combines a broad, rubbery duck-like bill, webbed feet, a flat beaver-like tail used for steering and fat storage, and dense waterproof fur.

Unlike placental or marsupial mammals, female platypuses lay one to three leathery eggs in an underground riverbank burrow. After the hatchlings emerge roughly 10 days later, the mother nurses them with milk that oozes directly through specialized pores in her skin (as she has no nipples).

When diving underwater for up to two minutes at a time, a platypus tightly shuts its eyes, ears, and nostrils.

The platypus is one of the very few venomous mammals. Adult males possess a hollow spur on the inside of each hind ankle connected to a venom gland. While not lethal to humans, the venom causes excruciating, long-lasting pain and is primarily used when competing with rival males during the breeding season.

Under ultraviolet light, the platypus's brown coat glows a vivid bluish-green—a rare trait among mammals whose exact evolutionary purpose is still being studied.

Instead of relying on sight, it sweeps its sensitive bill from side to side across the riverbed to detect prey using electroreception and mechanoreception. Thousands of specialized receptors in its bill sense the faint electrical impulses generated by the muscle contractions of bottom-dwelling invertebrates, such as freshwater shrimp, crayfish, insect larvae, and worms.

Adult platypuses lack true teeth and a functional acid-secreting stomach. Instead, they scoop up gravel along with their prey and use horny, keratinized pads inside their bill to grind food before passing it directly into the intestine.

Platypuses inhabit freshwater rivers, streams, and lakes along Australia's eastern coast, from tropical Queensland down to cold alpine creeks in Tasmania.
