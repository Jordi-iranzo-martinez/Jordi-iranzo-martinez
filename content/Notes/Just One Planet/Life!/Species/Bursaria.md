Bursaria (from Latin *bursa*: "purse" or "pouch") is a genus of giant, single-celled predatory protists belonging to the phylum Ciliophora (class Colpodea).

These freshwater ciliates are famous among microscopists for their massive size, distinctive scoop-shaped feeding cavity, and unusually complex nuclear structure.

The best-known species, _Bursaria truncatella_, typically measures between 0.5 and 1.5 millimeters in length, making it one of the largest single-celled ciliates on Earth. Under good lighting, individual cells are large enough to be seen swimming in pond water with the naked eye as tiny, fast-moving white specks.

The front end of the cell is truncated and opens into a deep, funnel-like oral cavity (peristome) that curves toward the left side of the body. This cavity is lined with a dense band of specialized ciliary membranelles that beat rhythmically to generate strong water currents.

Like all ciliates, Bursaria has two types of nuclei: tiny micronuclei for sexual reproduction and a massive, ribbon-like macronucleus that winds through the cytoplasm like a long worm, controlling everyday cellular metabolism.

Because it lives in freshwater, its outer cytoplasm contains dozens of small contractile vacuoles that continuously pump out excess water to prevent the cell from bursting. They are found worldwide in clean, still, or slow-moving freshwater habitats rich in organic matter and smaller protists, including forest ponds, marshes, ditches, and vegetated lake margins.

Bursaria is an active filter-feeder and carnivore that sweeps smaller microorganisms directly into its pouch-like mouth. Its diet includes smaller ciliates (such as Paramecium and Colpidium), flagellates, rotifers, and unicellular algae.

Because a 1-millimeter cell is relatively heavy for a single-celled organism, Bursaria sinks quickly when it stops swimming. Biologists frequently study B. truncatella to understand negative gravitaxis—how a single cell without a nervous system senses Earth's gravity and automatically steers itself upward toward oxygen-rich surface waters.

When pond conditions deteriorate or food becomes scarce, Bursaria sheds its cilia and secretes a thick, double-layered protective wall to form a dormant resting cyst, surviving drought or freezing temperatures until conditions improve.
