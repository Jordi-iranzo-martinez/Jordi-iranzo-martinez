The polar bear (*Ursus maritimus*) is a hypercarnivorous bear native to the [[Arctic]] Circle and the largest extant land carnivore on Earth (rivaled in size only by the Kodiak brown bear). 

Classified scientifically as a marine mammal because it spends most of its life on Arctic sea ice, it is uniquely adapted to survive in some of the coldest environments on the planet.

Adult males (boars) typically measure 2.4 to 3 meters (8–10 ft) in length and weigh 350 to 700 kg (770–1,540 lbs), while adult females (sows) weigh roughly 150 to 300 kg (330–660 lbs).

Beneath its seemingly white coat, a polar bear actually has jet-black skin that absorbs heat from the sun. Its fur consists of a dense, insulating undercoat topped by transparent, hollow guard hairs that scatter and reflect visible light (making the bear look white or cream against snow) while trapping warm air close to the body.

Polar bears have a 10-centimeter (4 in) layer of blubber, small ears and tails to minimize heat loss, broad paws up to 30 centimeters (12 in) wide lined with tiny bumps (_papillae_) for traction on ice, and partial webbing between their toes. Capable swimmers, they can paddle continuously for dozens of miles at speeds up to 10 km/h (6 mph).

Polar bears inhabit the Arctic sea ice, islands, and coastlines of [[Canada]], [[Greenland]] (Denmark), [[Norway]] (Svalbard), [[Russia]], and the United States ([[Alaska]]). Their primary prey consists of ringed seals and bearded seals, which they hunt by waiting patiently at seal breathing holes (_aglus_) in the ice or stalking seals resting on ice floes.

Although polar bears share their Arctic habitat with walruses, healthy adult walruses are massive and armed with lethal tusks, making them dangerous targets; polar bears generally only hunt walrus calves, injured individuals, or scavenge carcasses, and occasionally trigger fatal stampedes when approaching crowded coastal walrus haul-outs.

Aside from mothers raising cubs, polar bears live solitary lives across vast home ranges. Pregnant females dig snow dens on land or sea ice in autumn, entering a state of dormancy (_walking hibernation_) where they give birth to one to three tiny, blind cubs weighing about 600 grams (1.3 lbs) before emerging in spring.

Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (with an estimated global population of roughly 22,000 to 31,000 bears across 19 subpopulations), polar bears face an existential threat from Arctic sea ice loss. Because the Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the global average, earlier spring ice breakup and later autumn freeze-up shorten their seal-hunting season, forcing bears onto land for longer fasting periods and pushing some into coastal communities in search of food.
