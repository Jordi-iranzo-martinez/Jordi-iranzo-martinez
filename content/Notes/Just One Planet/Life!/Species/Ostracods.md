
Ostracods (class Ostracoda, commonly known as seed shrimp) are a large, ancient class of microscopic crustaceans enclosed within a hinged, clam-like bivalve shell.

Typically measuring around 1 millimeter in length—though ranging from 0.2 mm to the deep-sea giant Gigantocypris at over 30 mm—they inhabit nearly every aquatic environment on Earth, including oceans, freshwater lakes, ponds, temporary puddles, and even damp forest litter.

- **Bivalve carapace.** Their entire unsegmented body is protected inside two calcified or chitinous valves connected by a dorsal hinge. When threatened, an adductor muscle snaps the shell tightly shut, making them look like tiny seeds or miniature clams.

- **Swimming antennae.** They extend feathery antennae and specialized legs out through a narrow gap in their shell to swim smoothly through the water or scuttle across sediment.

- **Bioluminescence ("blue tears").** Certain marine species, such as _Vargula hilgendorfii_ (known as sea fireflies), produce a bright blue bioluminescent glow used both to deter predators and in elaborate courtship displays.

- **Drought-resistant eggs.** Many freshwater ostracods reproduce via parthenogenesis (all-female populations) and lay tough, desiccation-resistant eggs that can survive in dry mud for decades until rain returns.

Because their shells are made of low-magnesium calcite, ostracods have left the richest fossil record of any arthropod group, stretching back roughly 485 million years to the Ordovician period.

Paleontologists and geologists use fossilized ostracods to date rock layers, reconstruct ancient climates, and measure historical water salinity and temperature.

In freshwater aquariums and ponds, ostracods are harmless detritivores that graze on algae, biofilm, and decaying plant matter, while also serving as a nutritious live food source for small fish and fry.
