Horseshoe crabs (family Limulidae) are ancient marine arthropods that have remained virtually unchanged for over 445 million years, earning them the title of "living fossils." 

Despite their name, they are not true crabs or crustaceans—they are chelicerates, more closely related to spiders, scorpions, and ticks than to crabs or lobsters.

Their armored body consists of a broad, horseshoe-shaped front shell (prosoma), a hinged middle abdomen (opisthosoma) lined with movable spines, and a long, rigid tail spine (telson). Contrary to popular myth, the telson is harmless and is used strictly as a rudder while swimming and as a lever to flip themselves upright if overturned by waves.

Horseshoe crabs have two large compound eyes on the sides of their shell for finding mates, plus eight additional light-sensing organs scattered across their back, underside, and tail that help regulate circadian rhythms and sensitivity to moonlight.

Underneath their shell, they possess five pairs of walking legs (the first pair modified into pincers called chelicerae for moving food to their central mouth) and **book gills**—thin, page-like membranes that absorb oxygen underwater and allow them to survive on land for days as long as the gills remain moist.

Only four species survive today, along [[North America]]'s Atlantic and Gulf coasts (_Limulus polyphemus_), and across South and Southeast [[Asia]].

- Atlantic horseshoe crab (_Limulus polyphemus_) 
- *Tachypleus tridentatus*
- *Tachypleus gigas*
- *Carcinoscorpius rotundicauda*

Unlike human blood, which uses iron-based hemoglobin and turns red, horseshoe crab blood uses hemocyanin—a copper-based oxygen-carrying protein that turns bright blue when exposed to air.

Their blood contains specialized immune cells called amebocytes that instantly clot around even microscopic traces of bacterial endotoxins. Since the 1970s, pharmaceutical companies have harvested horseshoe crab blood to produce Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL), the global gold standard used to test vaccines, intravenous drugs, and medical implants for bacterial contamination. (Note: Synthetic alternatives like recombinant Factor C [rFC] are increasingly replacing wild blood harvesting.)

Every spring during high full-moon and new-moon tides, hundreds of thousands of horseshoe crabs crawl onto sandy beaches to lay millions of tiny green eggs in the sand.

These nutrient-rich eggs serve as a critical food source for migrating shorebirds—most notably the endangered red knot (*Calidris canutus*), which stops along Delaware Bay during its 15,000-kilometer migration from South America to the Arctic to double its body weight on horseshoe crab eggs.

Listed as Vulnerable (Atlantic species) and Endangered (tri-spine horseshoe crab) on the IUCN Red List, populations face threats from coastal habitat loss, overharvesting for eel and whelk fishing bait, and biomedical bleeding mortality.
