
House dust mites (primarily *Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus* and *Dermatophagoides farinae*) are microscopic, eight-legged arachnids closely related to ticks and spiders. 

Measuring only about 0.2 to 0.3 millimeters—too small to see with the naked eye—they live in household dust worldwide and are one of the most common triggers of year-round allergies and asthma.

[[Arachnids]]

Unlike bed bugs or fleas, dust mites do not bite, sting, or burrow into human skin. Instead, they feed on flakes of dead skin shed daily by humans and pets, along with microscopic fungi that grow on skin scales.

They do not drink water; they absorb moisture directly from the air through specialized glands on their bodies. Because of this, they thrive in warm (20–25°C / 68–77°F), humid environments (relative humidity above 50–60%) and die off quickly in dry climates.

They congregate deep inside soft fabrics that trap moisture and skin flakes, including mattresses, pillows, upholstered furniture, carpets, curtains, and stuffed toys.

Allergic reactions are not caused by bites, but by inhaling proteins found in dust mite fecal pellets and decomposing body fragments. A single mite produces roughly 20 waste pellets per day.

Exposure commonly causes sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, itchy/watery eyes, postnasal drip, and—in people with asthma—wheezing, coughing, and shortness of breath, often worsening at night or right after waking up.
