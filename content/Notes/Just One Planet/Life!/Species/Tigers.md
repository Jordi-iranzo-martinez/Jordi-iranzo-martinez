
The tiger (*Panthera tigris*) is the largest cat species in the world and one of Asia's most iconic apex predators. It is instantly recognizable by its orange coat with dark vertical stripes, a pattern as unique to each individual as a human fingerprint.

*[[Carnivores]]*

Adult males can measure over 3 meters (10 feet) in total length, including the tail, and weigh between 180 and over 250 kg (400 to 550+ lbs), especially in northern populations like the Siberian tiger. They possess massive muscular builds and canine teeth up to 8 cm (3 inches) long.

Their dark stripes break up their body outline against tall grasses and dappled forest shadows, allowing them to stalk prey undetected. Unlike most cats, tigers love the water. They frequently bathe in rivers and lakes to cool off and can easily swim across wide rivers or hunt in flooded mangrove swamps.

Tigers live solitary lives and mark vast home ranges using scent markings, tree scratches, and loud roars. They only come together briefly to mate or when a mother is raising her cubs.

They hunt mostly at dawn and dusk, relying on stealth and a sudden burst of speed to overpower large prey such as deer, wild boar, gaur, and water buffalo.

Tigers inhabit a wide range of Asian environments, from the snowy boreal forests of the Russian Far East to the tropical jungles, grasslands, and mangrove forests of [[India]] and Southeast [[Asia]].

The tiger is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Over the 20th century, tigers lost more than 90% of their historical range due to habitat destruction, poaching, and human-wildlife conflict.

Thanks to dedicated conservation programs in countries like India, Nepal, and Russia, wild tiger numbers have slowly rebounded in recent years to over 5,500 individuals globally. However, three regional populations—the Bali, Javan, and Caspian tigers—went extinct in the 20th century.
