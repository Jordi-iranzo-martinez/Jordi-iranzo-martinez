Desmidiales (commonly known as desmids) are an order of microscopic, mostly unicellular freshwater green algae renowned for the striking geometric symmetry of their cells.

Belonging to the division Charophyta—the algal group most closely related to land plants—this order includes roughly 40 genera and 5,000 to 6,000 species.

Most desmid cells are divided into two identical halves called semicells. These mirror-image halves are joined at the center by a narrow bridge called an isthmus, where the cell's nucleus is located.

Their cellulose cell walls contain tiny pores that secrete mucilage, allowing the algae to glide slowly across underwater surfaces. Many species feature intricate spines, deep lobes, or granules that give them star-like, crescent, or discoid shapes.

Unlike many other green microalgae, desmids have no flagella at any stage of their life cycle. Each semicell holds at least one large, bright-green chloroplast responsible for photosynthesis.

Most commonly, desmids reproduce by dividing their nucleus and splitting cleanly at the isthmus. Each parent semicell then regenerates a brand-new mirror half to restore its full symmetry.

When environmental conditions become harsh, two neighboring cells fuse their cellular contents (protoplasts) to form a thick-walled resting spore called a zygospore. This spore can survive prolonged droughts or freezing temperatures before germinating.

Desmids thrive primarily in still, clear, slightly acidic, and nutrient-poor freshwaters, such as peat bogs, mountain tarns, and wetlands rich in *Sphagnum* moss.

Because they are highly sensitive to pollution and nutrient runoff (eutrophication), ecologists frequently monitor desmid diversity to assess the ecological health and conservation status of freshwater habitats.

Famous examples include *Micrasterias* (flat, deeply lobed, star-shaped cells), *Closterium* (elongated, crescent-shaped cells lacking a central constriction), and *Cosmarium* (the largest genus, characterized by two rounded or oval semicells).
