
Pelomyxa is a genus of giant amoebae (phylum Amoebozoa, class Archamoebae) that live in the mud and bottom sediments of stagnant freshwater ponds and lakes

Best known through its flagship species, *Pelomyxa palustris*, it is a remarkable organism in cell and evolutionary biology due to its massive size and its lack of standard eukaryotic organelles like mitochondria.

While most amoebae are microscopic, _Pelomyxa palustris_ typically measures 0.5 to 5 millimeters in length—occasionally reaching up to 8 mm—making it clearly visible to the naked eye as a sluggish grey or brownish droplet gliding across pond mud.

A single _Pelomyxa_ cell can contain anywhere from a few dozen to thousands of nuclei, depending on its size and growth stage.

It lacks functional mitochondria, peroxisomes, and a conventional Golgi apparatus. To compensate for the absence of mitochondria and generate energy in oxygen-depleted environments, it hosts endosymbiotic bacteria (including methanogenic archaea) inside its cytoplasm that perform essential metabolic functions.

Unlike classic amoebae that extend multiple pseudopods in different directions, _Pelomyxa_ usually moves as a single, broad cylindrical wave (monotactic form) driven by internal cytoplasmic streaming, featuring a rear bulb covered in microvilli called a **uroid**.

It thrives in the muddy bottoms of still ponds, marshes, and ditches rich in decaying organic matter and very low oxygen levels (microaerophilic to anaerobic conditions).

As it glides over sediment, it engulfs food particles via phagocytosis, feeding primarily on algae (such as diatoms and desmids), decaying plant debris, and small microorganisms.

It reproduces mainly asexually through plasmotomy—a process in which the giant multinucleate cell divides directly into two or more multinucleate daughter cells without synchronized nuclear division—though flagellated stages and dormant cysts also occur in its life cycle.

*For decades, biologists believed Pelomyxa was a primitive "living fossil" representing an early stage of eukaryotic evolution before cells acquired mitochondria. Modern genetic studies overturned this idea, proving that its ancestors did possess mitochondria and that Pelomyxa lost them secondarily as an adaptation to anaerobic sediment life.*
