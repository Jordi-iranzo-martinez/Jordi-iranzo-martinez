
The African cherry (*Prunus africana*), also known as pygeum or red stinkwood, is an evergreen forest tree native to the mountain regions of sub-Saharan Africa and Madagascar, renowned for its medicinal bark and ecological value.

It is a large canopy tree in the rose family (Rosaceae) capable of growing 30 to 40 meters (100 to 130 feet) tall. It features dark, rough, fissured bark, glossy elliptical leaves with reddish stalks, and small, fragrant greenish-white flowers.

Its small, cherry-like fruits ripen from green to reddish-purple. While birds, monkeys, and gorillas rely on them for food, the fruits are very bitter to humans, and the seeds and young shoots contain cyanogenic glycosides that can be toxic if ingested.

It thrives in Afromontane tropical forests at elevations between 900 and 3,400 meters (3,000 to 11,000 feet) across central, eastern, and southern Africa, as well as Madagascar and islands in the Gulf of Guinea.

The tree's bark contains bioactive phytosterols and triterpenes. Extracts—commonly sold under the name _pygeum_—are widely exported to Europe and North America to formulate herbal supplements for managing symptoms of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) (enlarged prostate) and urinary tract inflammation.

Across local communities, bark and leaf preparations have long been used to treat fevers, malaria, stomachaches, chest infections, and wounds. Its dense, reddish-brown hardwood is also prized for making tool handles, wagons, and furniture.

Because commercial demand for its bark led to widespread, unsustainable stripping of wild trees—often killing them—_Prunus africana_ is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List and protected under CITES Appendix II, which strictly regulates international trade in its bark and extracts.
