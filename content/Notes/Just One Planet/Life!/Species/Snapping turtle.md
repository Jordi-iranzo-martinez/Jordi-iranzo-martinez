
Snapping turtles (family Chelydridae) are large, prehistoric-looking freshwater turtles native to the Americas, best known for their powerful hooked jaws, muscular build, and defensive snapping strike when threatened on land. 

[[Reptiles]]

The family comprises two main extant genera: the common snapping turtle (Chelydra) and the much larger alligator snapping turtle (Macrochelys).

- Common Snapping Turtle (*Chelydra serpentina*)
- Alligator Snapping Turtle (*Macrochelys temminckii*)

Unlike most turtles, snapping turtles have a very small bottom shell (plastron) and cannot pull their head or limbs inside for protection. In water, they are shy and swim away; on land, they compensate for their vulnerability with a long, flexible neck and rapid defensive lunges.

Both species possess a thick tail nearly as long as their carapace, lined with raised bony plates along the top.

They spend almost their entire lives in muddy-bottomed ponds, lakes, marshes, and slow-moving rivers, coming ashore primarily to lay eggs. In the wild, they commonly live 30 to 50 years, with alligator snapping turtles capable of exceeding 70–100 years.
