Bats (order Chiroptera) are the only mammals capable of true, sustained flight.

*[[Mammalia]]*

*The order name Chiroptera comes from Greek words meaning "hand-wing."* 

Making up roughly 20% of all classified mammal species worldwide—with over 1,400 known species—they are found on every continent except Antarctica and play indispensable roles in global ecosystems.

*[[Europe]], [[Asia]], [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Wonderful Places/Africa|Africa]], [[North America]], [[South America]], [[Oceania]]*

A bat's wing is anatomically similar to a human hand with vastly elongated finger bones connected by a thin, flexible, double-layered skin membrane called the patagium.

Bats vary dramatically in size—from the Kitti's hog-nosed bat (bumblebee bat), the world's smallest mammal at just 2 grams (0.07 oz), to giant flying foxes (megabats) with wingspans reaching up to 1.7 meters (5.6 feet).

Specialized locking tendons in their feet automatically clench when they relax, allowing bats to hang effortlessly upside down while sleeping without using any muscular energy.

Contrary to the popular saying "blind as a bat," all bats can see—and large fruit bats actually rely primarily on sharp eyesight and smell rather than sonar.

Most microbats navigate and hunt insects in total darkness using echolocation. They emit high-frequency ultrasonic calls through their mouths or noses and listen to the returning echoes with large, intricately folded ears to map out the exact size, distance, speed, and texture of objects in mid-air.

Diverse Diets and Ecological Importance

- **Insectivores** (Pest Control). Around 70% of bat species eat insects. A single little brown bat can eat up to 1,000 mosquito-sized insects in an hour, saving farmers billions of dollars annually in crop protection and reducing insect-borne diseases. ([[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Taxonomy/Zoology/Insects|Insects]])

- **Frugivores** (Seed Dispersers). Fruit bats eat ripe fruit and disperse seeds across vast distances in flight, making them vital reforestation agents in tropical rainforests.

- **Nectarivores** (Pollinators). Nectar-feeding bats use long tongues to pollinate over 500 species of night-blooming plants, including bananas, mangoes, guavas, cacao, and the **agave plant** (used to make tequila).

- **Carnivores and Vampire Bats**. A handful of specialized species eat frogs, fish, or small birds, while only three species (all native to the Americas) are vampire bats that feed exclusively on blood—mostly from livestock and birds—using heat sensors in their noses and anticoagulants in their saliva.

Despite their small size and fast metabolism, bats live extraordinarily long lives compared to other small mammals, with several species regularly exceeding 30 to 40 years in the wild.

Bat populations face serious pressures from habitat loss, wind turbine collisions, roost disturbance during winter hibernation, and white-nose syndrome—a devastating fungal disease that has killed millions of hibernating bats in North America.
