A pika (genus *Ochotona*) is a small, mountain-dwelling mammal closely related to rabbits and hares (order Lagomorpha), rather than rodents.

*[[Mammalia]]*

Native to cold, rocky regions of [[Asia]] and [[North America]], pikas are famous for their round, hamster-like appearance, high-pitched alarm whistles, and habit of harvesting wildflowers to store as "haypiles" for winter.

Like rabbits, pikas have two pairs of upper incisors that grow continuously. They have compact, egg-shaped bodies, short limbs, rounded ears, thick fur (even on the soles of their feet for traction on snow and rocks), and no visible external tail.

Most species measure only 15 to 23 centimeters (6 to 9 inches) long and weigh between 120 and 350 grams (4 to 12 ounces).

Often called "whistling hares," pikas communicate using sharp, high-pitched squeaks or calls to warn neighbors of predators (such as hawks, weasels, and coyotes) and to defend their territory.

Unlike marmots or ground squirrels, pikas do not hibernate. Instead, they remain active all winter underneath the insulating snowpack.

Throughout late summer, pikas make hundreds of trips a day to gather grasses, herbs, and wildflowers, laying them out in the sun to dry before storing them in rocky crevices as winter food reserves. They even prioritize plants with natural toxins that act as preservatives to keep the hay from rotting until winter.

Pikas inhabit high-altitude boulder fields (_talus_ slopes) and alpine meadows across western North America and mountainous areas of Central and East Asia (such as the Tibetan Plateau and [[Himalayas]]).

Because they are adapted to freezing temperatures with a dense fur coat and a high resting body temperature (around 40 °C / 104 °F), pikas can fatally overheat if exposed to temperatures above 25 °C (78 °F) for even a few hours. As mountains warm, many populations are forced to retreat to higher elevations.
