The American cockroach (*Periplaneta americana*) is the largest species of common household cockroach and a widespread urban pest. It actuallly originated in tropical Africa and the Middle East before spreading globally via 17th-century trade ships. In parts of the United States.

Adults typically measure between 3.5 and 5 cm (1.4 to 2 inches) in length. They have glossy reddish-brown bodies with a distinct pale-yellow or tan figure-eight band on the shield behind their head (pronotum).

Both males and females have fully developed wings. While capable of short, gliding flights—especially in warm, humid climates—they primarily rely on their exceptionally fast running speed.

Among common pest cockroaches, Periplaneta americana has the longest life cycle. Nymphs take roughly 6 to 12 months (and 6 to 14 molts) to mature, and adults can live for another 1 to 2 years.

American cockroaches prefer dark, moist, warm places such as sewers, drains, basements, boiler rooms, and mulch beds. They often enter buildings through plumbing pipes, floor drains, or gaps under doors during extreme weather.

They are nocturnal omnivores that eat almost anything, including decaying organic matter, food crumbs, starches, paper glue, and fabrics.

Because they frequently travel through sewers and drains, they can mechanically carry bacteria (such as Salmonella and E. coli) onto food and surfaces, while their shed skins and droppings can trigger allergies and asthma.
