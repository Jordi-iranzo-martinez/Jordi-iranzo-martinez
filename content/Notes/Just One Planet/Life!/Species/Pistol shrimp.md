A pistol shrimp (family Alpheidae, also called a snapping shrimp) is a small marine crustacean famous for possessing one dramatically oversized claw capable of snapping shut so fast that it creates a high-pressure cavitation bubble, producing one of the loudest sounds in the ocean and stunning prey with a shockwave

*[[Crustacea]], [[Ocean]]*

Measuring only 3 to 5 cm (1.2 to 2 inches) in body length, the pistol shrimp features one normal pincer and one massive, specialized snapping claw that can grow up to half the length of its entire body.

If the shrimp loses its giant snapping claw to a predator or fight, its smaller normal claw gradually grows into a new snapping claw during subsequent molts, while a new small claw regenerates on the opposite side.

Many species display vivid, translucent patterns of orange, red, green, or stripes, helping them blend into coral reefs, seagrass beds, and oyster flats.

The claw has a plunger-like projection on the movable upper finger that fits into a socket on the lower finger. When the shrimp cocks the claw open and snaps it shut at roughly 100 km/h (62 mph), it shoots out a high-velocity water jet.

The extreme speed causes water pressure to drop so low that water vaporizes into a microscopic cavitation bubble. As surrounding water rushes back in, the bubble collapses violently in less than a millisecond.

The implosion unleashes a powerful acoustic shockwave loud enough to stun or kill small fish and crabs, reaching up to 210–218 decibels (louder than a gunshot—and loud enough in large colonies to interfere with submarine sonar). The collapse also generates a brief flash of light (shrimpoluminescence) and localized temperatures momentarily reaching several thousand degrees Celsius.

Several species of pistol shrimp live in a famous mutualistic partnership with watchman gobies (small reef fish).

Because pistol shrimp have poor eyesight, they rely on the sharp-eyed goby to stand guard outside their shared sandy burrow. The shrimp keeps one antenna touching the goby's tail at all times; if a predator approaches, the goby flicks its tail to warn the shrimp so both can dart safely underground.
