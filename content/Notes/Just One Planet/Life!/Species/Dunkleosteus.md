Dunkleosteus (*Dunkleosteus terrelli*) is an extinct genus of large, heavily armored placoderm (arthrodire) fish that ruled the oceans during the Late [[Devonian]] period, roughly 382 to 358 million years ago.

Existing over 100 million years before the first dinosaurs, it was one of Earth's earliest vertebrate apex predators.

Its head and thorax were encased in thick, interlocking plates of dermal bone up to 5 cm (2 inches) thick. Because the rest of its skeleton was made mostly of cartilage that rarely fossilizes, almost all known fossils consist of its massive skull and chest armor.

Dunkleosteus had no true teeth. Instead, its jaws featured two pairs of sharp, guillotine-like bony plates that rubbed against each other every time it closed its mouth, keeping the edges razor-sharp.

For decades, scientists estimated the largest species (Dunkleosteus terrelli) reached 6 to 10 meters (20 to 33 feet) long. However, 2023 and 2024 paleontological studies using eye-to-gill ratios revealed it had a much shorter, deeper, tuna-like body. Typical adults measured around 3.4 meters (11 feet) and weighed 950 to 1,200 kg (2,100 to 2,650 lbs), with the largest individuals reaching 4.1 meters (13.5 feet).

Biomechanical modeling shows Dunkleosteus possessed one of the most powerful bites of any fish in history, generating over 5,300 newtons at the jaw tip (and up to 7,400 newtons at the rear blades). This concentrated enough pressure to easily shear through the hard shells of ammonites, crustaceans, and fellow armored placoderms.

Thanks to a unique four-bar linkage mechanism in its skull, it could snap its jaws open in roughly 20 to 50 milliseconds. This created a powerful suction force that pulled fast-moving prey straight into its mouth.

Fossils have been found alongside regurgitated boluses of semi-digested fish bones, showing that _Dunkleosteus_ swallowed large chunks of prey whole and coughed up indigestible hard parts—much like modern owls or sharks.

Fossils of various _Dunkleosteus_ species have been unearthed across [[North America]] (especially Ohio's Cleveland Shale), Morocco, Belgium, Poland, and [[Russia]].

The entire placoderm class vanished at the end of the Devonian period during the Hangenberg Event (~359 million years ago), a severe mass extinction triggered by rapid ocean deoxygenation and climate shifts that cleared the way for sharks and bony fishes to diversify.
