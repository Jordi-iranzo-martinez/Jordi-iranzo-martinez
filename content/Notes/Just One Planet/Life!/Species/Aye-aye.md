The Aye-aye (*Daubentonia madagascariensis*) is the world's largest nocturnal primate and one of [[Madagascar]]'s most unusual endemic animals.

A specialized type of lemur, it is famous for its rodent-like continuously growing teeth, bat-like ears, and an extraordinarily long, skeletal middle finger used to tap on trees and extract hidden insects.

*[[Bats]], [[Primates]]*

Adults measure 30 to 37 cm (12 to 15 inches) in body length—plus a bushy tail longer than their body at 44 to 53 cm (17 to 21 inches)—and weigh around 2 to 2.5 kg (4.4 to 5.5 lbs).

Its most iconic trait is an ultra-thin, elongated third finger equipped with a ball-and-socket joint that allows 360-degree rotation. Its fourth finger is also unusually long, giving the hand a skeletal, witch-like appearance.

Unlike any other living primate, the aye-aye has large, chisel-shaped front teeth (incisors) that grow continuously throughout its life, just like a beaver's or squirrel's, designed for gnawing through thick tree bark.

Oversized Ears and Dark Fur. It has coarse, shaggy black or dark brown fur, large round eyes with a reflective layer (tapetum lucidum) for night vision, and huge, sensitive, hairless ears.

The aye-aye is the only primate known to find food using percussive foraging—a technique similar to how woodpeckers locate prey:

- Tapping for Hollows. Moving slowly through the forest canopy at night, the aye-aye taps rapidly on tree trunks and branches up to eight times per second with its bony middle finger.

- Listening for Grubs. Using its giant, satellite-like ears, it listens carefully to the acoustic reverberations inside the wood to detect hollow tunnels and the faint movements of wood-boring beetle larvae beneath the bark.

- Chewing and Extracting. Once it locates a tunnel, it uses its powerful incisors to tear open the wood, then inserts its slender middle finger—tipped with a hooked claw—to skewer and pull out the grub. They also eat fungi, nectar, coconuts, and fruits.

Native exclusively to the eastern rainforests and northwestern deciduous forests of Madagascar, aye-ayes are solitary and spend the day sleeping inside spherical nests woven from leaves and vines high in the forks of trees.

*[[Tropical Rainforest]]*

In traditional Malagasy folklore, the aye-aye is widely feared as a symbol of bad luck and a harbinger of death—local superstition holds that if an aye-aye points its long middle finger at someone, that person is doomed. As a result, villagers have historically killed aye-ayes on sight whenever they entered farmland or villages.

Listed as Endangered on the [[IUCN]] Red List, wild aye-aye populations are declining rapidly due to widespread deforestation across Madagascar and retaliatory killings rooted in cultural superstition.
