
Aphids (superfamily Aphidoidea), commonly known as greenflies or blackflies, are small, soft-bodied, sap-sucking insects that rank among the most destructive agricultural and garden pests worldwide.

Most aphids measure between 1 and 10 millimeters (0.04 to 0.4 inches) long and come in a wide range of colors—including green, yellow, black, brown, red, and white—often matching the plants they feed on.

A defining anatomical feature of most aphids is a pair of small, tube-like projections called _cornicles_ sticking out from the rear of their abdomen, which secrete defensive droplets when threatened.

Within the same species, individuals can be wingless (apterous) when food is abundant, or develop two pairs of transparent wings (alate) to migrate when a host plant becomes overcrowded or starts to die.

During spring and summer, female aphids reproduce asexually without mating (_parthenogenesis_) and give birth to live, genetically identical nymphs rather than laying eggs. Because nymphs are often born already carrying developing embryos inside them ("telescoping generations"), a single female can produce thousands of descendants within weeks.

In temperate climates, shortening days in autumn trigger the birth of sexual males and females, which mate to lay hardy overwintering eggs on host plants.

Aphids use needle-like mouthparts (_stylets_) to pierce plant phloem and suck nutrient-rich sap, causing leaves to curl, yellow, and stunt. More importantly, they are major vectors of plant viruses—such as potato virus Y and cucumber mosaic virus—which can devastate crops.

Because plant sap is high in sugar and low in amino acids, aphids consume large volumes and excrete excess sugar as a sticky liquid called honeydew. Honeydew coats leaves and encourages the growth of black sooty mold, which blocks sunlight and reduces photosynthesis.

Many ant species form a mutualistic relationship with aphids—protecting them from predators like ladybugs and lacewings, and even carrying them to fresh plant shoots, in exchange for milking them for honeydew.

Aphids are a crucial food source for predatory insects such as ladybugs (ladybirds), green lacewing larvae, hoverfly larvae, and parasitoid wasps (which lay eggs inside aphids, turning them into swollen golden "mummies").

In gardens and organic farming, infestations are commonly managed by spraying plants with a strong jet of water, applying insecticidal soap or neem oil, controlling ant populations, and planting companion flowers (such as marigolds, dill, or yarrow) to attract natural predators.
