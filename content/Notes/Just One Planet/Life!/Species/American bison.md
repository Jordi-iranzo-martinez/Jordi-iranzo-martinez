The American bison (*Bison bison*) —commonly called the American buffalo—is the largest land mammal in [[North America]] and a keystone species of the continent's grasslands

*[[Mammalia]]], [[Great American Prairie]]*

Adult bulls can stand up to 2 meters (6.5 feet) at the shoulder and weigh up to 900–1,000 kg (2,000–2,200 pounds). They feature a massive muscular shoulder hump that allows them to sweep their heads side-to-side like a snowplow to uncover buried grass in winter, along with a thick, two-layered coat built for extreme cold. Despite their bulk, they can run up to 56 km/h (35 mph).

There are two living subspecies: the plains bison (*Bison bison bison*), which inhabits open prairies and grasslands, and the larger wood bison (*Bison bison athabascae*), found in boreal forests and meadows in northern [[Canada]] and [[Alaska]].

As herbivores, bison spend 9 to 11 hours a day grazing primarily on grasses and sedges. Their grazing, trampling, and dust-bathing ("wallowing") create varied microhabitats that boost plant biodiversity and support prairie birds, insects, and small mammals.

Tens of millions of bison once roamed North America before commercial overhunting and systematic slaughter in the 19th century reduced the wild population to fewer than 1,000 animals by the late 1880s. Thanks to early conservationists, Indigenous nations, and national parks like Yellowstone, around 31,000 bison now live in conservation herds alongside several hundred thousand managed on private commercial ranches.

Designated as the national mammal of the United States in 2016, the species is listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List because wild herds remain fragmented and dependent on active management.
