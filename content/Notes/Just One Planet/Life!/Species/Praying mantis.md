Praying mantises (order Mantodea) are carnivorous ambush predators comprising over 2,400 species found across temperate and tropical regions worldwide (including Mantis religiosa, the European mantis common across Spain and the Mediterranean). 

Named for the distinctive folded posture of their spiked front legs—which resembles hands folded in prayer—they are among the most specialized visual hunters in the insect world.

- **3D stereoscopic vision.** Mantises are the only insects known to possess true stereoscopic (3D) depth perception. Their large, widely spaced compound eyes—accompanied by three simple eyes (_ocelli_) between their antennae—allow them to judge the exact distance of moving prey before striking. (The dark spot visible on each eye is a "pseudopupil," an optical illusion caused by light absorption).
    
- **180-degree rotating head.** Unlike almost any other insect, a mantis has a flexible neck joint that lets it swivel its triangular head up to 180 degrees to scan its surroundings without moving its body.

- **Lightning-fast raptorial legs.** Their enlarged forelegs are lined with sharp interlocking spines. Once prey comes within reach, a mantis strikes in **50 to 70 milliseconds**—faster than the blink of a human eye—pinning the victim tightly before eating it alive.

- **Echolocation detector.** Many flying mantises have a single ultrasonic "ear" located on the underside of their thorax, allowing them to hear the echolocation clicks of hunting bats and execute mid-air evasive dives.

Depending on their habitat, mantises mimic green leaves, dead twigs, tree bark, lichen, or vibrant blossoms. Species like the Southeast Asian **Orchid Mantis** (_Hymenopus coronatus_) have petal-shaped legs and pink-and-white coloration so convincing that pollinating insects fly straight to them.

They feed exclusively on live prey—primarily moths, flies, crickets, beetles, and spiders—though large species occasionally capture small lizards, frogs, fish, and even hummingbirds.

Female mantises are typically larger than males and sometimes eat their mates during or after copulation. While this behavior is well-documented—especially in captivity or when females are hungry—it occurs in roughly 13–28% of wild encounters rather than every time, providing the female with a vital protein boost for egg production.

In autumn, females lay dozens to hundreds of eggs inside a frothy liquid that hardens into a protective, weatherproof styrofoam-like casing called an _ootheca_, from which tiny nymphs hatch the following spring.
