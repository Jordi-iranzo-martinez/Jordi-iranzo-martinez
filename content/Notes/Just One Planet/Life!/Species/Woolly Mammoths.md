The woolly mammoth (*Mammuthus primigenius*) is an extinct species of Ice Age elephant that roamed the cold northern grasslands—known as the "mammoth steppe"—across Eurasia and North America from roughly 400,000 years ago until its final disappearance around 4,000 years ago.

Closely related to modern Asian elephants (sharing more than 99% of their DNA), woolly mammoths are among the best-understood prehistoric animals thanks to frozen carcasses preserved in Arctic permafrost and depictions in Paleolithic cave art.

[[Mammals]]

- **Size & build.** Roughly comparable in height to modern African elephants, males reached 2.7 to 3.4 meters (9 to 11 feet) at the shoulder and weighed up to 6 metric tons. They had a high, domed skull, a sloping back, and a fatty hump behind the neck for energy storage.

- **Insulating coat & fat.** They were covered in a dense, woolly undercoat beneath coarse outer guard hairs up to a meter long, backed by a layer of subcutaneous fat up to 10 cm (4 inches) thick.

- **Minimized heat loss.** Unlike modern elephants, woolly mammoths had very small ears (around 30 cm long) and short tails to prevent frostbite and conserve body heat.

- **Curved tusks & grazing teeth.** Both males and females grew long, spiraled tusks—reaching over 4 meters (13 feet) in large bulls—used for clearing snow off grass, foraging, and defense. Their ridged molars were specialized for grinding tough tundra grasses and sedges.

Most woolly mammoth populations vanished around 10,000 to 12,000 years ago at the end of the last Ice Age due to a combination of rapid climate warming (which replaced dry steppe grasslands with wet forests and tundra) and hunting by early humans.

Small dwarf or isolated populations survived thousands of years longer on Arctic islands—until roughly 5,600 years ago on St. Paul Island (Alaska) and as recently as 4,000 years ago (c. 2000 BCE) on Wrangel Island in the Arctic Ocean, meaning mammoths were still alive while the Egyptian pyramids were being built.
