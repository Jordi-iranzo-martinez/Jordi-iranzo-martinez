Chameleons (family Chamaeleonidae) are a distinctive and highly specialized clade of Old World lizards renowned for their ability to change skin color, independently rotating eyes, and ballistic tongues. 

With more than 200 described species, they are native primarily to mainland [Africa] and [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Wonderful Places/Madagascar]] (which hosts nearly half of all known species), as well as parts of southern [Europe] (including southern [[Spain]]), the [[Middle East]], and southern [[Asia]]. 

*[[Reptiles]]*

Contrary to popular belief, chameleons do not change color simply to match any background. Instead, they shift colors primarily for social signaling (expressing aggression, submission, or mating readiness) and thermoregulation (turning darker to absorb heat or paler to reflect sunlight). They achieve this by actively tuning the spacing of microscopic guanine nanocrystals inside specialized skin cells called iridophores, which reflect different wavelengths of light.

Their eyes are encased in cone-shaped, fused eyelids with only a tiny central opening for the pupil in a 360-degree panoramic vision. Each eye moves completely independently, allowing chameleons to scan two different directions simultaneously and switch to sharp stereoscopic depth perception the instant they lock onto prey.

Chameleons possess an extraordinarily long muscular tongue—reaching up to twice their body length in smaller species—tipped with a sticky club. Powered by a collagen elastic-recoil mechanism, it launches out of the mouth in roughly 0.07 seconds to snatch insects and small vertebrates.

Built for arboreal life, their toes are fused into opposing bundles of two and three digits that act like pincers to grip branches securely. Most species also feature a prehensile tail used as a fifth limb for balance (though unlike many other lizards, chameleons cannot regrow a lost tail).

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/vdfMLAH1yJQ?si=JeIDCWr4BcN4Muc0" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

The largest chameleons—such as Parson's chameleon (Calumma parsonii) and Oustalet's chameleon (Furcifer oustaleti)—reach nearly 70 centimeters (27 in) in total length. At the opposite extreme, Madagascar's nano-chameleon (Brookesia nana), described in 2021, measures just 2.2 centimeters (0.87 in) from snout to tail tip as an adult male, making it one of the smallest known reptiles on Earth.

Most chameleons are oviparous (egg-laying) (roughly 80%), though several species are ovoviviparous—giving birth to live young that hatch from soft egg membranes right before or at birth.


<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/vHI3Giekkxo?si=QVjwh-G-_lyQRYC6" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Iconic representatives include the panther chameleon (_Furcifer pardalis_, famous for vivid turquoise, red, and orange patterns), the veiled chameleon (_Chamaeleo calyptratus_, sporting a tall casque on its head), Jackson's chameleon (_Trioceros jacksonii_, bearing three _Triceratops_-like horns), and the common chameleon (_Chamaeleo chamaeleon_), native to the [[Mediterranean]] basin.
