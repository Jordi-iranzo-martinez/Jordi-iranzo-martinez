*Kimberella quadrata* is an extinct genus of bilateral organisms that lived during the late [[Ediacaran period]], approximately 558 to 550 million years ago.

Discovered primarily in South [[Australia]] and [[Russia]]'s White Sea region, it is one of the most important fossils in paleontology because it represents one of the earliest confirmed bilaterian animals—organisms with a distinct front and back, top and bottom, and mirror-image left and right sides.

Kimberella had an oval, flattened body measuring up to 15 cm (6 inches) long and 5 to 7 cm wide.

Unlike modern snails or clams with hard calcium carbonate shells, _Kimberella_ was covered by a tough, flexible, univalve organic shell (or stiffened dorsal cuticle) into which it could retract its softer body parts when threatened or battered by storm currents.

Beneath its shell sat a broad, muscular foot used to glide slowly across the seafloor, bordered by a ruffled, frilled edge that likely functioned as gills for breathing.

During the Ediacaran period, shallow ocean floors were carpeted by thick, slimy layers of bacteria and algae known as microbial mats. _Kimberella_ was an active grazer that crawled across these mats.

Fossils of _Kimberella_ are frequently preserved right at the end of fan-shaped sets of paired scratch marks (ichnofossil _Kimberichnus_ or _Radulichnus_). Paleontologists concluded that _Kimberella_ extended a retractable proboscis equipped with tooth-like structures to scrape up microbes and pull food back toward its mouth, much like a modern snail uses its radula.

Chemical biomarker analysis of well-preserved Russian specimens revealed molecules derived from phytosterols, proving that _Kimberella_ actively digested green algae and bacteria in a dedicated gut—making it one of the oldest animals known to possess a functional digestive tract.
