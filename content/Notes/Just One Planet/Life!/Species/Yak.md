The yak (Bos grunniens for the domestic form, and Bos mutus for the wild form) is a large, long-haired bovine native to the high-altitude plateaus and mountain ranges of Central [[Asia]], primarily the Tibetan Plateau and the Himalayas. Essential to high-altitude human survival for millennia, it is uniquely adapted to freezing temperatures and thin, oxygen-poor air.

*[[Tibet]], [[Himalayas]], [[Mammalia]]*

Wild yaks are massive, standing up to 2 to 2.2 meters (6.5 to 7.2 feet) tall at the shoulder and weighing up to 1,000 kg (2,200 lbs), whereas domestic yaks are noticeably smaller (typically 350 to 580 kg) and display varied coat colors (black, brown, white, or piebald).

Both sexes have long, curved horns and a dense undercoat of fine wool covered by a long outer "skirt" of coarse hair that hangs nearly to the ground, trapping body heat and insulating them down to −40 °C (−40 °F).

Living at elevations between 3,000 and 5,500 meters (10,000 to 18,000 feet), yaks have enlarged hearts and lungs (roughly three times the lung capacity of domestic cattle), specialized high-affinity hemoglobin to absorb scarce oxygen, and almost no functional sweat glands to conserve body heat and water.

Unlike cattle, yaks do not moo—instead, they communicate using low, guttural grunts (the origin of their scientific name, _grunniens_), especially during the breeding season.

Domesticated roughly 3,000 to 5,000 years ago, millions of domestic yaks sustain nomadic and pastoralist communities across [[Tibet]], [[Nepal]], [[Bhutan]], [[Mongolia]], and western [[China]]—providing high-fat milk (used for yak butter tea and hard chhurpi cheese), meat, warm fiber, leather, transport across steep mountain passes, and dried dung (the primary fuel source above the treeline).
