
The kea (*Nestor notabilis*) is the world's only alpine parrot, endemic to the forested and mountainous regions of [[New Zealand]]'s South Island.

[[Birds]]

The name comes in Māori after its loud, piercing "keee-aaa" flight call, it is famous for its exceptional intelligence, playful curiosity, and mischievous interactions with humans.

Measuring about 48 cm (19 inches) long, the kea has dull olive-green plumage with dark-edged feathers that blend into alpine scrub and rock. In flight, however, it reveals brilliant scarlet-orange feathers beneath its wings and a turquoise-blue rump.

Its long, narrow, dark-gray upper beak is adapted for digging up roots, grubs, and insects, stripping bark, and prying open tough alpine plants—and equally adept at unzipping hikers' backpacks.

Kea are among the most intelligent birds on Earth, performing on par with primates and corvids in cognitive tests. They can solve complex multi-step mechanical puzzles, cooperate with partners, understand basic statistical probabilities, and even use sticks as tools to trigger traps or clean feathers.

Unlike most wild animals, kea play extensively—rolling snowballs, sliding down snowy roofs, and tossing objects. Researchers even discovered that kea have a distinct "play call" (a warbling vocalization) that acts like infectious laughter, prompting nearby kea to immediately start playing.

Because alpine environments offer scarce food, kea evolved an insatiable curiosity toward anything new. Around ski fields and scenic lookouts, they notoriously inspect vehicles, pull rubber stripping off car windows, and steal unattended gear.

Their diet includes leaves, roots, berries, nectar, seeds, insects, and carrion. Historically, they occasionally attacked live sheep to feed on fat around their backs, which led to a government bounty that killed over 150,000 kea before they gained full legal protection in 1986.

Today, the wild population is estimated at only 3,000 to 7,000 individuals, classified as Endangered on the [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]] Red List due to introduced mammalian predators (such as stoats and possums), lead poisoning from old building roofs, and road accidents.
