The African wild dog (*Lycaon pictus*), also known as the painted wolf or Cape hunting dog, is a wild canine native to sub-Saharan [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Wonderful Places/Africa]].

*Its Latin name, literally means "painted wolf."*

Renowned for its colorful, mottled coat, huge rounded ears, and extraordinary social cooperation, it is one of the most efficient predators on the African continent.

*[[Carnivores]]*

Every individual has a completely unique patchwork coat of black, brown, tan, yellow, and white fur, which helps pack members recognize one another from a distance.

Adults stand about 60 to 75 cm (24 to 30 inches) tall at the shoulder and weigh 18 to 36 kg (40 to 79 lbs). They have long, slender legs built for endurance running and oversized, satellite-like ears that help dissipate heat and detect prey and packmates.

Unlike domestic dogs, wolves, and foxes—which have five toes on their front feet—African wild dogs are the only living canids with just four toes on every foot, an adaptation that increases stride length and running speed.

African wild dogs live in highly cooperative packs usually ranging from 6 to 20 adults (and occasionally over 40), led by a monogamous breeding pair. Remarkably, it is the females that disperse upon reaching maturity to join new packs, while males stay with their birth pack.

Before setting out on a hunt, pack members gather in energetic greeting rallies. Researchers have discovered that dogs actually "vote" on whether to start hunting by making sharp sneeze-like exhalations—the more sneezes from dominant members, the more likely the pack is to depart.

Packs show remarkable altruism. After a successful hunt, adults regurgitate meat for the pups as well as for sick, injured, or elderly pack members who could not join the chase.

Rather than relying on stealth or ambush, they hunt in broad daylight (typically at dawn and dusk) using coordinated relay chases. They can run at speeds of 60 km/h (37 mph) over long distances, wearing down medium-sized antelopes such as impala, kudu, Thomson's gazelle, and wildebeest calves.

*While lions succeed in roughly 25–30% of their hunts, African wild dog packs achieve a 60% to 80% success rate.*

Listed as Endangered on the [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]] Red List, fewer than 6,600 adults remain in the wild today, restricted mostly to fragmented populations in southern and eastern [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Life!/Wonderful Places/Africa]] (such as Botswana, Zimbabwe, [[Namib Desert]], Zambia, Tanzania, and [[South Africa)]].

Because packs require massive, uninterrupted territories to roam, they are heavily threatened by habitat fragmentation, human-wildlife conflict with livestock farmers, road accidents, and infectious diseases like rabies and canine distemper spread by domestic dogs.
