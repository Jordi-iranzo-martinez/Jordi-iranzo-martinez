Jumping spiders (family Salticidae) are the largest family of spiders, with over 6,600 described species worldwide. Instantly recognizable by their compact, fuzzy bodies, oversized front-facing eyes, and cat-like agility, they are among the sharpest-sighted and most intelligent invertebrates on Earth.

*[[Arachnids]]*

Jumping spiders have eight eyes arranged in three or four rows. Their two massive, forward-facing anterior median eyes act like high-resolution telephoto lenses, giving them color vision, sharp detail, and depth perception superior to many vertebrates of similar size. The remaining six smaller eyes wrap around the sides and top of their head, providing nearly 360-degree peripheral motion detection.

Hydraulic Jumping. Despite lacking large leg muscles, jumping spiders can leap up to 50 times their own body length. They achieve this by rapidly increasing blood pressure (hemolymph) inside their rear legs to snap them straight like hydraulic pistons—always anchoring a thin silk dragline behind them as a safety tether before jumping.

Most species measure between 1 and 25 mm (0.04 to 1 inch) in body length, with the giant Hyllus giganteus being one of the largest.

Unlike orb-weavers or cobweb spiders, jumping spiders do not spin webs to trap insects. Instead, they are active daytime hunters that stalk, calculate trajectories, and pounce on flies, mosquitoes, moths, and other spiders. They use silk purely to build cozy sleeping retreats, molt, and protect their eggs.

Studies on species like Portia (a spider-hunting jumping spider) show advanced cognitive abilities rarely seen in animals with brains smaller than a poppy seed. Portia can plan complex detours out of sight of its target, mimic the web vibrations of trapped insects to lure other spiders out, and adapt its tactics through trial and error.

Because they rely on vision and curiously tilt their heads to look at moving objects—including human fingers and camera lenses—jumping spiders (especially the regal jumping spider, Phidippus regius, and bold jumping spider, Phidippus audax) are popular, docile pets that pose no medical danger to humans.

Because of their acute color vision, male jumping spiders perform intricate visual courtship displays. Native to Australia, tiny peacock spiders raise vibrant, iridescent abdominal flaps patterned in blues, reds, and golds while waving their legs and vibrating to a rhythmic "dance" to impress a watching female—and avoid being eaten.
