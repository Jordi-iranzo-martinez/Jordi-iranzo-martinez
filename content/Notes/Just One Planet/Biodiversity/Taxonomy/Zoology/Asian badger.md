The Asian badger (*Meles leucurus*) -also known as the sand badger- is a burrowing omnivorous mammal belonging to the weasel family (Mustelidae).

*[[Mammalia]]*

Closely related to the European badger, it is native to a vast expanse of central and eastern [[Asia]], where it thrives in forests, steppes, and semi-arid grasslands.

It has a distinctive striped face, though its dark facial stripes are narrower and usually brown rather than jet black. These stripes curve up over the eyes and extend back toward the white-tipped ears, contrasting with a white muzzle and cheeks.

Adults measure roughly 50 to 70 cm (20 to 28 inches) in body length—with a short tail of 13 to 20 cm—and typically weigh between 3.5 and 9 kg (7.7 to 20 lbs), growing heaviest in autumn as they build up fat reserves for winter.

It has a stocky, wedge-shaped body, short muscular legs, and long, strong front claws adapted for excavating deep underground tunnel systems. Its upper coat is a coarse silvery-grey to yellowish-brown, while its belly and legs are dark brown or black.

Found across [[China]], Mongolia, the Korean Peninsula, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, and [[Russia]] (from the Volga River east to Siberia), it inhabits deciduous and mixed forests, open steppes, scrublands, and mountain meadows up to 4,000 meters (13,000 feet).

Asian badgers are mostly nocturnal and live in large, multi-generational underground burrow complexes called setts, which feature dozens of entrances and ventilated sleeping chambers. In colder northern regions, they enter a prolonged period of winter torpor (dormancy) inside their burrows from November to March, surviving off stored body fat.

They forage primarily by sniffing out food on or just beneath the soil surface. Their varied diet includes earthworms, beetles, grasshoppers, small rodents, frogs, bird eggs, roots, bulbs, nuts, berries, and fallen fruit.
