Otters are semi-aquatic, carnivorous mammals belonging to the weasel family (Mustelidae).

Known for their playful behavior, sleek bodies, and dense waterproof fur, there are 13 living species of otters found on every continent except [[Antarctica]] and [[Australia]].

Otters have long, streamlined bodies, short legs with webbed feet, and powerful, muscular tails that act like rudders to propel them through the water. Their nostrils and small ears automatically close tightly when diving.

Unlike whales or seals, most otters have little to no insulating blubber. Instead, they rely on an ultra-thick, two-layer coat that traps a layer of air against their skin to keep them warm and dry—with the **sea otter** boasting up to 1 million hairs per square inch.

Long, stiff whiskers on their snouts allow them to detect the faint underwater vibrations of swimming fish and crustaceans in dark or murky water.

Major Groups and Famous Species

- **River Otters** (e.g., North American river otter, Eurasian otter). Agile both on land and in freshwater rivers, lakes, and coastal estuaries, they feed primarily on fish, crayfish, frogs, and aquatic insects.

- **Sea Otter** (*Enhydra lutris*). The smallest marine mammal in North America and the heaviest member of the weasel family (weighing up to 45 kg / 100 lbs). Sea otters spend almost their entire lives in the coastal kelp forests of the North Pacific, rarely coming ashore.

- **Giant Otter** (*Pteronura brasiliensis*). Native to South America's Amazon and Pantanal river systems, it is the longest member of the mustelid family, reaching up to 1.7 meters (5.6 feet) in length. Giant otters live in loud, tight-knit family groups that cooperatively hunt large fish and even fend off caimans and jaguars.

Sea otters are among the few non-primate mammals known to use tools. While floating on their backs, they balance a flat rock on their chest and smash hard-shelled clams, mussels, and sea urchins against it to crack them open—often keeping a favorite rock tucked inside a loose flap of skin under their arm.

Resting sea otters frequently wrap themselves in strands of giant kelp like a blanket so ocean currents do not carry them away, and they sometimes hold paws with nearby otters to stay together in groups called rafts.

Sea otters play a critical ecological role by eating voraciously (consuming 25–30% of their body weight daily) to keep sea urchin populations in check. Without otters, sea urchins overgraze and destroy underwater kelp forests, which provide vital habitat for hundreds of marine species and absorb atmospheric carbon.
