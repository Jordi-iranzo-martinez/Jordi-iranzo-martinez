
Hemichordata is a small phylum of marine deuterostome invertebrates—most famously acorn worms—that sit on the evolutionary tree right between echinoderms (like starfish and sea urchins) and chordates (vertebrates). 

Around 130 living species are known today, alongside extinct fossil groups like graptolites.

Their bodies are divided into three distinct regions, each with its own coelomic (body) cavity:

- **Proboscis** (Protosome). A muscular, often acorn-shaped front structure used for burrowing and collecting food particles.

- **Collar** (Mesosome). A short middle ring housing the mouth and dorsal collar nerve cord.

- **Trunk** (Metasome). A long posterior section containing the gill slits, digestive tract, and gonads.

Like chordates, hemichordates possess openings along their pharynx used for filter feeding and respiration.

They have a stomochord: a flexible, hollow outgrowth of the gut extending into the proboscis; once thought to be a primitive notochord (which gave the phylum its name, meaning "half-chordate"), it is now recognized as a separate structure.

They feature an open circulatory system with a dorsal contractile vessel ("heart") and a specialized excretory organ in the proboscis called the glomerulus.

Main Classes

- **Enteropneusta** (Acorn Worms). Solitary, worm-like marine animals (such as _Balanoglossus_ and _Saccoglossus_) that burrow in coastal mud and sand, feeding on detritus or suspended organic matter.

- **Pterobranchia**. Tiny, mostly colonial, tube-dwelling animals (such as _Rhabdopleura_ and _Cephalodiscus_) that use ciliated tentacles on their collar to filter food from the water.

- **Graptolithina** (Graptolites). A major group of Paleozoic colonial fossils that are now classified within or closely alongside pterobranchs.

Genetic and developmental studies group hemichordates and echinoderms together in a clade called Ambulacraria, supported by the fact that acorn worm larvae (*tornaria*) look remarkably similar to starfish larvae (*bipinnaria*).

Because they share traits with both echinoderms and chordates, hemichordates help biologists understand what the earliest ancestors of vertebrates looked like.
