
Echiurida (more broadly classified as Echiura or Echiuroidea) is a group of marine invertebrates commonly known as spoon worms.

Once treated as an independent phylum, modern DNA studies show they are actually derived annelids (segmented worms) that lost their body segmentation over the course of evolution. Around 150 to 230 living species are known across the world's oceans.

Unlike earthworms or typical marine bristle worms, adult spoon worms have a soft, cylindrical, sausage- or pear-shaped body lacking external or internal segments. They range from under 1 cm to over 1 meter in length (with some proboscises extending even farther).

Extending from the front of the body is a muscular, flattened, non-retractable proboscis (often shaped like a scoop or split into a Y-fork). Ciliated grooves along the proboscis trap organic detritus and plankton and sweep it toward the mouth at its base.

Retaining a classic annelid trait, they possess a pair of hooked bristles called chaetae on the underside near the front—and in some families (like Echiuridae), one or two rings of bristles around the rear end (which gave the group its name, from the Greek echinos meaning "spine" and oura meaning "tail").

Spoon worms live on the seabed from shallow coastal mudflats down to deep-sea trenches. Most dig U-shaped burrows in sand or mud, extending only their long proboscis onto the surface to feed while staying safely hidden below.

*One famous California species builds long U-shaped tunnels and spins a mucus net to filter-feed; its spacious burrows are shared by so many commensal crabs, gobies, and scale worms that it earned the nickname "innkeeper worm."*

While many species release eggs and sperm into the water, the green spoon worm (*Bonellia viridis*)—found in the [[Mediterranean]] and Northeast [[Atlantic Ocean]]—exhibits environmental sex determination. 

Free-swimming larvae that settle on bare seafloor grow into 1-meter-long females, whereas larvae that land on a female's proboscis absorb a chemical cue (bonellin) and develop into tiny, 1–3 mm parasitic males that live inside the female's reproductive tract solely to fertilize her eggs.

In parts of East [[Asia]]—especially South [[Korea]], where *Urechis unicinctus* is known as gaebul—spoon worms are harvested and eaten raw as hoe (sashimi) or fermented.
