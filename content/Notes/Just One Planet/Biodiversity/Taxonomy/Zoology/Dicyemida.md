
Dicyemida (also known as Rhombozoa) is a phylum of microscopic, worm-like endosymbionts and parasites that live exclusively inside the renal appendages (kidneys) of benthic cephalopods—such as octopuses, cuttlefishes, and bottom-dwelling squids.

*The name comes from Greek for "two embryos", referring to the two distinct reproductive stages tied to the age and population density inside their host:*

Two-Phase Life Cycle:

- **Asexual phase** (Nematogens). In young, growing cephalopods, worm-like adults called nematogens reproduce asexually from internal stem cells (axoblasts), producing vermiform larvae that grow into new nematogens and rapidly colonize the host's kidneys.

- **Sexual phase** (Rhombogens). When the host matures or parasite density becomes crowded, nematogens transform into rhombogens. Inside their axial cell, hermaphroditic structures (_infusorigens_) produce sperm and eggs that self-fertilize, generating tiny, free-swimming infusoriform larvae (equipped with heavy, light-refracting granules). These larvae exit the cephalopod in its urine to disperse into the open ocean—though how they infect a new host remains one of marine biology's enduring mysteries.

Comprising roughly 100 to 120 described species, dicyemids are among the simplest multicellular animals on Earth, with adults consisting of fewer than 50 cells.

Adult dicyemids measure 0.5 to 7 millimeters long and display _eutely_—meaning every adult of a given species has a fixed, exact number of cells (typically 8 to 42 cells).

They completely lack digestive, nervous, circulatory, respiratory, and excretory systems, as well as a body cavity or extracellular matrix.

Their body consists of a single, giant, elongated axial cell (which houses reproductive cells) wrapped inside a single outer jacket of 20 to 30 ciliated somatic cells. At the front end, four to ten specialized cells form a cap-like calotte used to anchor onto folds in the host's kidney tissue while absorbing nutrients directly from cephalopod urine.
