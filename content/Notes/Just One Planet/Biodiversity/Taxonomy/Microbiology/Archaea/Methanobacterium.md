Methanobacterium is a genus of rod-shaped, methane-producing microorganisms belonging to the domain [[Archaea]]—not Bacteria, despite its name.

First named before scientists recognized Archaea as a separate domain of life, these strictly anaerobic microbes generate energy by converting hydrogen and carbon dioxide (or formate) into methane gas.

Unlike true bacteria, *Methanobacterium* cells lack peptidoglycan in their cell walls, instead using pseudomurein (a similar polymer that resists penicillin and lysozyme), and have ether-linked membrane lipids typical of archaea.

They are non-motile, curved or straight rods (bacilli) that frequently link end-to-end into long chains or filaments.

Oxygen is toxic to them; they thrive only in completely oxygen-free (anoxic) environments.

Most species produce methane primarily through hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O), while several species (such as M. formicicum) can also break down formate.

Methanobacterium species are widespread in warm, oxygen-depleted habitats—including wetland sediments, rice paddies, hot springs, sewage sludge, anaerobic digesters, and the digestive tracts of ruminant animals (like cows and sheep).

By consuming hydrogen produced by fermenting bacteria, they keep anaerobic ecosystems balanced. Industrially, they are essential drivers in biogas reactors that convert organic waste into renewable methane fuel, while naturally contributing to global atmospheric methane emissions.
