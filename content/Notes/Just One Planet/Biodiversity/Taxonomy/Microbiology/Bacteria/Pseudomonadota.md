Pseudomonadota (formerly and still widely known as Proteobacteria) is the largest and most diverse phylum of Gram-negative bacteria.

*[[Bacteria]]*

This phylum encompasses an extraordinary variety of free-living environmental microbes, agricultural symbionts, and major human pathogens.

- **Gram-negative cell envelope.** Members possess an outer membrane composed largely of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) surrounding a thin peptidoglycan layer.

- **Extreme morphological & metabolic diversity.** Originally named _Proteobacteria_ after the shape-shifting Greek sea god Proteus, the phylum includes rods (bacilli), spheres (cocci), spirals, stalked cells, and budding forms. They employ nearly every known bacterial metabolism, including aerobic respiration, anaerobic fermentation, chemolithoautotrophy (deriving energy from inorganic compounds like sulfur or iron), and anoxygenic photosynthesis.

- **Motility.** Most motile species move using whip-like flagella, though some move by gliding or twitching.

Major Classes

- **Alphaproteobacteria**: Includes nitrogen-fixing bacteria that live in symbiosis with plants (e.g., _Rhizobium_) and the evolutionary ancestors of eukaryotic mitochondria.

- **Betaproteobacteria**: Includes ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and pathogens like _Neisseria_ (causing meningitis and gonorrhea).

- **Gammaproteobacteria**: The largest class. It includes famous enteric bacteria like _Escherichia coli_ (_E. coli_), _Salmonella_, and deep-sea quimiolithotrophs found near hydrothermal vents and brine lakes.

- **Zetaproteobacteria**. Marine iron-oxidizing chemolithoautotrophs (such as _Mariprofundus ferrooxydans_) commonly found around deep-sea hydrothermal vents and coastal iron-rich sediments.

- **Acidithiobacillia**. Acid-loving (acidophilic) sulfur- and iron-oxidizing bacteria widely used in industrial biomining (bioleaching) to extract copper and gold from ores.

- **Hydrogenophilalia**. Thermophilic and hydrogen- or sulfur-oxidizing bacteria found in hot springs and hydrothermal environments.

 Pseudomonadota dominate marine and soil ecosystems (accounting for over half of marine microbial biomass in many habitats), driving global carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling.
 
*[[Soil|Soil]]*

 In healthy human guts, Pseudomonadota make up a small fraction of the microbiome; a sharp expansion of this phylum is a widely studied marker of gut dysbiosis and inflammation.
