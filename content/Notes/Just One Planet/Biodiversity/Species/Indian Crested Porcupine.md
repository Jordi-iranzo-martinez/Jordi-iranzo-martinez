The Indian crested porcupine (*Hystrix indica*) is a large, nocturnal rodent native to Southern and Central [[Asia]] as well as parts of the [[Middle East]]. 

*[[Mammals]]*

Known for its distinctive crest of long bristles and formidable coat of black-and-white banded quills, it is one of the largest porcupine species in the world.

Adults measure 70 to 90 cm (28 to 35 inches) in body length and typically weigh between 11 and 18 kg (24 to 40 lbs). They have broad feet equipped with long claws built for digging.

Their quills are modified hairs made of thick keratin. They feature a prominent crest of flexible, coarse bristles along the head and neck that can reach up to 51 cm (20 inches) long, backed by shorter, rigid, sharp spines across the torso and rump.

The short tail holds specialized hollow quills that produce a loud hissing rattle when shaken, serving as a warning signal to deter threats.

Porcupines cannot shoot their quills at attackers. However, the quills detach easily upon contact and embed themselves deeply into the skin of predators.

When cornered by predators like leopards, tigers, or wild dogs, the porcupine stamps its feet, rattles its tail, flares its quills to appear twice its size, and charges backward to drive the spines into the threat.

Found from Turkey and Israel across India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, these animals thrive in rocky hillsides, tropical and temperate forests, shrublands, and mountain slopes up to 2,400 meters (7,900 feet).

They spend the daytime sheltering in natural rock crevices or elaborate underground burrow systems they excavate themselves. At night, they emerge to forage for up to seven hours.

They primarily eat roots, tubers, bulbs, bark, fruits, and agricultural crops. They also frequently gnaw on shed antlers and animal bones to obtain calcium and minerals needed to grow their massive quills.

Their populations remain stable across most of their wide range, though they are occasionally hunted for meat or killed as agricultural pests.
