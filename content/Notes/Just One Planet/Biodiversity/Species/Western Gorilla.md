The western gorilla (*Gorilla gorilla gorilla*) is the most populous and widespread of the two living gorilla species (alongside the eastern gorilla, Gorilla beringei).

Inhabiting the dense tropical forests and swamps of Central and West [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]], it is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

*[[Primates]]*

The most numerous subspecies (with an estimated wild population of 100,000–300,000 individuals) and the subspecies to which virtually all gorillas in zoos belong.

It lives across Cameroon, the Central African Republic, the Republic of the [[Congo]], Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Angola (Cabinda), and extreme western Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Although slightly smaller and more slender than its eastern cousin (such as the mountain gorilla), it is still a massive primate: adult males stand around 1.65–1.75 meters (5.4–5.7 ft) tall upright and weigh 140–200 kg (310–440 lbs), while females weigh roughly 70–90 kg (155–200 lbs).

It has shorter, finer fur than the mountain gorilla, typically brownish-grey in color, often featuring a distinctive reddish-brown or chestnut crest on top of the head.

Upon reaching sexual maturity (around 12–15 years old), adult males develop silvery-grey hair across their backs and hips, which in western gorillas often extends down onto the thighs.

They live in stable family groups (troops) of 5 to 20 individuals, led by one dominant silverback male, several adult females, and their offspring. Peaceful, diurnal, and shy, they construct a fresh nest of branches and leaves on the ground or in trees each night.

Primarily herbivorous, western lowland gorillas consume a much higher proportion of fresh fruit than mountain gorillas, supplemented by leaves, stems, pith, bark, termites, and ants. Wild western gorillas have also been observed using simple tools, such as sticks to gauge water depth when wading through swamps.

Despite outnumbering eastern gorillas, western gorilla populations are declining rapidly due to poaching (the illegal bushmeat trade), recurring outbreaks of the Ebola virus (which has wiped out entire forest populations), and habitat loss from logging and mining.
