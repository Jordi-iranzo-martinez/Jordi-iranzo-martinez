The spotted boxfish (*Ostracion meleagris*), also called the white-spotted or ornate boxfish, is a rigid, box-shaped reef fish native to the [[Indian Ocean]] and [[Pacific Ocean]]. 

Renowned for its dramatic sexual dimorphism—where jet-black, white-polka-dotted females transform into vibrant blue-and-gold males—it is encased in a fused bony carapace and secretes a potent defensive toxin when stressed.

Growing up to 25 cm (9.8 inches) long, its body is enclosed in a hard, four-sided shell made of fused hexagonal scale plates, leaving openings only for its mouth, eyes, gill slits, anus, and fins.

Dark brown to jet-black all over, covered in crisp, tiny white polka dots.

Like many reef fish, spotted boxfish are protogynous hermaphrodites (born female, with dominant individuals changing sex to male). Males develop a black-and-white-spotted back separated by a bright yellow-orange band from vivid cobalt-blue flanks dotted with dark-rimmed orange spots.

Because its rigid shell restricts its swimming speed to a slow, hovering scull using its small fins, the spotted boxfish relies on chemical defense. When stressed, cornered, or dying, it releases a toxic mucus (ostracitoxin) into the water that destroys red blood cells and can rapidly kill nearby fish—including itself in a closed aquarium.

Their internal organs and skin (especially female ovaries) can also accumulate tetrodotoxin (TTX), making them dangerous to eat.]

Found from East [[Africa]] across the Indo-Pacific to [[Hawaii]], southern [[Japan]], and the Pacific coast of [[Mexico]], they inhabit clear lagoons and seaward reefs at depths of 1 to 30 meters. Juveniles often shelter among the spines of long-spined sea urchins.

Males are territorial and patrol small reefs with a harem of three to four females.

Using a small, puckered mouth with fleshy lips, they blow jets of water into sand and reef crevices to uncover sponges, polychaete worms, tunicates (sea squirts), small mollusks, crustaceans, and algae.
