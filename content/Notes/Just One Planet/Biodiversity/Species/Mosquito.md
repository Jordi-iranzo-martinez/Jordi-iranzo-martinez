Mosquitoes (family Culicidae) are small, slender flies comprising over 3,600 species found on every continent except Antarctica. 

The word "mosquito" originates from Spanish and Portuguese for "little fly." While both males and females feed on flower nectar and plant juices for energy, females of most species require protein from blood to develop their eggs—making mosquitoes the deadliest disease vectors to humans worldwide.

Four-Stage Life Cycle

- **Egg**. Females lay 100 to 200 eggs at a time either directly on stagnant water (often glued together in floating "rafts") or on damp soil/containers that will flood later.

- **Larva** ("wriggler"). Aquatic larvae hatch in water and feed on microorganisms and organic debris, breathing through a siphon tube at the water's surface.

- **Pupa** ("tumbler"). A non-feeding, comma-shaped aquatic stage during which the insect transforms into an adult over a few days.

- **Adult**. Emerging onto the water's surface, the adult dries its wings and flies away. The entire cycle from egg to adult can take as little as 7 to 10 days in warm weather.

Female adult mosquitoes possess a specialized, needle-like mouthpart (proboscis) made of six tiny stylets that pierce skin, locate a blood vessel, and inject saliva containing anticoagulants and mild painkillers. The itchy red bump after a bite is an allergic immune reaction to that saliva.

Mosquitoes locate hosts from meters away by detecting exhaled carbon dioxide (CO₂), followed by body heat, sweat compounds (such as lactic acid), and visual contrast as they get closer.

The familiar whining sound comes from their narrow wings beating between 450 and 600 times per second—a frequency males and females also use to harmonize and recognize mates.

Different mosquito genera transmit life-threatening pathogens: *Anopheles* transmits malaria; *Aedes* (including _Aedes aegypti_ and the Asian tiger mosquito, _Aedes albopictus_) spreads dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and yellow fever; and *Culex* carries West Nile virus and Japanese encephalitis.

Despite their danger to humans, mosquitoes serve as pollinators for many plants (especially orchids and arctic flora), while their aquatic larvae and adult forms are a vital food source for fish, dragonflies, frogs, bats, and birds.
