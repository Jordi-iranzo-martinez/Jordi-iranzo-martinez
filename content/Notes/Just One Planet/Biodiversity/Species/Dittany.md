The Dittany (*Dictamnus albus*) is an aromatic, long-lived herbaceous perennial in the citrus family (Rutaceae).

*[[Plants]]*

Native to warm, open woodlands and rocky calcareous slopes across southern and central [[Europe]] (including northern and eastern Spain), North [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]], and temperate Asia, it is famous for producing volatile essential oils that can briefly ignite in midair without harming the plant.

Growing 60 to 100 cm (2 to 3.5 feet) tall, it forms a sturdy clump of glossy, compound dark-green leaves that resemble ash tree leaflets (inspiring its old name _fraxinella_, "little ash"). When crushed, the leaves release a strong, pleasant lemon-cinnamon fragrance due to their citrus-family oil glands.

In late spring to early summer, tall racemes bear striking, asymmetrical five-petaled flowers with long, curving stamens. Blooms are either pale pink with dramatic dark purple-pink veins (_var. purpureus_) or pure white (_var. albiflorus_).

After flowering, the plant develops distinctive five-pointed, star-shaped seed capsules coated in sticky resin glands. As the pods dry in late summer, they snap open elastically, launching glossy black seeds up to several meters away.

On hot, calm summer evenings—especially just as the seed pods ripen—the plant's glandular hairs secrete high concentrations of volatile terpenes and essential oils (such as limonene and estragole).

Holding a lit match near the flower stalk ignites the surrounding vapor in a brief flash of blue flame that leaves the plant completely unharmed. Because of this trait, it is frequently associated in folklore with the biblical burning bush.

Like other members of the Rutaceae family (such as rue and wild parsnip), Dictamnus albus contains furanocoumarins. Direct skin contact with its sap or seed pods followed by exposure to ultraviolet sunlight can trigger phytophotodermatitis—a painful skin reaction causing redness, swelling, and blistering.
