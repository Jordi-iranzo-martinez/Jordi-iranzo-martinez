
The King Cobra (*Ophiophagus hannah*) is the world's longest venomous snake, native to the forests of South and Southeast [[Asia]].

*Despite its name and iconic flared hood, it is not a true cobra (genus Naja), but the sole member of its own genus, Ophiophagus—meaning "snake-eater."*

Adults typically measure 3 to 4 meters (10 to 13 feet) long, with the largest confirmed wild specimen reaching 5.85 meters (19.2 feet).

When threatened, it raises up to a third of its body off the ground—often standing eye-to-eye with an adult human—and flares out a narrow, olive-brown or black hood marked with pale yellow or white chevron bands.

Unlike the high-pitched hiss of most snakes, a king cobra's hiss sounds remarkably like a deep, resonant dog's growl due to specialized tracheal diverticula (air sacs) in its windpipe.

Its diet consists almost entirely of other snakes, including pythons, rat snakes, and venomous species such as kraits and true cobras. When food is scarce, it occasionally eats monitor lizards or small vertebrates.

While its neurotoxic venom is drop-for-drop less toxic than that of some smaller elapids (like the inland taipan or black mamba), a single bite delivers a huge volume of venom—up to 400 to 600 mg—capable of killing an adult human within 30 minutes or even bringing down an Asian elephant.

Despite its fearsome reputation, the king cobra is reclusive and actively avoids humans whenever possible, only striking when cornered, startled, or defending its nest.

King cobras are the only snakes in the world known to build an actual nest for their eggs. Using loops of her body, the female gathers fallen leaves, twigs, and forest debris into a mound up to a meter high, depositing 20 to 40 eggs inside the warm, decaying chamber.

The mother coils atop the nest for 60 to 90 days to guard the eggs against predators, leaving only just before they hatch so her natural snake-eating instincts do not drive her to eat her own young.

Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, wild king cobra populations are declining across [[India]], southern [[China]], and Southeast [[Asia]] due to deforestation, agricultural expansion, and illegal poaching for skin, meat, and traditional medicine.

*(Recent taxonomic studies have also proposed splitting the king cobra into four distinct regional species.)*
