The whitetip reef shark (*Triaenodon obesus*) is a slender, nocturnal requiem shark native to the coral reefs of the Indo-Pacific, easily recognized by the bright white tips on its first dorsal fin and upper tail lobe.

Unlike many open-ocean sharks that must swim constantly to breathe, it can pump water over its gills while resting motionless on the seafloor—often piling up in groups inside reef caves during the day.

A brownish-grey to slate-grey body with scattered dark spots and crisp white tips on the first dorsal fin and the upper lobe of the caudal (tail) fin (occasionally on the second dorsal and lower tail lobe as well).

Most adults measure around 1.6 meters (5.2 feet) long—rarely exceeding 2.1 meters (7 feet)—with a noticeably slim, flexible body, a short broad snout, and prominent oval eyes with vertical pupils.

Despite the similar name, the oceanic whitetip shark (*Carcharhinus longimanus*) is a much larger, bulkier, and far more aggressive open-ocean species with huge rounded pectoral fins.

Because they use buccal pumping to breathe while stationary, whitetip reef sharks spend daylight hours resting on sandy patches, beneath ledges, or stacked side-by-side like logs inside coral caves, returning to the same home caves for months or years.

After dusk, they become active hunters. Their slender, flexible bodies allow them to wriggle deep into narrow coral crevices to flush out sleeping reef fish, eels, octopuses, crabs, and spiny lobsters—sometimes foraging in coordinated groups where individuals block escape routes around the reef.

Generally shy and non-aggressive toward humans, they are a favorite sight for scuba divers and snorkelers across Hawaii, French Polynesia, the Great Barrier Reef, and the Red Sea. Bites are rare and typically happen only when a shark is provoked, cornered, or attracted by spearfishing.

Found in clear tropical waters from the [[Red Sea]] and East [[Africa]] across the [[Indian Ocean]] and [[Pacific Ocean]] to [[Hawaii]], the [[Galápagos Islands]], and [[Central America]], typically at depths of 8 to 40 meters.

*Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (previously Near Threatened), its populations face growing pressure from overfishing, coral reef degradation, and its slow reproductive rate (females give birth to just 1 to 5 live pups every two years).*
