The yeti crab (genus *Kiwa*) is a genus of deep-sea decapod crustaceans—specifically squat lobsters—famous for the dense, silky, hair-like bristles covering their claws and legs. 

*[[Deep Ocean]], [[Crustacea]]*

First discovered in 2005 near hydrothermal vents in the South [[Pacific Ocean]], they are so anatomically distinct that scientists placed them in their own new family, Kiwaidae.

Measuring roughly 15 cm (6 inches) in length, yeti crabs lack pigment and functional eyes—adaptations to living in total darkness more than 2,000 meters (6,500 feet) beneath the ocean surface:

- **Furry claws** (_setae_). Their elongated front claws (_chelipeds_) are covered in dense mats of flexible, hair-like filaments called **setae**, giving them the appearance of the mythical Himalayan Yeti.

- **Three described species**.
    - *Kiwa hirsuta* (the original "yeti crab," discovered south of Easter Island along the Pacific-Antarctic Ridge).
    - *Kiwa puravida* (discovered off Costa Rica near cold methane seeps).
    - *Kiwa tyleri* (the "Hoff crab," discovered in dense colonies around hydrothermal vents in the Southern Ocean near Antarctica).

Rather than using their furry claws to stay warm, yeti crabs use them as underwater gardens to cultivate their own food source:

- **Symbiotic bacteria**. The bristles on their claws host massive colonies of filamentous chemosynthetic bacteria. Because sunlight never reaches the deep ocean floor, these bacteria derive energy by oxidizing toxic chemicals—such as hydrogen sulfide and methane—pouring out of hydrothermal vents and cold seeps.

- **"Dancing" to feed their crops**. In species like _Kiwa puravida_, scientists observed the crabs rhythmically waving their claws back and forth over seafloor seeps. This "dancing" motion stirs the water around their bristles, supplying the bacteria with fresh oxygen, methane, and sulfide so they multiply faster.

- **Harvesting**. Using specialized comb-like mouthparts, the yeti crab regularly scrapes the bacteria off its arm hairs and eats them.

Yeti crabs live on a razor-thin thermal edge: the water shooting out of hydrothermal vents can exceed 380 °C (716 °F), while the surrounding abyssal ocean hovers near freezing (0 to 2 °C).

Around Antarctic vents, thousands of Kiwa tyleri pack together on top of one another in the narrow warm zone—staying close enough to avoid freezing to death, yet far enough back to avoid being cooked alive.
