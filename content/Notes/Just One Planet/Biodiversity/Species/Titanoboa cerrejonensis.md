Titanoboa (Titanoboa cerrejonensis, meaning "titanic boa from Cerrejón") is an extinct genus of giant boid snakes that lived in northeastern [[Colombia]] during the Middle to Late [[Paleocene]] epoch, roughly 60 to 58 million years ago—just a few million years after the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs. It is the largest, longest, and heaviest snake ever discovered.

*[[Reptiles]]*

Discovered in the early 2000s in the massive open-pit coal mines of Cerrejón (La Guajira, Colombia) and formally described in 2009, _Titanoboa_ dwarfed all modern snakes:

Based on over 180 fossilized vertebrae and ribs from roughly 30 individuals, paleontologists estimate adults reached 12.8 to 14.3 meters (42 to 47 feet) in length—longer than a school bus—and weighed around 1,135 kg (2,500 lbs / 1.25 tons), with a body thickness exceeding 1 meter at its widest point.

Despite its colossal size, its skeletal structure indicates it was closely related to modern anacondas and boa constrictors (family Boidae).

During the Paleocene, the Cerrejón region was a hot, humid, river-crossed tropical rainforest bordering an ancient sea:

Like modern green anacondas, Titanoboa would have struggled to move rapidly on dry land due to its immense weight, instead spending most of its time buoyed by water in swamps and rivers.

Fossils found alongside Titanoboa show it shared waterways with giant prehistoric crocodilians (*Cerrejonisuchus* and *Acherontisuchus*), giant freshwater turtles (*Carbonemys*), and large lungfish.

Initially thought to crush crocodilians and turtles via constriction, later skull and tooth analyses revealed numerous small, weakly attached teeth adapted for grasping and swallowing large fish, making it primarily a giant fish-eater (piscivore) that likely also preyed on crocodilians opportunistically.

Because snakes are cold-blooded (ectothermic) animals whose maximum body size is directly limited by ambient environmental temperature, *Titanoboa* served as a natural "paleothermometer" for scientists:

To sustain a metabolic rate capable of growing a 13-meter snake, average annual temperatures in equatorial South America 60 million years ago had to be roughly 30 to 34 °C (86 to 93 °F)—significantly warmer than modern tropical rainforests—demonstrating how tropical ecosystems thrived during intense Paleocene greenhouse warming.
