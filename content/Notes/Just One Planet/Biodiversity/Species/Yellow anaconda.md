The yellow anaconda (*Eunectes notaeus*), also known as the Paraguayan anaconda or curiyú, is a large, semi-aquatic boa endemic to southern [[South America]]. 

While noticeably smaller than its famous cousin, the green anaconda (*Eunectes murinus*), it is still one of the largest snakes in the world and is known for its striking golden-yellow coloration and defensive temperament.

A bright yellow, golden-tan, or greenish-yellow base color covered in a series of bold black or dark brown saddles, blotches, and spots along the back and flanks.

Adults typically measure 3 to 4 meters (10 to 13 feet) long and weigh 25 to 35 kg (55 to 77 lbs), though exceptionally large females can exceed 4.5 meters (15 feet) and 45 kg (100 lbs). Like other anacondas, females are significantly larger than males.

Its eyes and nostrils are positioned high on the top of its head, allowing the snake to breathe and watch for prey while keeping almost its entire body submerged underwater.

Unlike the green anaconda of the Amazon basin, the yellow anaconda inhabits marshes, swamps, flooded grasslands, and slow-moving rivers farther south—across [[Paraguay]], [[Bolivia]], southern [[Brazil]] (especially the Pantanal), northeastern [[Argentina]], and northern [[Uruguay]].

Non-venomous, it relies on stealth in murky water to ambush prey, wrapping its muscular coils around victims to suffocate or stop blood flow before swallowing them whole.

It feeds on wading birds, fish, turtles, frogs, lizards, small caimans, and semi-aquatic mammals such as capybaras, coypus (nutria), and peccaries.

Like all boas, yellow anacondas are ovoviviparous—females incubate eggs internally and give birth to 20 to 40+ live young at a time.

In the wild and in captivity, yellow anacondas are generally more high-strung and quicker to strike defensively than green anacondas or boa constrictors, making them suitable only for experienced reptile keepers with large, custom-built semi-aquatic enclosures.
