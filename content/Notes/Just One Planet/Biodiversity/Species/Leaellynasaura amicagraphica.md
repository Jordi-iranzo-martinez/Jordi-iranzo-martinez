*Leaellynasaura amicagraphica* ("Leaellyn's lizard") is a small, bipedal herbivorous dinosaur that lived in [[Australia]] during the Early [[Cretaceous]] period, roughly 118 to 110 million years ago.

Discovered at Dinosaur Cove in Victoria ([[Australia]]), it is famous as a specialized polar dinosaur that thrived inside the ancient Antarctic Circle—enduring months of continuous winter darkness and freezing temperatures.

Fossil specimens reveal a tail containing over 70 caudal vertebrae, measuring nearly three times the length of the rest of its body. Paleontologists suggest it may have had a coat of insulating proto-feathers or hair-like filaments and wrapped its long, fluffy tail around itself like a scarf to stay warm during polar cold snaps.

The holotype skull shows unusually large eye sockets and enlarged optic lobes in the brain, which many scientists interpret as an adaptation for acute low-light vision during months of polar winter darkness (though some researchers note the specimen may be a juvenile, which naturally have proportionally larger eyes).

Known specimens measure around 1 to 2 meters (3 to 6.5 feet) long—including that massive tail—and weighed roughly 3 to 10 kg (7 to 22 lbs).

Bone histology shows continuous growth without seasonal "growth rings" (lines of arrested growth) in young specimens, suggesting _Leaellynasaura_ was endothermic (warm-blooded) and remained active year-round rather than hibernating through winter.

During the Early Cretaceous, southeastern Australia was still connected to Antarctica and sat well inside the Antarctic Circle. While there were no permanent polar ice caps, the forested rift valley experienced cool temperatures (averaging around -2°C to 10°C) and extreme seasonal swings between 24-hour summer daylight and prolonged winter night.

Equipped with a sharp beak and ridged cheek teeth, it fed on low-growing polar vegetation such as ferns, horsetails, and cycads.

Fossils of multiple individuals found together suggest it lived in small herds or family groups, and nearby fossilized burrows in Victoria suggest small local ornithopods may have dug underground shelters to escape harsh winter weather.

*Described in 1989 by husband-and-wife paleontologists Tom Rich and Patricia Vickers-Rich, the genus is named after their daughter, Leaellyn Rich, while the species name amicagraphica honors the Friends of the Museum of Victoria and the National Geographic Society for supporting the excavation.*
