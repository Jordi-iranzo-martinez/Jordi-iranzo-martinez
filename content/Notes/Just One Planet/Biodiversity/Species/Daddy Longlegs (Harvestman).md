Cellar spiders (family Pholcidae), widely known as daddy longlegs spiders or vibrating spiders, are true spiders famous for their fragile, thread-like legs, messy cobwebs, and remarkable ability to prey on much larger, medically dangerous spiders. 

*[[Arachnids]]*

With over 1,800 described species worldwide, they are among the most common indoor spiders on every continent except Antarctica.

Cellar spiders have the classic two-part anatomy of true spiders:

They have a tiny, pale grey or tan cephalothorax paired with a long, cylindrical, or peanut-shaped abdomen (measuring 2 to 10 mm in length), supported by eight extremely long, delicate legs that can reach up to 50 mm (2 inches)—making their leg span roughly five to six times their body length.

Most species (such as the common long-bodied cellar spider, _Pholcus phalangioides_) have **eight eyes** arranged in two groups of three on either side with two smaller eyes in the middle, while some genera have six.

Cellar spiders rely on clever mechanical strategies rather than sticky silk or potent venom to survive:

- **Tangled, non-sticky webs**. They hang upside down in loose, irregular webs built in dark, sheltered corners—cellars, garages, ceilings, and under furniture. Because their silk is not adhesive, the tangled strands act as a tripwire snare that slows prey down.

- **Defensive "whirling."** When a predator (or a person) disturbs their web, cellar spiders rapidly gyrate their bodies in tight circles while keeping their feet anchored to the silk. This high-speed vibration turns them into a faint blur, making it difficult for birds, wasps, or jumping spiders to target them.

- **Giant-slayer hunting tactics**. Despite their frail build, cellar spiders routinely invade the webs of—and kill—much heavier spiders, including black widows, redbacks, **brown recluses**, and hobo spiders. Using their long legs to stay safely out of reach of their prey's fangs, they throw streams of silk to immobilize the target from a distance before delivering a bite.

Both halves of the myth are false: their short, unhinged fangs can occasionally penetrate thin human skin if pressed against it, and their venom is very weak—causing at most a brief, mild burning sensation lasting a few seconds with no lasting effects.

Because they quietly stay in ceiling corners, do not aggressively bite, and actively eliminate mosquitoes, flies, and venomous spiders, arachnologists generally consider them beneficial to keep indoors.
