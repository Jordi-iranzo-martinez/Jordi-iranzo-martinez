The Tuatara (*Sphenodon punctatus*) is a rare, lizard-like reptile endemic to [[New Zealand]]. 

*[[Reptiles]]*

Despite closely resembling an iguana or bearded dragon, it is not a lizard—it is the sole surviving member of the ancient reptile order Rhynchocephalia, a distinct evolutionary lineage that flourished alongside the dinosaurs over 200 million years ago.

The tuatara's lineage split from lizards and snakes roughly 250 million years ago during the Triassic period. Its name comes from the Māori language meaning "spiny back," referring to the crest of soft, triangular skin folds running down its neck and spine.

Tuataras possess a photoreceptive parietal eye on the top of their head, complete with a lens, cornea, and retina. In hatchlings, it is visible beneath a translucent scale before being covered by skin after a few months; scientists believe it helps regulate circadian rhythms, seasonal hormone cycles, and vitamin D absorption.

Unlike any living reptile, tuataras have two rows of teeth in their upper jaw that overlap a single row in the lower jaw, creating a shearing, saw-like bite. Because their teeth are fused directly to the jawbone rather than replaced, elderly tuataras eventually wear their teeth down to the bone and must switch to softer prey.

They lack external ear openings and eardrums, though they can still hear low-frequency sounds.

Unlike most reptiles that require warm temperatures to stay active, tuataras thrive in cool climates and remain active at temperatures as low as 5 °C (41 °F), emerging mostly at night to hunt beetles, crickets, worms, spiders, frogs, lizards, and seabird chicks.

Tuataras have the slowest growth rate of any reptile, continuing to grow for up to 35 years and reaching sexual maturity around 10 to 20 years old. They routinely live 60 to 100+ years in the wild—one famous captive male named Henry at New Zealand's Southland Museum fathered offspring for the first time at age 111.

Like crocodiles and sea turtles, the sex of tuatara hatchlings is determined by egg incubation temperature: cooler soil produces females, while slightly warmer soil produces males.

Once widespread across mainland New Zealand, tuataras were wiped out from the main islands after the arrival of humans and invasive mammalian predators (especially Polynesian rats, cats, and stoats) that ate their eggs and young.

Today, wild populations survive primarily on 32 predator-free offshore islands (such as Stephens Island in Cook Strait), as well as inside fenced mainland ecological sanctuaries like Zealandia in Wellington, where strict conservation programs monitor their recovery against rising global temperatures.
