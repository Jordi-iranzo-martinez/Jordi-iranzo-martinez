Pikaia gracilens is an extinct marine animal from the Middle [[Cambrian]] period (around 508 million years ago), famous for being one of the earliest known chordates and a close relative of the ancestors of all modern vertebrates (including humans). Its fossils come from the renowned [[Burgess Shale]] formation in British Columbia, Canada.

Measuring only about 3.8 to 6 centimeters (1.5–2.4 in) in length, it had a slender, ribbon-like, laterally compressed body resembling a small worm or a modern lancelet (_amphioxus_).

It possessed a notochord (a flexible rod running along its body that acted as an evolutionary precursor to the spinal column) along with V- or W-shaped segmented muscle blocks called myomeres, enabling it to swim through side-to-side undulations.

It featured a small, distinct head with two tiny sensory tentacles and a series of pharyngeal gill slits near the front, likely used to filter food particles from the water.

Discovered in 1911 by paleontologist Charles Doolittle Walcott, it was initially classified as a polychaete annelid worm due to its segmented appearance. In the 1970s, Simon Conway Morris reexamined the fossils and identified its notochord, turning _Pikaia_ into an icon of early vertebrate evolution—further popularized by Stephen Jay Gould in his book _Wonderful Life_.

For decades, paleontologists thought a tubular structure along one edge was a mysterious "dorsal organ" and placed its fin on the belly. However, a 2024 study demonstrated that classic reconstructions were inverted: that tube was actually its gut (on the ventral underside), while the fin and notochord ran along its back, resolving long-standing anatomical puzzles.
