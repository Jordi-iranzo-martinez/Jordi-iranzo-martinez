
The cuckoo catfish (*Synodontis multipunctatus*), also called the cuckoo squeaker, is a spotted freshwater catfish endemic to East [[Africa]]'s Lake Tanganyika, famous as the only known obligate brood parasite among fish.

*Named after cuckoo birds, it tricks mouthbrooding cichlids into incubating its eggs inside their mouths—where the catfish young hatch early and devour the host's own offspring.*

When mouthbrooding cichlids (such as Ctenochromis horei or Simochromis babaulti) lay and fertilize their eggs on the lakebed before scooping them into their mouths for protection, a group of cuckoo catfish rushes into the spawning chaos.

The catfish eat some of the cichlid eggs while simultaneously laying and fertilizing their own eggs right into the mix. In the confusion, the female cichlid scoops up both her remaining eggs and the catfish eggs, sealing them inside her mouth.

Cuckoo catfish embryos develop twice as fast as cichlid embryos, hatching in just 2 to 4 days compared to the cichlids' 6 to 7 days. Once hatched inside the safe shelter of the cichlid's mouth, the baby catfish feed on the unhatched cichlid eggs and newly hatched fry until the cichlid releases them as fully formed young catfish.

Reaching up to 27.5 cm (10.8 inches) in the wild (and typically ~15 cm in aquariums), it has a pale tan-to-silvery body covered in bold dark brown or black spots, three pairs of sensory barbels around its mouth, and white-edged fins.

Like other Synodontis catfish, it can produce audible squeaking or creaking noises by rubbing its pectoral fin spines against its shoulder girdle when stressed or handled.

Unlike many strictly nocturnal catfish, cuckoo catfish are active both day and night and thrive in social groups along rocky shorelines.

They are widely kept in African rift-lake aquariums alongside Lake Tanganyika or Lake Malawi cichlids, as they share the same hard, alkaline water preferences (pH 7.5–8.5, 24–27°C).

They are generally peaceful toward other species—especially when kept in a shoal of 3 to 4 or more—though solitary individuals can become territorial toward other Synodontis.
