Field crickets (subfamily Gryllinae, primarily genus Gryllus) are stout, dark brown or black insects belonging to the true cricket family (Gryllidae) and order Orthoptera (alongside grasshoppers and katydids).

*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Insects|Insects]]*

Ranging from 1.5 to 2.5 cm (0.6 to 1 inch) long, they are found in pastures, meadows, gardens, and roadsides worldwide.

Field crickets have large, rounded heads, long thread-like antennae, powerful hind legs built for jumping, and two rear sensory appendages (_cerci_). Adult females are easily distinguished by a long, needle-like **ovipositor** extending from the rear used to lay eggs in soil.

Like other crickets, their hearing organs (tympana) are located on their front legs just below the knee joints.

Only male field crickets chirp. They produce their familiar song by raising their leathery forewings and scraping a sharp edge on one wing against a toothed ridge ("file") on the other—using specialized wing areas ("harps" and "mirrors") as acoustic amplifiers to attract mates and defend territory.

Because crickets are cold-blooded, their chirping speeds up in warm weather and slows down when it cools—so reliably that counting chirps over 14–15 seconds can estimate air temperature.

In the wild, field crickets are an essential food source for birds, lizards, frogs, spiders, and small mammals.
