"Predator X" is the famous media nickname given to *Pliosaurus funkei*, a colossal, short-necked marine reptile (pliosaur) that ruled the oceans during the Late [[Jurassic 1]] period, roughly 150 million years ago. 

Unearthed on the [[Arctic]] archipelago of Svalbard, Norway, between 2006 and 2008, it was publicized upon its 2009 announcement as one of the largest and most powerful marine apex predators ever discovered.

Initially estimated by early media reports at 15 meters (50 feet), formal scientific description in 2012 refined the length of Pliosaurus funkei to roughly 10 to 12.8 meters (33 to 42 feet) and its weight to around 15 to 25 metric tons—still making it one of the largest pliosaurs known.

Its massive, crocodile-like skull measured roughly 2 to 2.5 meters (6.5 to 8.2 feet) long. Its jaws were lined with huge, trihedral (three-sided) conical teeth up to 30 cm (12 inches) long—built specifically for piercing, gripping, and tearing large prey apart.

Unlike mosasaurs or crocodiles that swam with undulating tails, pliosaurs propelled themselves through the water using four massive, wing-like flippers (each over 3 meters long). By alternating strokes like underwater wings, _Pliosaurus funkei_ achieved high acceleration and agile maneuverability in open ocean waters.

Using biomechanical CT scans of its skull and jaw muscle attachments, paleontologists calculated that Predator X possessed an astonishing bite force of roughly 15,000 to 33,000 newtons (over 3.3 metric tons of force per square inch)—up to four times stronger than Tyrannosaurus rex and roughly ten times stronger than any modern animal.

In the cold, deep Jurassic seas of the Boreal Basin, Pliosaurus funkei preyed on large marine reptiles—including long-necked plesiosaurs and dolphin-like ichthyosaurs—as well as giant squid-like cephalopods and large sharks.

Led by Norwegian paleontologist Jørn Hurum of the Natural History Museum in Oslo, excavation teams endured freezing winds, snowstorms, and polar bear patrols over three summers on Spitsbergen (Svalbard) to recover roughly 20,000 bone fragments from permafrost rock.

*While scientists studied the specimen and filmed a documentary, they referred to it as "Predator X." In 2012, the species was formally named Pliosaurus funkei in honor of Bjørn and May-Liss Funke, the volunteer fossil hunters who first spotted the bones eroding out of the Arctic hillside.*
