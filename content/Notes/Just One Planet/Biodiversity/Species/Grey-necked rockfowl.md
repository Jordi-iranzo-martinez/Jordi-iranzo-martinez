
The grey-necked rockfowl (*Picathartes oreas*), also known as the grey-necked picathartes, is an elusive, medium-sized passerine bird native to the closed-canopy rainforests of Central [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]]. 

*[[Birds]]*

Famous for its striking, featherless, brightly colored head and unique rock-nesting habits, it belongs to the ancient, two-species family Picathartidae.

Often referred to as the "grey ghost" of Central African forests, the bird combines understated body plumage with a dramatic facial pattern: 

Adults measure 33 to 38 cm in length and weigh between 200 and 250 grams, featuring a long neck, a long tail, and strong, muscular legs adapted for hopping across the forest floor.

Its bare, featherless skin is powder blue on the forehead and crown, bright carmine red on the back of the head (nape), and marked with large black patches behind the eyes.

It has slate-grey upperparts, a pale grey neck and chest, and lemon-yellow or creamy underparts.

The species has a patchy, fragmented range tied closely to specific geological features within primary tropical rainforests:

It is primarily found in Cameroon, Nigeria, Equatorial Guinea (including Bioko Island), Gabon, and the Republic of Congo. Recent camera-trap discoveries have also extended its known range southward into the western Democratic Republic of Congo and Angola's Cabinda province.

*[[Tropical Rainforest]]*

It favors hilly, forested terrain near streams and rivers with large boulders, caves, or overhanging rock faces.

Unlike most songbirds, the grey-necked rockfowl spends most of its time on or near the ground and rarely flies long distances:

It forages silently in the leaf litter for insects, spiders, worms, frogs, and small lizards, frequently following swarms of army (driver) ants to catch fleeing prey.

Monogamous pairs breed semi-colonially during the rainy season. They construct deep, cup-shaped nests out of mud and plant fibers attached directly to sheltered cave walls or overhanging cliffs to protect them from rain.
