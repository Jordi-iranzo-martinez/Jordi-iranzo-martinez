The brown widow (*Latrodectus geometricus*) is a venomous spider closely related to the black widow, easily recognized by its tan-to-dark-brown mottled body, banded legs, orange-to-yellow hourglass marking, and distinctive spiky egg sacs. While its venom is neurotoxic, bites are generally much milder than those of black widows because the spider is timid and injects very little venom.

Females range from light tan and gray to dark brown or nearly black, typically featuring a geometric pattern of black, white, yellow, or orange stripes and spots on the upper abdomen. 

Like black widows, brown widows have an hourglass shape on the underside of their abdomen, but it is bright orange or yellowish-orange (often wider at the bottom) rather than deep red.

Their legs are alternatingly striped with light brown and dark brown or black bands. 
The most unmistakable sign of a brown widow is its round, cream-colored egg sac covered in pointed silk spikes, resembling a sandspur or pollen grain.

Brown widows are shy and non-aggressive; when disturbed, they usually retreat or play dead rather than bite. Bites almost always happen when a spider is accidentally pressed against the skin.

Although drop-for-drop their venom is potent, brown widows deliver a tiny dose. Most bites cause localized pain, redness, and minor swelling rather than the severe muscle cramps (_latrodectism_) associated with black widows. 

Thought to have originated in Africa or South America, the brown widow has spread across warm, tropical, and subtropical regions worldwide—including the southern United States, parts of the Mediterranean, the Middle East, Asia, and Australia.

They spin messy, irregular webs in sheltered outdoor areas near buildings, such as under patio furniture, inside empty flower pots, under eaves, in recessed trash-can handles, and in garages.
