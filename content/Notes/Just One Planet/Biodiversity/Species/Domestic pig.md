The pig (*Sus scrofa domesticus*) is an omnivorous, even-toed ungulate mammal belonging to the family Suidae. Domesticated from the Eurasian wild boar (*Sus scrofa*) roughly 9,000 to 10,000 years ago, pigs are among the most widespread, intelligent, and economically significant domestic animals in the world.

*[[Mammalia]]*

Pigs have a stout body, short legs with four toes on each foot (walking on the two larger central hooves), and a cartilaginous, flat-tipped snout. This flexible snout is reinforced by a prenasal bone, allowing them to root and dig efficiently in soil for roots, tubers, and invertebrates.

They possess a remarkably acute sense of smell—hundreds of times stronger than a human's—which has historically made them useful for locating truffles. Their eyesight is relatively weak, though their hearing is well developed.

Because pigs have very few functional sweat glands, they rely on external cooling methods such as wallowing in mud or water. Mud not only lowers their body temperature as it evaporates, but also protects their skin from sunburn and parasites.

As opportunistic omnivores, pigs consume a wide variety of plant matter (grains, fruits, roots, leaves) as well as insects, worms, eggs, and small animals.

Scientific studies consistently rank pigs among the smartest domestic animals, demonstrating problem-solving abilities comparable to dogs and young children. They can learn complex tricks, recognize their own names, navigate mazes, and even use mirrors to locate hidden food.

In natural or free-range settings, female pigs (sows) and their offspring (piglets) live in cooperative family groups called sounders, while adult males (boars) are typically solitary outside the breeding season. They communicate using more than 20 distinct vocalizations ranging from soft grunts to high-pitched squeals.

Contrary to popular stereotypes, pigs are naturally clean animals. When given sufficient space, they strictly separate their sleeping and eating areas from where they defecate.

Archaeological and genetic evidence indicates that pigs were domesticated independently in at least two regions—the Near East (Anatolia) and East Asia (China)—around 9,000 years ago.

Today there are hundreds of domestic breeds worldwide, ranging from large commercial varieties (such as Yorkshire, Landrace, and Duroc) and heritage breeds (like the Iberian pig) to smaller varieties kept as companion animals (such as Vietnamese pot-bellied pigs and Göttingen minipigs).

Because pig physiology, organ size, and skin structure closely resemble those of humans, they play a vital role in biomedical research, including insulin production, heart valve replacements, and xenotransplantation studies.
