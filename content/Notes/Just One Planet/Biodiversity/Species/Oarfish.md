The oarfish (*Regalecidae* family) is a rare, deep-sea fish famous for its extraordinarily long, ribbon-like silver body and bright red dorsal crest. Holding the record as the world's longest bony fish, it is rarely seen alive and has inspired centuries of sea serpent legends and earthquake folklore.

[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Fishes|Fishes]]

The giant oarfish (_Regalecus glesne_) commonly grows up to 8 meters (26 feet) long, with unconfirmed sightings reaching up to 11 meters (36 feet).

It has a scaleless, metallic silver body adorned with dark spots and wavy markings, along with a vibrant crimson dorsal fin that runs the entire length of its back and forms a crown-like crest over its head.

Unlike most fish, oarfish often hang and swim vertically in the water column, using gentle undulations of their long dorsal fin to propel themselves.

Oarfish live in temperate and tropical oceans worldwide, typically inhabiting the mesopelagic ("twilight") zone at depths between 200 and 1,000 meters (650 to 3,300 feet).

Despite their intimidating, serpent-like size, they are completely harmless to humans. They lack teeth and feed primarily on tiny organisms such as krill, plankton, and small crustaceans.

Because they occasionally surface when sick, injured, or dying, ancient sailors often mistook their undulating red crests and massive bodies for mythical sea monsters.

In Japanese folklore, the oarfish is known as ryugu no tsukai ("Messenger from the Sea God's Palace"). Legend holds that when oarfish wash ashore, an earthquake or tsunami is imminent, though scientific studies have found no reliable link between oarfish strandings and seismic activity.
