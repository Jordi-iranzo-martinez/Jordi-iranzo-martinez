
*Polychaeta* (commonly known as polychaetes or bristle worms) is a large, diverse class of segmented marine worms within the phylum Annelida. 

Comprising more than 10,000 described species, they are among the most abundant and ecologically important organisms in the world's oceans, thriving everywhere from intertidal mudflats and coral reefs to deep-sea hydrothermal vents.

*The name Polychaeta comes from Ancient Greek meaning "many bristles", referring to their hallmark physical trait:*

- ***Parapodia and chaetae.** Nearly every body segment bears a pair of fleshy, paddle-like appendages called parapodia, tipped with bundles of stiff, chitinous bristles known as chaetae (or setae). These structures function as legs for crawling, paddles for swimming, anchors inside burrows, and even gill surfaces for breathing.*

- *Distinct head. Unlike earthworms and leeches (class Clitellata), polychaetes possess a well-developed head equipped with sensory tentacles, palps, eyes, and sometimes extendable jaws or feathery feeding crowns.*

Polychaetes are traditionally divided by lifestyle into free-moving hunters (Errantia) and tube-dwelling or burrowing species (Sedentaria):

- **Active predators**. Species like the bobbit worm (_Eunice aphroditois_) bury their bodies in the seafloor and ambush passing fish with snapping, scissor-like jaws, while ragworms (_Nereis_) roam tidal flats hunting small invertebrates.

- **Feathered filter-feeders**. Stationary tubeworms like Christmas tree worms (_Spirobranchus giganteus_) and feather duster worms (_Sabella_) build protective parchment or calcareous tubes and extend colorful, spiral crowns of tentacles (radioles) to trap plankton and absorb oxygen.

- **Extreme-environment specialists**. Giant hydrothermal vent tubeworms (_Riftia pachyptila_) lack a digestive tract entirely, surviving via symbiotic chemosynthetic bacteria in the pitch-black abyss, while bone-eating worms (_Osedax_) secrete acid to bore into sunken whale skeletons on the ocean floor.

- **Venomous fireworms**. Species such as the bearded fireworm (_Hermodice carunculata_)—native to the Atlantic and Mediterranean—possess hollow, white venom-filled bristles that break off on contact, causing an intense burning sting.

Many marine polychaetes reproduce by transforming part or all of their bodies into swimming, gamete-filled forms called epitokes (such as the famous Pacific palolo worm), rising to the ocean surface in synchronized lunar swarms to spawn.

By burrowing through seafloor sediments (bioturbation), polychaetes oxygenate mudflats, recycle nutrients, and serve as a foundational food source for fish, shorebirds, and crustaceans.
