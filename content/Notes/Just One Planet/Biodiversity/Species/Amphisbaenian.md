
An amphisbaenian (commonly known as a worm lizard) is a group of mostly legless, burrowing reptiles belonging to the clade Amphisbaenia. 

Named after the mythical two-headed serpent (amphisbaena, meaning "goes both ways"), they are uniquely adapted for life underground and can move both forward and backward with ease.

*In ancient Greek legend, the Amphisbaena was a venomous, two-headed serpent born from the blood dripping off Medusa's severed head as Perseus flew over the Libyan desert.*

With over 200 known species, most have elongated, cylindrical bodies covered in smooth scales arranged in distinct rings (_annuli_). Many species have pinkish, skin-like coloration, making them look remarkably like giant earthworms.

Nearly all amphisbaenians lack legs. The only exception is the Mexican mole lizard (_Bipes_), which retains two sturdy, mole-like front legs used for digging.

They possess heavily reinforced, bullet-shaped or shovel-shaped skulls that act as battering rams to push through compacted soil, along with powerful jaws and interlocking teeth for crushing prey.

Because they spend almost their entire lives underground, their eyes are tiny, covered by translucent scales, and capable only of detecting light and dark.

Their blunt tails look very similar to their heads, which confuses predators. They move underground using accordion-like muscle contractions that allow them to travel equally well forward or backward.

They are carnivorous predators that hunt subterranean invertebrates such as ants, termites, beetles, and earthworms, though larger species can also eat small vertebrates.

Amphisbaenians are found primarily in tropical and subtropical regions of [[South America]], [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]], the [[Caribbean]], and the [[Mediterranean]] (including the Iberian Peninsula), with a few species in [[North America]].
