

The mountain stone wētā (*Hemideina maori*) is a large, flightless, nocturnal insect endemic to [[New Zealand]]'s South Island, famous as the largest known insect in the world capable of surviving being frozen solid. 

Despite its name, it is technically classified as a tree wētā (*Hemideina*) that adapted to live in rocky alpine environments above the tree line.

During harsh alpine winters, mountain stone wētā can survive temperatures down to -10°C (14°F) for weeks at a time with 80% to 85% of their body water frozen.

Instead of trying to prevent freezing entirely, their hemolymph (insect blood) contains special ice-nucleating proteins and cryoprotectants (like proline and trehalose). These draw water out of cells and encourage ice crystals to form safely outside cell walls, preventing fatal cellular damage.

When spring arrives or temperatures warm up, they thaw out over several hours and resume normal activity.

Growing up to 6 cm (2.4 inches) in body length, they appear in two main color morphs: striking yellow-and-black stripes or a darker mahogany brown and black.

Males have disproportionately large heads and powerful jaws used to fight rivals over prime rock crevices, where a dominant male will guard a harem of up to seven females.

Because they lack wings, they rely on hiding under rock slabs (tors) by day. If threatened by predators like native owls (ruru), they play dead and regurgitate dark fluid.

They inhabit rocky outcrops and scree slopes at elevations of 1,100 to 1,500+ meters across the South Island's mountain ranges—with one unusual low-altitude exception on two predator-free islands in Lake Wānaka (Mou Waho and Mou Tapu), where ancestors likely drifted across on logs.

At night, they emerge to feed on small invertebrates as well as high-energy, lipid-rich alpine plants and berries (such as mountain snowberries) to fuel their survival.
