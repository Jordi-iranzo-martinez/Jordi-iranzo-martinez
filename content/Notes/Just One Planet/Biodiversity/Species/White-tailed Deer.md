The white-tailed deer (*Odocoileus virginianus*), commonly known as the whitetail or Virginia deer, is a medium-sized deer native to [[North America]], Central America, and northern [[South America]].

Named for the bright white underside of its tail—which it raises like a warning flag when startled—it is the most widely distributed and abundant wild ungulate in the Americas.

*[[Mammals]]*

Its fur changes color twice a year for camouflage. In spring and summer, it sports a thin, reddish-brown coat; in autumn and winter, it sheds into a thicker, hollow-haired greyish-brown coat that provides heavy insulation.

The upper side of its tail is brown, while the underside is pure white. When sensing danger, the deer flips its tail upright and bounds away—bounding at speeds up to 50–60 km/h (30–37 mph)—to alert nearby herd members and help fawns follow their mother through dense brush.

Adult males (bucks) grow branching bony antlers starting in spring, covered in blood-rich skin called _velvet_. By late summer, the velvet dries and is rubbed off against trees before the autumn mating season (the rut), and the antlers are shed each winter. Unlike mule deer, whose antlers fork evenly, whitetail antlers grow unbranched tines rising from a single main forward-curving beam.

Size varies dramatically by latitude (following Bergmann's rule). Northern bucks in Canada and the northern U.S. can exceed 135–180 kg (300–400 lbs), whereas tropical subspecies and the endangered Florida Key deer (_O. v. clavium_) weigh only 20–35 kg (45–75 lbs).

White-tailed deer thrive in forest edges, deciduous woodlands, brushlands, swamps, prairies, and suburban parks from southern Canada through Mexico and Central America down to Peru, Bolivia, and Brazil.

Equipped with a four-chambered stomach, they browse selectively on tender shoots, leaves, twigs, acorns, wild fruits, fungi, and agricultural crops, feeding most actively at dawn and dusk (crepuscular).

Females (does) typically give birth to one to three spotted fawns in late spring. Newborn fawns have almost no scent and remain motionless in tall grass for their first few weeks while their mother forages nearby, returning periodically to nurse them.


Overhunted to near-extirpation in much of eastern North America by the late 19th century, strict conservation laws and the decline of natural apex predators (such as wolves and cougars) allowed whitetail populations to rebound to over 30 million individuals today.

In areas without natural predators, overabundant deer herds can overbrowse forest understories, reducing plant diversity, spreading tick-borne illnesses (such as Lyme disease), and causing frequent vehicle collisions.
