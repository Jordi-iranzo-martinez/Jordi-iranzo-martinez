*Elasmotherium sibiricum* (popularly known as the "Siberian unicorn") was an extinct genus of giant rhinoceroses that inhabited the Eurasian steppes from the Late Pliocene to the Late Pleistocene, disappearing around 39,000 years ago.

[[Cenozoic]]

Its best-known species, _Elasmotherium sibiricum_, overlapped in time and range with early modern humans ([[Homo sapiens]]) and [[Homo neanderthalensis]].

Comparable in scale to a woolly mammoth, it reached 4.5 to 5 meters (15–16 ft) in length, stood over 2 meters (6.6 ft) tall at the shoulder, and weighed an estimated 3.5 to 4.5 metric tons.

Unlike modern rhinoceroses (whose horns sit atop their nasal bones), _Elasmotherium_'s skull features a massive, heavily vascularized bony dome directly on its forehead. For decades it was reconstructed with a giant keratin horn up to 1.5–2 meters long, though recent anatomical studies suggest it may have sported a much shorter, broader keratinous boss or helmet-like structure instead.

It possessed relatively long, slender legs compared to other rhinos of its era, giving it the ability to trot and gallop efficiently across open grasslands.

It lacked incisors and canines but had high-crowned (_hypsodont_), ever-growing molars with complex enamel folds—giving rise to its name, meaning "plated beast"—ideally suited for grinding tough, abrasive steppe grasses.

It lived primarily across the arid and semi-arid steppes of Central [[Asia]], southern [[Russia]], Kazakhstan, and Eastern [[Europe]].

Isotopic analyses of fossilized remains show it was an extreme grazing specialist that fed almost exclusively on dry, tough steppe grasses, unlike contemporary herbivores (such as saiga antelopes or woolly rhinos) that had more flexible diets.

Long thought to have died out around 200,000 years ago, radiocarbon dating published in 2018 revealed it survived until roughly 39,000 years ago. Its extinction is linked to Late Pleistocene cooling and aridification, which shrank the open grasslands it depended on while its specialized anatomy prevented it from switching to shrubs or lichens.
