
The African fish eagle (Icthyophaga vocifer, formerly Haliaeetus vocifer) is a large, iconic bird of prey native to sub-Saharan [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]]. 

Famous for its striking white-and-chestnut plumage and ringing, evocative call—often described as "the voice of Africa"—it serves as the national bird of Malawi, Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe.

Closely related to North America's bald eagle, the African fish eagle shares a similar appearance with several distinct features:

*[[Bald eagle]]*

Adults have a snow-white head, chest, and tail contrasting sharply with a rich chestnut-brown belly and shoulders, and black flight feathers. Juveniles have mottled brown-and-white plumage until reaching maturity at around four to five years of age.

Adults measure 63 to 75 cm in length with a wingspan of 1.75 to 2.1 meters. As with most raptors, females are significantly larger than males, weighing 3.2 to 3.6 kg compared to 2.0 to 2.5 kg for males. 

Its toes feature rough, spiny projections called spiricules alongside strong curved talons, giving it a secure grip on slippery fish.]

Found across most of sub-Saharan Africa near large, open bodies of freshwater or coastal lagoons—such as the [[Okavango Delta]], Zambezi River, and Rift Valley lakes—it relies on tall waterside trees for perching and nesting.

Unlike the osprey, which plunges completely underwater, the African fish eagle watches from a high perch and swoops down to snatch fish (such as catfish, tilapia, cichlids, and lungfish) right from the water's surface.

While it typically catches fish under 1 kg, it can seize prey weighing over 4 kg. If a fish is too heavy to lift into the air, the eagle drags it across the surface or drops into the water and uses its wings like oars to paddle to shore.

Beyond fish, it hunts waterbirds (including flamingos, ducks, and herons), small reptiles, and mammals, scavenges carrion, and frequently engages in kleptoparasitism—stealing catches from Goliath herons, pelicans, and ospreys.

Pairs bond for life and defend year-round territories along waterways. They build massive stick nests—which can grow over 2 meters wide after years of reuse—typically laying 1 to 3 eggs during the dry season.

According to BirdLife International, the species is classified as Least Concern, with a stable wild population estimated at around 300,000 individuals.
