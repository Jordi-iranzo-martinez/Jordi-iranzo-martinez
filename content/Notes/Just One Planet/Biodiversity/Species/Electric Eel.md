The Electric Eel (genus *Electrophorus*) is a South American freshwater fish famous for its ability to generate powerful electric shocks—up to 860 volts—to stun prey and defend against predators. 

Despite its name and snake-like appearance, it is not a true eel, but a type of knifefish (order Gymnotiformes) more closely related to catfish and carp.

*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Fishes|Fishes]], [[South America]]*

For over two centuries, scientists believed there was only one species (_Electrophorus electricus_).

In 2019, genetic and ecological studies revealed three distinct species across the [[Amazon]] and Orinoco river basins:

- ***Electrophorus electricus***. Native to the Guiana Shield, generating shocks up to around 650 volts.
- ***Electrophorus varii***. Found in murky, slow-moving lowland rivers of the Amazon basin.
- ***Electrophorus voltai***. Inhabits clearwater highland rivers and holds the record as the strongest bioelectricity generator on Earth, producing discharges measured at 860 volts.

Adults grow up to 2.5 meters (8.2 feet) long and weigh up to 20 kg (44 lbs). They have a scaleless, dark grey-brown body with a bright orange or yellow throat and a single long anal fin running along the underside for propulsion.

Although they have gills, electric eels obtain roughly 80% of their oxygen by gulping air at the water's surface every 10 minutes through a richly vascularized mouth lining. This allows them to thrive in stagnant, oxygen-poor swamps and floodplains.

Nearly 80% of the eel's body is packed with specialized organs made of thousands of modified muscle cells called electrocytes stacked like batteries in series:

- **Main Organ and Hunter's Organ**. Generate high-voltage pulses used to stun prey or deter attackers.

- **Sach's Organ**. Produces weak, continuous low-voltage pulses used like radar to navigate murky water and locate hidden prey (electrolocation).

High-voltage pulses do not just shock prey—they hijack the prey's nervous system, forcing involuntary muscle contractions. By emitting rapid doublets of pulses, an electric eel can trigger hidden fish to twitch, revealing their location in muddy water before delivering a paralyzing volley.

When threatened by land predators or wading animals, electric eels can press their chins directly against the threat—or even leap partially out of the water—to maximize the current delivered into the target.

While long thought to be strictly solitary, researchers have documented groups of Electrophorus voltai coordinating in Amazonian lakes to herd schools of small fish into tight balls and shock them simultaneously.
