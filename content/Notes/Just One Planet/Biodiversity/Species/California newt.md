
The California newt (*Taricha torosa*) is a poisonous salamander endemic to [[California]], easily recognized by its rough, grainy brown back and vivid orange-yellow belly.

Spending most of the year in woodlands and chaparral before migrating to ponds and slow streams to breed, it is famous for secreting tetrodotoxin—one of the most potent neurotoxins in nature.

*[[Amphibians]]*

Adults grow to 12.5 to 20 centimeters (5–8 in) in total length. During their terrestrial phase, they have dry, warty, yellowish-brown to dark brown upper skin that contrasts sharply with a bright orange or yellow underside.

When males enter water during the winter and spring rainy season, their skin turns smooth and slimy, their tails flatten into swimming fins, and they develop dark, rough pads on their toes to grip females during mating.

It closely resembles the rough-skinned newt (_Taricha granulosa_), but can be told apart by its light-colored lower eyelids and large eyes that protrude slightly beyond the outline of its head when viewed from above.

Skin glands in adult California newts—as well as their eggs—contain tetrodotoxin (TTX), the same lethal neurotoxin found in pufferfish and blue-ringed octopuses. While safe to observe (and harmless to touch briefly if hands are washed thoroughly afterward), ingesting even a single newt can be fatal to humans and pets.

When threatened by a predator, the newt performs a defensive behavior called the _unken_ reflex: it arches its back, raises its head, and lifts its tail straight up into the air to flash its bright orange belly as a clear visual warning.

Almost no predators can eat California newts except certain populations of the common garter snake (*Thamnophis sirtalis*), which have evolved genetic mutations giving them extreme resistance to tetrodotoxin.

Endemic to [[California]], it inhabits coastal ranges from Mendocino County south to San Diego County, as well as the foothills of the Sierra Nevada (where the Sierra newt, _Taricha sierrae_, was historically treated as a subspecies).

Adults feed on earthworms, slugs, snails, woodlice, aquatic insects, and occasionally the eggs or larvae of other amphibians (including their own species).

While still locally abundant in northern California, southern coastal populations are designated as a California Species of Special Concern due to urbanization, road mortality during winter breeding migrations, severe droughts, and predation by invasive red swamp crayfish and mosquitofish.
