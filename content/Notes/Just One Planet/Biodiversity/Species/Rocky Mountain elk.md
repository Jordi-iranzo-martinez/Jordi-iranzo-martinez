The Rocky Mountain elk (*Cervus canadensis nelsoni*), the largest and most widespread subspecies of elk in [[North America]]. 

Famous for their massive branching antlers and haunting autumn "bugle" calls, mature bulls are among the most iconic big-game mammals of the North American West.

Mature bulls typically weigh 315 to 450 kg (700 to 1,000+ lbs) and stand about 1.5 meters (5 feet) tall at the shoulder, making them significantly larger than female cows (~225 kg / 500 lbs).

They have a light tan-to-copper body contrasting with a dark brown neck mane, dark legs, and a distinctive creamy-beige rump patch.

Only bulls grow antlers, which are shed every spring and regrown over the summer at up to 2.5 cm (1 inch) per day while covered in nutrient-rich velvet. Of all living elk subspecies, Rocky Mountain bulls grow the largest antlers—a mature "royal" bull typically sports six points on each side (6×6), with beams reaching over 1.2 meters (4 feet) long and weighing up to 18 kg (40 lbs) per pair.

During the autumn breeding season (the rut, from late August through October), bulls produce an unmistakable high-pitched whistle that drops into a deep, resonant roar and ends in a series of grunts—used to attract females and warn away rivals.

Dominant bulls gather and defend harems of 15 to 20 or more cows, clashing antlers in intense shoving matches against challenger bulls to prove their strength.

Bulls dig muddy pits ("wallows"), urinate in them, and roll around to coat themselves in strong-smelling mud as a chemical signal of dominance.

Native to the Rocky Mountains and adjacent ranges from British Columbia and Alberta down to New Mexico and Arizona (and widely reintroduced across eastern North America), they spend summers grazing in high alpine meadows before migrating down to sheltered valleys and foothills for winter.

Primarily grazers, they feed mainly on grasses and sedges, supplementing with shrubs, bark, and aspen shoots during deep snow.
