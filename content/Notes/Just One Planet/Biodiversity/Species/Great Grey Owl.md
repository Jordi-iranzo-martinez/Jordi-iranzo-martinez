The Great Grey Owl (*Strix nebulosa*) is the world's largest species of owl by length, native to the dense boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere.

Often called the "Phantom of the North," it is famous for its massive round facial disc, piercing yellow eyes, and extraordinary ability to hunt rodents hidden deep beneath winter snow.

*[[Birds]]*

Adults measure 60 to 84 cm (24 to 33 inches) in length with a wingspan averaging 140 to 152 cm (4.5 to 5 feet). Despite this imposing frame, it weighs only about 0.8 to 1.7 kg (1.8 to 3.7 lbs)—less than a great horned owl or snowy owl—because almost all of its bulk consists of thick, fluffy feathers and a long tail.

It has the largest facial disc of any raptor, marked with concentric dark and light grey rings that funnel sound directly to its ears. Below its yellow beak sits a crisp white mustache-like patch often nicknamed a "bow tie."

Unlike horned or screech owls, the great grey owl has a completely smooth, rounded head.

During harsh northern winters, voles and mice tunnel beneath deep snow. Relying entirely on hearing rather than sight, a great grey owl can detect a rodent moving under up to 60 cm (2 feet) of snow.

Once it pinpoints its prey, the owl glides silently over the snow and dives headfirst, punching through hard snow crusts with its talons to snatch the rodent.

Its ear openings are positioned at slightly different heights on its skull, allowing its brain to triangulate the exact horizontal and vertical location of faint sounds beneath the snow.

It is the only owl species in the genus Strix found in both North America (across [[Canada]], [[Alaska]], and the northern [[Rocky Mountains]]) and Eurasia (across [[Scandinavia]], [[Russia]], and [[Siberia]]).

Like other Strix owls, they do not build their own nests. Instead, they take over abandoned stick nests built by hawks, ravens, or eagles, or nest atop broken tree stumps.
