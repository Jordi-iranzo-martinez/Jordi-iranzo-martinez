Leaf insects (family Phylliidae, also known as walking leaves) are herbivorous insects in the order Phasmatodea famous for evolving flat, leaf-shaped bodies that provide near-perfect camouflage. Native to the tropical rainforests of South and Southeast Asia, Australia, and Melanesia, over 100 known species spend their lives blending into tree canopies.

[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Insects|Insects]]

Their broad, flattened bodies and widened leg extensions feature leaf-like veins, stems, and even brown spots or "bite marks" that resemble aging or damaged foliage.

When walking or threatened, leaf insects rock back and forth in a slow "shimmer" to mimic a real leaf blowing in the breeze.

Newly hatched nymphs start out dark red or brown to resemble forest debris or ants, turning bright green after they begin feeding on leaves.

Females are large (up to 10–15 cm depending on the species), broad, and flightless with large leaf-like forewings. Males are much smaller, slimmer, and capable of flight, sporting long antennae and transparent hindwings.

In the absence of males, females can reproduce asexually through parthenogenesis, laying unfertilized eggs that hatch exclusively into female clones.

Females drop hard, seed-shaped eggs to the forest floor. Some species have eggs with a nutrient-rich cap (capitulum) that attracts ants, which carry the eggs underground and inadvertently protect them until they hatch.

In the wild, they feed on tropical foliage

*[[Tropical Rainforest]]*

 They are completely harmless and non-venomous, relying entirely on stillness, camouflage, or dropping to the ground to play dead when startled.
