The American bullfrog (*Lithobates catesbeianus*) is the largest true frog in [[North America]] and one of the world’s most notorious invasive species.

*[[Amphibians]]*

Adults typically measure 9 to 15 cm (3.5 to 6 inches) in body length—with exceptional individuals reaching up to 20 cm (8 inches)—and can weigh up to 800 grams (1.75 pounds).

They have large, circular external eardrums directly behind their eyes. In males, the tympanum is significantly larger than the eye; in females, it is roughly the same size as the eye.

Their backs range from olive-green to dark brown, often with darker mottling, while their undersides are cream or white with gray marbling. Unlike many other frogs, they lack dorsolateral folds (ridges of skin running down the back).

Males produce a loud, deep, resonant bellow—often described as sounding like _"jug-o-rum"_ or a cow mooing—that can be heard over 1 kilometer (0.6 miles) away during the breeding season.

Native to eastern North America (from southeastern [[Canada]] through the eastern [[United States of America]] to northeastern [[Mexico]]), they rely on permanent, calm, warm bodies of fresh water such as lakes, ponds, marshes, and slow-moving streams.

Adult bullfrogs are nocturnal "sit-and-wait" carnivores that will eat virtually anything they can fit into their mouths. Their diet includes insects, crayfish, fish, other frogs (including smaller bullfrogs), snakes, small turtles, birds, and rodents.

Females can lay up to 20,000 eggs in a single floating film. Depending on water temperature, tadpoles can take anywhere from a few months to three years to metamorphose into frogs, growing up to 15 cm (6 inches) long in the larval stage.

Introduced to over 40 countries across four continents—often for frog-leg farming, pest control, or via the aquarium and pet trade—the American bullfrog is listed among the [100 of the World's Worst Invasive Alien Species](https://www.iucngisd.org/gisd/species.php?sc=80).

Outside their native range, they devastate local ecosystems by preying on native amphibians, reptiles, and fish, outcompeting native species for food, and acting as asymptomatic carriers of chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*) and ranavirus, diseases responsible for global amphibian declines.
