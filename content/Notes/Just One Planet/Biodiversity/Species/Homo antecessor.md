*Homo antecessor* ("pioneer" or "explorer human") is an extinct archaic human species that lived in Western Europe roughly 800,000 to 900,000 years ago (with related remains dating up to 1.2 million years ago).

First discovered in 1994 at the Gran Dolina cave site in Spain's Sierra de [[Atapuerca]] (near Burgos), it is one of the earliest known hominin species in Europe and completely overturned the idea that humans arrived on the continent only half a million years ago.

Homo antecessor is famous for having a remarkably modern-looking mid-face—complete with sunken cheekbones (canine fossae) and a projecting nose similar to Homo sapiens—paired with primitive features like a prominent double-arched brow ridge, a receding forehead, and no chin.

They stood around 1.60 to 1.85 meters (5'3"–6'1") tall with a sturdy, broad-chested build weighing roughly 60–90 kg. Their brain capacity was around 1,000 cm³, smaller than modern humans' (~1,350 cm³).

Their teeth retained archaic traits, including large premolars with multiple roots and shovel-shaped incisors.

They crafted simple pebble and flake tools belonging to Mode 1 (Oldowan) technology, using quartz and flint to butcher game.

They hunted and scavenged deer, horses, wild cattle, and rhinos across the warm woodlands and grasslands of Early Pleistocene Iberia.

Fossilized human bones from Gran Dolina show clear stone-tool cut marks and fractures identical to those on butchered animal bones. Because this occurred during periods when food was abundant rather than during famines, researchers believe they practiced gastronomic or cultural cannibalism—eating rival groups.

Named in 1997 by paleoanthropologist José María Bermúdez de Castro and his team, Homo antecessor was initially proposed as the last common ancestor of *Neanderthals* and modern humans.

A landmark 2020 paleoproteomic study analyzing 800,000-year-old tooth enamel proteins concluded that Homo antecessor was likely a closely related sister lineage to the common ancestor of Homo sapiens, Neanderthals, and Denisovans rather than a direct ancestor.
