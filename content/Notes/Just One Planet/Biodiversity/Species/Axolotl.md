The axolotl (*Ambystoma mexicanum*), also known as the Mexican walking fish, is a critically endangered aquatic salamander native to [[Mexico]] City, famous for remaining in its larval form throughout adulthood and for its extraordinary ability to regenerate entire limbs and organs.

 *[[Amphibians]]*


Unlike most salamanders, which undergo metamorphosis to lose their gills and move onto land, axolotls retain their juvenile traits—including three pairs of feathery external gills behind the head and a dorsal tail fin—reaching sexual maturity while remaining fully aquatic.

Adults typically measure 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 inches) long (occasionally up to 45 cm) and live 10 to 15 years. While wild axolotls are dark brown, gray, or olive with black mottling for camouflage, selective breeding in laboratories and the aquarium trade has produced well-known pink-white leucistic (white with black eyes), albino, golden, and melanistic color morphs.

Axolotls are a premier model organism in regenerative medicine because they can regrow severed limbs, tails, spinal cords, heart tissue, eyes, and even parts of their brains without scarring.

In the wild, axolotls are native exclusively to the ancient lake and canal complex of Lake Xochimilco (and historically Lake Chalco) in southern Mexico City, preferring cool, slow-moving, plant-rich waters (ideally 14–18 °C / 57–64 °F).

Axolotls have tiny, cone-shaped vestigial teeth meant for gripping rather than chewing. They hunt primarily at night by opening their mouths rapidly to create a vacuum that sucks in worms, mollusks, crustaceans, insect larvae, and small fish whole.

*Named after Xolotl, the Aztec god of fire and lightning who transformed himself into an āxōlōtl ("water dog" or "water monster") to avoid being sacrificed, the species remains a major Mexican cultural icon—even appearing on Mexico's 50-peso banknote.*

Although millions exist in laboratories and home aquariums worldwide, the axolotl is listed as Critically Endangered on the [IUCN Red List](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESbgHEIkXXT-TIUTDSrW_NeeEwfFFDJkQ3RyM521R5mDpGmPgJEO24WpD_Mi5IgT_FSWvp1R5c574nNVHBpz-p33O5eg-CJ2ByVzxVpTNsqnvVkFob9QS0SxAKB7vnP3w_X52kJKqNLxqTtWPQlFdE) (with estimates of only 50 to 1,000 mature individuals left in the wild) and protected under CITES Appendix II.

Urban expansion, water pollution, and introduced predatory fish—particularly common carp and Nile tilapia—have devastated their native habitat, prompting conservation projects like _Refugio Chinampa_ to restore traditional Aztec floating gardens (_chinampas_) as filtered, predator-free sanctuaries.
