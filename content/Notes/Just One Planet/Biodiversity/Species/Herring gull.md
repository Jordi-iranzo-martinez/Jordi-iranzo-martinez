The Herring gull (*Larus argentatus*) is a large, noisy, and highly adaptable gull native to northern and western [[Europe]]. 

*Often considered the quintessential "seagull," it is famous for its light grey back, pink legs, yellow bill with a red spot, and bold, opportunistic foraging habits in both coastal and urban environments.*

Adults measure 55 to 67 cm (22 to 26 inches) in length with a wingspan of 125 to 155 cm (4.1 to 5.1 feet). They have a crisp white head and underparts, a pale silver-grey back and upper wings, black wingtips with white "mirrors," pink legs, and a heavy yellow bill marked with a bright red spot near the tip of the lower mandible.

Young herring gulls look completely different from adults. First-year birds are mottled brown all over with dark bills and eyes, gradually shedding their brown feathers over four years before acquiring the clean grey-and-white adult plumage.

The red spot on an adult's lower bill serves as a visual target for newly hatched chicks. When hungry, a chick instinctively pecks at the red spot, triggering the parent to regurgitate food.

Herring gulls are remarkably intelligent, opportunistic omnivores. Along coasts, they eat fish, crabs, starfish, and mussels—famously flying high and dropping hard-shelled mollusks onto rocks or paved roads to crack them open. They also rhythmically "patter" (stamp) their feet on wet sand or grassy fields to mimic the vibrations of rainfall, tricking earthworms and burrowing invertebrates to come to the surface.

In recent decades, many populations have moved inland to cities and towns, nesting on flat building rooftops and scavenging around fishing ports, landfills, and seaside boardwalks.
