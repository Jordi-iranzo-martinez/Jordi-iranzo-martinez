The Chinese green tree viper (*Trimeresurus stejnegeri*), also known as Stejneger's bamboo pit viper or simply the bamboo viper, is a venomous arboreal pit viper endemic to East and Southeast [[Asia]]. 

Famous for its bright emerald-green camouflage, piercing red or orange eyes, and rusty-red tail tip, it is one of the most widespread—and medically significant—green pit vipers in the region.

*[[Reptiles]]*

Adults reach up to 75 to 90 centimeters (30 to 35 inches) in total length. They have a vibrant grass-green upper body and a pale green or whitish belly separated by a distinct lateral stripe—typically white in females, and bicolored (white over red or orange) in males.

Like other pit vipers, it has a broad, triangular head distinct from its neck, vertically elliptical pupils set in bright coppery-red or orange irises, and heat-sensing loreal pits between its eyes and nostrils. The tip of its prehensile tail is distinctly reddish-brown.

Native to southern [[China]], Taiwan, Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, Nepal, and northeastern [[India]], it lives in forests, bamboo thickets, and vegetated areas near mountain streams up to 2,000 meters (6,600 feet) in elevation.

It spends the day coiled motionless among branches and leaves. At night, it uses its heat-sensing pits to ambush frogs, lizards, small birds, and rodents.

Unlike egg-laying snakes, it is viviparous (gives birth to live young), typically producing litters of 2 to 15 fully independent, venomous offspring during the summer.

Its venom consists primarily of hemotoxins and necrotoxins. Bites cause immediate, intense burning pain, rapid swelling, severe bruising, and localized tissue necrosis around the puncture wounds, though fatalities in humans are relatively rare with prompt medical treatment.

Because it blends so well into foliage and adapts easily to agricultural edges and tea plantations, it accounts for a high percentage of venomous snakebites in places like Taiwan and southern China.
