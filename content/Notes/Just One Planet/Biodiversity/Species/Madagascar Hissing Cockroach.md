The Madagascar hissing cockroach (*Gromphadorhina portentosa*) is one of the largest species of cockroach in the world, endemic to the island of [[Madagascar]] and famous for the loud hissing sound it produces when disturbed or courting.

*[[Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Insects|Insects]]*

Unlike household pest cockroaches, it has no wings, moves slowly, does not bite, and lives cleanly on rotting wood and fallen fruit on the forest floor—making it a popular pet and science classroom animal.

Most insects make sounds by rubbing body parts together (like crickets or cicadas), but hissing cockroaches produce sound like mammals do—by forcefully expelling air through modified breathing pores (**spiracles**) along the sides of their abdomen.

Males use different types of hisses for specific social purposes:

- **Alarm/Disturbance Hiss**. Used by both males and females when startled or handled to deter predators.

- **Aggressive Hiss**. Used by males during territorial battles over females.

- **Courtship Hiss**. A softer, rhythmic hiss produced by males while stroking a female's antennae before mating.

Adults grow 5 to 7.5 cm (2 to 3 inches) long with a glossy, reddish-brown to black oval exoskeleton. Neither sex ever develops wings.

Adult males are easily distinguished by two prominent, horn-like bumps on their pronotum (the shield behind the head), which they use like rams when pushing rival males, whereas females have a smoother shield.

They have specialized adhesive pads (_arolia_) and tiny claws on their feet that allow them to climb smoothly up vertical glass walls.

Native to Madagascar's tropical forests, they hide inside hollow logs and leaf litter by day and emerge at night to eat fallen fruit, leaves, and decaying plant matter.

Instead of dropping an egg case (_ootheca_) like many roaches, they are ovoviviparous: females retain their egg case inside their body for about 60 days until the eggs hatch internally, giving birth to 20 to 60 live white nymphs that darken and harden within hours.

They typically live 2 to 5 years in captivity—unusually long for an insect.
