The common marmoset (*Callithrix jacchus*) is a tiny, diurnal New World monkey native to the tropical forests of northeastern [[Brazil]]. Easily recognized by its prominent white ear tufts, long banded tail, and squirrel-like agility, it is famous for its specialized tree-gouging diet and cooperative family life.

[[Primates]]

Adults measure only 14 to 18 cm (5.5 to 7 inches) in body length—excluding a tail up to 30 cm (12 inches) long—and weigh roughly 230 to 250 grams (8 to 9 ounces), about the size of a squirrel.

They have mottled grey, brown, and yellow fur, a white patch on the forehead, and fluffy white fan-shaped ear tufts. Their long, non-prehensile tail is marked with alternating dark and pale rings and serves for balance when leaping between branches.

Unlike most primates that have flat nails, marmosets possess sharp, claw-like nails on all digits except their big toes, allowing them to cling vertically to wide tree trunks like woodpeckers or squirrels.

Common marmosets are exudativores. They possess specialized, chisel-like lower incisor teeth designed to gnaw holes into the bark of trees and vines to feed on plant exudates such as tree gum, sap, and latex.

Because tree sap is hard to digest, they have an enlarged cecum in their digestive tract to ferment the gum. They supplement their diet with insects, spiders, small lizards, bird eggs, fruits, and nectar.

They live in stable, territorial groups of 3 to 15 individuals, usually centered around a dominant breeding pair and their offspring.

Common marmosets almost always give birth to non-identical twins twice a year, which together weigh up to 20–25% of the mother's body weight. To help the mother recover, the father and older siblings carry, groom, and care for the infants almost nonstop, handing them back to the mother only to nurse.

They communicate using a rich repertoire of high-pitched chirps, trills, and whistles ("phee" calls) to stay in contact through dense foliage and warn of predators.

Native to the [[Atlantic Forest]] and [[Caatinga]] dry forests of northeastern [[Brazil]], they adapt remarkably well to disturbed habitats, secondary forests, and even urban parks. Due to pet releases, introduced populations have also established themselves in parts of southeastern Brazil.
