Thylacosmilus (meaning "pouch knife") is an extinct genus of saber-toothed metatherian mammals that lived in [[South America]] from the Late [[Miocene]] to the Late [[Pliocene]] (roughly 9 to 3 million years ago). 

Although it looked remarkably like a saber-toothed cat such as Smilodon, it was not a feline at all—it belonged to Sparassodonta, an extinct order of carnivorous marsupial relatives, making it one of the most striking examples of convergent evolution in mammalian history.

Measuring roughly 1.2 to 1.5 meters (4 to 5 feet) long and weighing 80 to 120 kg (175 to 265 lbs)—similar in size to a modern jaguar—_Thylacosmilus atrox_ had an even more specialized skull than placental saber-toothed cats:

- **Rootless, ever-growing canines**. Its massive, triangular upper canines never stopped growing throughout its life, with roots extending far back over the top of its skull above its eye sockets.

- **Protective chin flanges**. Its lower jaw extended downward into two long, bony sheaths (flanges) that shielded the fragile saber teeth from breaking when the mouth was closed.

- **No incisors and wide gape**. Unlike cats, it completely lacked upper and lower incisors, and its jaw could hinge open to nearly 90 degrees to clear the tips of its canines.

Biomechanical studies of _Thylacosmilus_'s skull have sparked intense debate among paleontologists regarding how it fed:

Its jaw-closing muscles were relatively weak—producing a bite force far lower than a leopard's—but it possessed massive neck muscles designed to drive its head downward and backward.

Traditionally viewed as an ambush predator that pinned prey with its muscular forelimbs before delivering a precision slash to the throat, a 2020 biomechanical study noted that its teeth lacked bone-crushing strength and showed microscopic wear consistent with soft tissue, suggesting it may have specialized in slicing open carcasses to feed on internal organs.

Fossils of *Thylacosmilus* are known primarily from northern [[Argentina]]. 

For decades, scientists assumed it was driven to extinction by competition with North American saber-toothed cats (Smilodon) during the Great American Biotic Interchange. 

However, updated fossil dating shows that Thylacosmilus went extinct around 3 million years ago—more than a million years before large placental predators arrived in South America—pointing instead to regional climate cooling and the loss of its grassland prey.
