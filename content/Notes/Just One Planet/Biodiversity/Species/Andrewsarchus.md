Andrewsarchus (*Andrewsarchus mongoliensis*) is an extinct genus of massive, hoofed mammal that lived during the Middle to Late [[Eocene]] epoch (roughly 48 to 37 million years ago) in what is now [[Mongolia]] and northern [[China]].

*Andrewsarchus* is known almost entirely from a single, massive skull (without the lower jaw) discovered in June 1923 in the Gobi Desert of Inner Mongolia by paleontologist Kan Chuen Pao, a member of the American Museum of Natural History expedition led by explorer Roy Chapman Andrews.

*Paleontologist Henry Fairfield Osborn formally described the specimen in 1924, naming it Andrewsarchus ("Andrews' ruler") in honor of the expedition leader. Only a handful of isolated teeth and jaw fragments have been attributed to the genus since.*

The holotype skull measures 83.4 cm (2.74 feet) long and 56 cm (1.84 feet) wide—roughly twice the length of a modern brown bear's skull—featuring a greatly elongated snout, large curved canines, and heavy, blunt premolars and molars.

Because no postcranial skeleton (bones below the skull) has ever been found, its exact body shape remains speculative. Early reconstructions based on wolf-like carnivores estimated a length of up to 3.8 meters (12.5 feet), a shoulder height of nearly 1.9 meters (6 feet), and a weight around 800 to 1,000 kg (1,750 to 2,200 pounds)—leading to its long-standing reputation as the largest terrestrial mammalian carnivore ever to live.

Although it was originally classified as a mesonychid (an extinct group of wolf-like hoofed predators), modern phylogenetic analyses place _Andrewsarchus_ within the order Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates) and the clade Cetancodontamorpha. This means its closest relatives are extinct pig-like entelodonts ("hell pigs"), modern hippopotamuses, and cetaceans (whales and dolphins).

While popular media often portrays it as an active apex predator, studies of its dentition and jaw mechanics suggest its teeth were better suited for crushing bones and tough shells than slicing flesh. Today, paleontologists largely view it as a heavily built, hoofed opportunistic omnivore or scavenger—similar in lifestyle to a giant hyena or wild boar—roaming coastal plains and floodplains.
