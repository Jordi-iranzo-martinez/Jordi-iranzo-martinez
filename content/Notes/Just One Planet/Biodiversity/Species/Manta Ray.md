The manya rays (genus *Manta*) are the world's largest rays and among the most intelligent, graceful creatures in the ocean. Closely related to sharks, these gentle filter-feeding giants glide effortlessly through tropical and subtropical waters using massive, wing-like pectoral fins.

Scientists recognize two distinct species—the giant oceanic manta ray (_Mobula birostris_) and the smaller reef manta ray (_Mobula alfredi_)—with ongoing genetic research also pointing toward a potential third Atlantic species:

The giant oceanic manta ray routinely reaches a wingspan of 4.5 to 7 meters (15 to 23 feet)—and occasionally up to 9 meters (30 feet)—weighing up to 2 metric tons (4,400 lbs). The reef manta ray typically spans 3 to 4.5 meters (10 to 15 feet).

Projecting forward from each side of their wide, front-facing mouth are two rolled, horn-like cephalic lobes. When feeding, the manta unrolls these lobes into a funnel to channel plankton-rich water straight into its mouth.

Manta rays have dark black or blue backs with distinctive white shoulder markings, and white undersides patterned with dark spots and blotches. Every individual's ventral spot pattern is completely unique—like a human fingerprint—allowing researchers to track individual rays for decades.

Unlike stingrays, manta rays do not have a venomous barb on their whip-like tail and pose no danger to divers or swimmers.

Manta rays eat vast quantities of microscopic zooplankton, fish larvae, and tiny crustaceans. As they swim forward with their mouths wide open, specialized comb-like structures called gill rakers strain plankton out of the water before it exits through their five pairs of gill slits. They often perform graceful underwater barrel rolls or chain-feed in large groups to maximize their catch.

Manta rays mature late (around 8 to 15 years old) and give birth to a single, fully formed pup (rolled up like a burrito at birth) every 2 to 5 years after a 12-month gestation period.

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/OHlXGcawrHo?si=DyZvODhwZTf22tzL" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>


Manta rays possess the largest brain-to-body weight ratio of any fish (including sharks and rays), with a well-developed neocortex-like region and a specialized network of blood vessels (rete mirabile) that keeps their brain warm in deep, cold water.

In mirror self-recognition experiments, captive manta rays exhibited unusual, repetitive self-directed movements—suggesting potential cognitive self-awareness rarely seen outside mammals and birds. In the wild, they regularly visit coral reef "cleaning stations," hovering patiently while small cleaner fish (like wrasses and angelfish) nibble parasites and dead skin off their bodies.
