Tiktaalik (Tiktaalik roseae) is an extinct genus of lobe-finned fish from the Late [[Devonian]] period, living roughly 375 million years ago. 

Discovered in the Canadian [[Arctic]] in 2004, it is one of the most famous transitional fossils in evolutionary biology, bridging the gap between aquatic fish and the earliest four-legged land vertebrates (tetrapods).

*[[Canada]]*

*Its name comes from the Inuktitut word meaning "large freshwater fish," chosen in consultation with [[Inuit]] elders in Nunavut, Canada.*

_Tiktaalik_ measured 1.25 to 2.75 meters (4 to 9 feet) in length and combined classic fish traits with features previously seen only in land animals:

It possessed gills, bony scales, and fin rays, proving it lived primarily in the water and swam like a fish.

Inside its front fins, _Tiktaalik_ had robust shoulder, upper-arm, forearm, and primitive wrist bones, allowing it to prop itself up like doing push-ups on shallow riverbeds or muddy shorelines.

Unlike true fish, whose heads are fused to their shoulder girdles, _Tiktaalik_ lacked bony gill covers (opercula) and possessed a flexible neck, enabling it to turn its head independently to snap at prey without moving its entire body.

Its flat head featured eyes positioned on top (ideal for peering above shallow water), a wide mouth filled with sharp teeth, and spiracles on top of its head connected to primitive lungs, allowing it to gulp air in warm, oxygen-poor swamps.

It had sturdy, overlapping ribs to support its body against gravity outside water, along with a surprisingly large, robust pelvis and rear fins that helped propel it through shallow mudflats.

Although its fossils were unearthed on icy Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic, 375 million years ago that landmass sat near the equator as a warm, subtropical river delta system.

Tiktaalik_ thrived in shallow streams, swamps, and mudflats. Using its sturdy fins to navigate tangled aquatic vegetation and hoist its head above the waterline, it ambushed fish and early terrestrial arthropods along the water's edge.

*Tiktaalik* demonstrates how adaptations for life in shallow, oxygen-depleted streams—such as sturdy fins for pushing through mud and lungs for breathing air—gradually paved the way for vertebrates to colonize land.
