The Indian cobra (*Naja naja*), also known as the spectacled cobra or binocellate cobra, is a highly venomous elapid snake native to the Indian subcontinent.

Famous for its expandable neck hood and prominent spectacles-shaped markings, it is one of [[India]]'s "Big Four" snake species responsible for the majority of medically significant snakebites in the region.

[[Reptiles]]

When threatened, the Indian cobra raises the front third of its body and spreads its elongated neck ribs to form a wide hood. The back of the hood typically features a distinctive pattern resembling a pair of eyeglasses (two circular eye-spots connected by a curved line).

Adults typically measure between 1 and 1.5 meters (3.3 to 5 feet) in length, though rare individuals can reach up to 2 meters (6.5 feet). Their skin coloration varies widely by region, ranging from tan, yellow, and brown to dark gray or black.

Its venom contains powerful postsynaptic neurotoxins and cardiotoxins that attack the nervous system, causing muscle paralysis and respiratory or cardiac failure if left untreated.

Despite its lethal reputation, the Indian cobra is not naturally aggressive and prefers to escape. It spreads its hood and hisses loudly as a warning display, striking only when cornered, stepped on, or provoked.

Native to [[India]], Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and Nepal, it thrives in forests, plains, agricultural fields, and even densely populated urban areas where rodents are abundant. It feeds mainly on rats, mice, frogs, toads, birds, and smaller snakes.

Revered in Hindu mythology as _Naga_ (often depicted alongside Lord Shiva and Lord Vishnu), the Indian cobra is legally protected in India under the Wildlife Protection Act of 1972, which bans the traditional practice of snake charming.
