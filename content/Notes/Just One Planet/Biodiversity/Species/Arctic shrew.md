The Arctic shrew (*Sorex arcticus*) is a small, insectivorous mammal native to the northern boreal forests and wetlands of [[North America]]. It is famous for its striking tricolor coat, high-speed metabolism, and restless activity throughout harsh northern winters.

*[[Mammals]], [[Shrew]]*

The Arctic shrew displays three sharply defined color bands. It features a dark brown or black back ("saddle"), lighter brown sides, and a greyish-brown belly, with a bicolored tail that is dark on top and pale underneath.

Arctic shrews molt twice a year, and their tricolor pattern becomes sharpest and most vibrant during the winter months when their fur grows thicker and longer.

Adults measure 10 to 12 cm (4 to 4.7 inches) in total length—including a tail of about 4 cm (1.6 inches)—and weigh roughly 5 to 13 grams (0.18 to 0.46 oz). Like other red-toothed shrews, the tips of their teeth are stained dark reddish-brown with iron deposits that strengthen the enamel against wear.

Because tiny mammals lose body heat rapidly in cold climates, Arctic shrews have an extreme metabolic rate and heart rate. They remain active day and night all year long—alternating short bursts of frantic foraging with brief naps—and must eat roughly their own body weight in food every day to survive.

They hunt beneath leaf litter and snow tunnels using touch and smell, feeding primarily on insects, larvae (especially larch sawflies), earthworms, spiders, and centipedes.

Arctic shrews are fiercely solitary and territorial outside of the breeding season. In the wild, they have an exceptionally fast life cycle, rarely living longer than 12 to 18 months.
