The dugong (*Dugong dugon*) is a medium-sized, strictly herbivorous marine mammal found in warm coastal waters across the Indo-West [[Pacific Ocean]]. 

Often called a "sea cow," it is the only living member of the family Dugongidae and one of just four surviving species in the order Sirenia (alongside three species of manatees).

Though closely related to manatees—and distantly related to elephants—dugongs have distinct anatomical traits adapted for bottom-feeding in saltwater:

- **Tail fluke**. Unlike manatees, which have a flat, paddle-shaped tail, dugongs possess a notched, dolphin-like tail fluke.

- **Downturned snout**. Their large, trunk-like upper lip angles sharply downward and is covered in sensitive bristles, functioning like an underwater vacuum to uproot plants from the seafloor.

- **Size and lifespan**. Adults typically measure around 3 meters (10 feet) in length, weigh 250 to 500 kg (550 to 1,100 lbs) (with exceptional individuals reaching heavier weights), and can live up to 70 years in the wild. Adult males also grow a pair of short, ivory-like tusks.

- **Marine lifestyle**. While manatees frequently move between freshwater rivers and coastal estuaries, dugongs spend their entire lives in saltwater.

Dugongs inhabit shallow, sheltered bays, mangrove channels, and coastal waters spanning roughly 40 countries from East [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa]] and the [[Red Sea]] to northern [[Australia]] and the western Pacific.

Dugongs graze almost exclusively on underwater meadows of seagrass (preferring fast-growing, nutrient-rich genera like _Halophila_ and _Halodule_), consuming 30 to 40 kg of vegetation daily.

*[[Seagrass Ecosystem]]*

As they feed, they leave distinctive meandering feeding trails in the seabed that stimulate new seagrass growth, recycle nutrients, and help maintain healthy coastal ecosystems that sequester "blue carbon".

Dugongs spend up to 10 hours a day grazing on tough, fibrous seagrass. Because seagrass is difficult to fully digest, dugongs expel nutrient-rich waste—providing remoras with a steady, easy-to-eat buffet without having to hunt.

Using a modified dorsal fin on top of their heads that acts like a suction cup, remoras hitch a ride on the dugong's body while it rests after feeding.
