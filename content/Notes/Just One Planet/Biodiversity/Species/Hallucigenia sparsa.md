*Hallucigenia sparsa* is an extinct, worm-like marine animal that lived during the Middle [[Cambrian]] period, roughly 508 million years ago. Discovered in [[Canada]]'s famous [[Burgess Shale]], it earned its name from its bizarre, dreamlike anatomy, which initially baffled paleontologists so thoroughly that it was reconstructed upside down and backward.

It was tiny, measuring only 1 to 5.5 centimeters (0.4 to 2.2 inches) in length.

Its slender body was lined with seven pairs of tall, rigid, conical spines along its back for defense against predators. Underneath, it walked on seven pairs of slender, flexible legs ending in tiny claws, plus three pairs of softer, neck-like tentacles near the front.

For decades, scientists mistook a dark stain (actually decayed gut fluid squeezed out during fossilization) for a bulbous head. High-resolution electron microscopy in 2015 revealed its true head at the opposite end, complete with a pair of simple eyes and a ring of needle-like teeth around its mouth—giving it a microscopic "smile."

Hallucigenia belongs to an extinct group of armored worm-like animals called lobopodians.

Anatomical studies of its nested, cone-in-cone claws place it as an early stem-group ancestor of modern velvet worms ([[Onychophora]]), helping scientists trace the evolutionary origins of arthropods, tardigrades, and velvet worms during the Cambrian explosion.
