The mandrill (*Mandrillus sphinx*) is the world's largest monkey and one of the most colorful mammals on Earth. Native to the tropical rainforests of west-central [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Wonderful places/Africa|Africa]] it is an Old World monkey closely related to baboons and drills (not an ape, as it possesses a short tail). Adult males are famous for their vivid red-and-blue grooved snouts, golden beards, and brightly colored hindquarters.

*[[Primates]]*

Mandrills exhibit extreme size differences between sexes. Adult males weigh 19 to 37 kg (42–82 lbs) (occasionally reaching 50 kg) and feature long, dagger-like canine teeth up to 5 centimeters (2 in) long, whereas females weigh roughly 10 to 15 kg (22–33 lbs) and have much more muted facial colors.

Adult males display a bright crimson stripe down the bridge of the nose flanked by ribbed, cobalt-blue cheeks, complemented by a yellow-orange beard and violet-blue skin on their rumps. These colors are structural and vascular rather than pigment-based—intensifying with testosterone levels and social rank, acting as a visual billboard of dominance that reduces the need for dangerous physical fights in dense forests.

Like other cercopithecine monkeys, mandrills have expandable cheek pouches used to temporarily store food while foraging on the forest floor.

Wild mandrills live exclusively in equatorial rainforests and gallery forests across southern Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, and the Republic of the Congo, bounded by the Sanaga River to the north and the Ogooué and Ivindo rivers to the east.

Primarily terrestrial foragers that sleep in trees at night, mandrills eat fruits, seeds, roots, fungi, insects, snails, frogs, and occasionally small vertebrates.

Mandrills form the largest cohesive social groups of any non-human primate. While smaller groups consist of a dominant male, several females, and their offspring, multiple groups routinely merge into giant hordes of 600 to over 800 individuals traveling together through the forest.

Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, mandrills face severe population declines driven by commercial bushmeat hunting (where large hordes are targeted with shotguns and dogs) and deforestation from logging and agriculture.
