
The portuguese man-o'-war (genus *Physalia*) is a venomous marine siphonophore—a colonial organism closely related to jellyfish, but not a true jellyfish.

Named after 18th-century Portuguese armed sailing ships (man-of-war), it drifts across the open ocean surface using a translucent, gas-filled bladder that acts like a sail.

Rather than being a single multicellular animal, a Portuguese man-o'-war is a floating colony of genetically identical, interdependent individuals called zooids (polyps and medusoids) that cannot survive independently: 

- **Pneumatophore** (The Float/Sail). A translucent, bluish-purple or pink bladder up to 30 centimeters (12 in) long filled with carbon monoxide and air. It has a crested ridge that catches the wind and can deflate briefly to submerge when threatened at the surface. Notably, colonies develop with either a "left-handed" or "right-handed" tilt so winds disperse populations in different directions rather than stranding all of them on the same shore.

- **Dactylozooids** (Stinging Tentacles). Long, trailing tentacles that average 10 meters (30 ft) in length and can extend up to 30–50 meters (100–165 ft). Packed with microscopic harpoon-like capsules (nematocysts), they paralyze and reel in small fish and crustaceans. 

- **Gastrozooids** (Digestive Polyps). Specialized feeding polyps that attach to captured prey and secrete enzymes to liquefy and absorb nutrients for the entire colony.

- **Gonozooids** (Reproductive Polyps). Branching structures responsible for producing eggs and sperm to create new colonies.

For over 250 years, the Portuguese man-o'-war was treated as a single global species (_Physalia physalis_). However, a [2025 whole-genome and citizen-science study](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESrwEBxCJF19EcmJ-kezSYATfWrY2yx2zTVFlSf3mxJitRqWhugKHpKe5L9S9txwpm2LrqE0mO4okT-o1sZduRmNcVcw8R7rKUOATKSlEFInx5NKxvaqio1ExITzmj4fnqjhHjdGc-f8zR-7F6I0OZxRzLPZUqShZFJo6uGigfES2qSJ8Eaw7giChi62IDxv-ZQFrcjw0b7qz9s7EKweH4H4qfH0vOcnuIqFDMXaKLukJw) demonstrated that the genus comprises at least four distinct, non-interbreeding species: 

- *Physalia physalis* ([[Atlantic Ocean]])
- *Physalia utriculus* (Indo-[[Pacific Ocean]] and southwest Atlantic)
- *Physalia megalista* (Southern Hemisphere oceans)
- *Physalia minuta* (waters near [[New Zealand]] and [[Australia]]).

Contact with its tentacles delivers a neurotoxic venom that causes excruciating, whip-like pain, red welts, and occasionally systemic symptoms such as nausea, fever, or respiratory distress. Beached specimens remain capable of stinging for days or weeks after washing ashore, even when dried out.

Several marine animals are immune to its venom and prey on it directly—most famously the loggerhead sea turtle (_Caretta caretta_), the ocean sunfish (_Mola mola_), the violet sea snail (_Janthina janthina_), and the blue dragon sea slug (_Glaucus atlanticus_), which steals the man-o'-war's stinging cells to use for its own defense. Meanwhile, the small man-o'-war fish (_Nomeus gronovii_) lives safely among its tentacles for shelter.
