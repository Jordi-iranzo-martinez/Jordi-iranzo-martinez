Yeast is a single-celled eukaryotic microorganism classified as a member of the fungus kingdom, essential to nature, food production, and biotechnology. 

The most well-known and widely used species is *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, which humans have relied on for thousands of years to ferment bread, beer, and wine.

Yeast cells feed on simple sugars (such as glucose and fructose) found in flour, fruits, and grains.

In the absence of oxygen, yeast converts these sugars into carbon dioxide (CO₂) and ethanol (alcohol) by alcoholic fermentation.

In baking, trapped CO₂ gas expands the dough's gluten network to make bread rise and become airy, while the alcohol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and winemaking, the goal is to retain the ethanol and, in sparkling varieties, the natural carbonation.

Common Culinary Types

- **Fresh Yeast** (Cake Yeast). Sold in moist, perishable blocks that require refrigeration. It has a short shelf life and is typically crumbled and dissolved in lukewarm liquid before mixing.

- **Active Dry Yeast**. Dehydrated into coarse granules, this type goes dormant until rehydrated in warm water (often with a pinch of sugar) before being added to dough.

- **Instant Yeast**. Milled into finer granules that dissolve quickly, allowing it to be mixed directly into dry ingredients without prior proofing in water.

- **Nutritional Yeast**. A deactivated yeast sold as yellow flakes or powder. It cannot leaven dough, but is prized as a dairy-free seasoning for its cheesy, nutty flavor and high vitamin B and protein content.

Baking powder and baking soda are not yeast; they are chemical compounds that react with moisture and heat to produce gas immediately rather than through biological fermentation.

Because _Saccharomyces cerevisiae_ shares many cellular processes with human cells and grows rapidly, it serves as a vital model organism in genetics and is engineered to produce insulin, vaccines, and biofuels.

While most yeasts are harmless or beneficial, certain species like _Candida albicans_ live naturally on the human body and can cause opportunistic fungal infections (candidiasis) if they overgrow.
