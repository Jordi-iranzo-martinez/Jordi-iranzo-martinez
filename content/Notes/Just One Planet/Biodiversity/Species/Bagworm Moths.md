Bagworm moths (family Psychidae) are a widespread group of lepidopterans whose caterpillars construct intricate, portable protective cases out of silk and natural debris. 

*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Biodiversity/Taxonomy/Zoology/Insects|Insects]]*

Named after these distinctive "bags," the family contains roughly 1,350 known species found worldwide.

Immediately upon hatching, a bagworm caterpillar spins a silk tube around itself and reinforces the outside with local materials—such as twigs, pine needles, bits of leaves, bark, lichen, sand, or tiny pebbles.

The case acts as both camouflage and armor against birds and predatory insects. As the caterpillar grows, it enlarges the bag from the front, sticking only its head and front legs out to crawl and feed on foliage.

Different species construct remarkably distinct shapes. Some build messy, cone-shaped bundles of twigs, while certain tropical species stack cut twigs at right angles to form miniature log cabins or spiral towers.

Bagworm moths display one of the most dramatic differences between males and females in the insect world:

- **Adult Males**. Males undergo complete metamorphosis into small, fuzzy, winged moths with clear or brownish scales and feathery antennae. Because they lack functional mouthparts, they live only a few days solely to locate a mate.

- **Adult Females**. In most species, adult females are larviform—they never develop wings, functional legs, or eyes, remaining grub-like inside their protective silk bags for their entire lives.

A wingless female releases pheromones from inside her case to attract a flying male, who mates with her through an opening at the bottom of the bag. Afterward, the female lays hundreds of eggs inside her own pupal shell within the bag and dies, sealing the case so the eggs can safely overwinter.

Most bagworms feed on a wide variety of trees and shrubs, particularly evergreens like juniper, arborvitae, cedar, and pine, as well as deciduous trees like oak and maple.

While small numbers cause little harm in natural forests, heavy infestations in ornamental gardens or orchards can quickly strip evergreen trees of their foliage, sometimes killing the plant if left unchecked.
