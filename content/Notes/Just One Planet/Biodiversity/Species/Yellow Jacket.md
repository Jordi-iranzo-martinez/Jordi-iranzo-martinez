A yellow jacket (or yellowjacket) is a predatory social wasp belonging to the genera Vespula or Dolichovespula, named for its bright yellow-and-black (or white-and-black) striped abdomen.

Known outside [[North America]] simply as common wasps, they are famous for defending their paper nests fiercely and scavenging around outdoor picnics in late summer.

Unlike fuzzy, golden-brown honeybees, yellow jackets have a sleek, shiny exoskeleton with crisp yellow and black bands, a pinched "wasp waist," and wings that fold lengthwise down their back when resting. 

Because their stingers lack the barbed hooks of a honeybee's stinger, female workers and queens can sting repeatedly without dying.

They often hover and dart quickly from side to side right before landing.]

Workers scrape wood fibers from dead trees or fences and mix them with saliva to build multi-tiered hexagonal paper combs enclosed inside a protective outer shell.

Species in the genus *Vespula* (like the Eastern and German yellow jackets) typically build concealed nests underground in abandoned rodent burrows or inside wall cavities, while *Dolichovespula* species build exposed, football-shaped aerial nests in trees or under roof eaves.

A single mated queen starts a nest each spring. By late summer, colonies peak at 1,000 to 5,000+ workers before dying off in autumn frosts, leaving only newly mated queens to hibernate under bark or soil until the next spring.

Adults hunt caterpillars, flies, spiders, and other soft-bodied insects, chewing them up to feed protein-hungry larvae inside the nest.

Larvae secrete a sugary liquid that feeds the adult workers. When the queen stops laying eggs in late summer and that sugar supply dries up, thousands of hungry workers aggressively swarm sugary drinks, ripe fruit, and barbecue meat.
