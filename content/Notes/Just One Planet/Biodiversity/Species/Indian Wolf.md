The Indian wolf (*Canis lupus pallipes*, also frequently proposed as a distinct species, Canis indica) is an ancient, slender wolf lineage native to the semi-arid grasslands, scrublands, and open plains of the Indian subcontinent and Southwest [[Asia]]. 

Adapted to hot, dry landscapes rather than snowy forests, it represents one of the oldest surviving wolf lineages in the world.

 Smaller and lighter than northern grey wolves, adults stand 57 to 72 cm (22 to 28 inches) at the shoulder and typically weigh between 17 and 25 kg (37 to 55 lbs).

Unlike northern wolves, it lacks a thick, insulating winter undercoat. Its short, thin fur is typically greyish-red to reddish-white with black-tipped hairs along the back and a dark V-shaped patch across the shoulders.

It features relatively long legs built for endurance travel across open plains and proportionally large, pointed ears that help dissipate body heat.

Rather than living in dense jungles, the Indian wolf is the apex predator of open dry scrublands, savannahs, and semi-arid plains across peninsular India, Pakistan, and parts of Southwest Asia.

They live in smaller family packs than northern wolves—usually 6 to 8 individuals—and are famously quiet, rarely howling compared to other wolf populations.

Hunting primarily at dusk and night, Indian wolves prey on blackbuck antelopes, chinkara gazelles, hares, and rodents. Packs often use clever cooperative tactics, with some wolves acting as decoys to lure fast antelopes toward others hidden in ambush. Where wild prey has been depleted, they also rely heavily on domestic livestock like sheep and goats.

 Genetic studies indicate that the Indian plains wolf diverged from other wolf lineages over 110,000 years ago, making it phylogenetically basal to modern grey wolves. In late 2025, the IUCN Red List separately evaluated it as a potential distinct species (Canis indica) and classified it as Vulnerable.
