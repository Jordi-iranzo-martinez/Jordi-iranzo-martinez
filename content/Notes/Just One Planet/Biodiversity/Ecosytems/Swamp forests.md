
Swamp forests (or forested wetlands) are wetland ecosystems dominated by woody vegetation—such as trees and shrubs—where the soil is permanently or seasonally saturated with standing or slow-moving water.

[[Wetlands]]

Unlike marshes (dominated by grasses and reeds) or bogs (acidic, rain-fed peatlands), swamp forests are defined by woody trees growing in mineral-rich or organic waterlogged soils.

Trees in swamp forests have evolved unique structures to survive low-oxygen (anaerobic) soil conditions, such as buttress roots, prop roots, and pneumatophores ("knees") that protrude above the waterline to facilitate gas exchange.

They act as critical nurseries and refuges for specialized amphibians, reptiles, migratory birds, fish, and mammals (such as orangutans in Southeast Asian peat swamps).

- **Freshwater** Swamp Forests. Found inland along rivers, floodplains, and lake margins (e.g., the Bald Cypress swamps of the southeastern United States or flooded várzea forests in the Amazon).

- **Peat** Swamp Forests. Waterlogged tropical forests where dead plant matter cannot fully decay due to lack of oxygen, accumulating over millennia into deep layers of carbon-rich peat (prominent in Borneo and Sumatra).

- **Mangrove** Swamps. Salt-tolerant coastal swamp forests located in tropical and subtropical intertidal zones, forming dense root networks that buffer coastlines against storm surges.
