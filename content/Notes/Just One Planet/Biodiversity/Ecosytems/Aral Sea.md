
The Aral Sea was once the fourth-largest lake in the world, located in Central [[Asia]] between Kazakhstan to the north and Uzbekistan to the south. Beginning in the 1960s, it suffered one of the worst human-caused environmental disasters in history, shrinking to less than 10% of its original size after Soviet irrigation projects diverted the rivers that fed it.

In the 1960s, the [[URSS]] launched massive irrigation projects to transform the arid steppes of Central Asia into major producers of cotton ("white gold") and rice.

Engineers diverted the two main rivers feeding the sea—the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya—into poorly built, unlined canals where up to 75% of the water evaporated or soaked into the desert sand before reaching crops.

Deprived of freshwater inflow, the Aral Sea began receding rapidly, splitting into separate smaller lakes by the late 1980s and leaving behind the Aralkum Desert, a vast salt flat littered with rusted fishing vessels.

Rising salinity killed off native fish species, destroying a thriving commercial fishery that once employed tens of thousands of people in port cities like Moynaq (Uzbekistan) and Aralsk (Kazakhstan).

Windstorms regularly pick up exposed seabed dust laced with salt, agricultural pesticides, and chemical fertilizers, carrying toxic plumes hundreds of kilometers and causing high rates of respiratory illnesses, kidney disease, and cancer among local populations.

Without the moderating effect of a massive body of water, the regional climate became more extreme, with hotter summers, colder winters, and shorter growing seasons.

With funding from the World Bank, Kazakhstan built the Kok-Aral Dam (2005) to separate the smaller North Aral Sea from the doomed southern basin and retain water from the Syr Darya river.

Water levels in the North Aral Sea rose significantly, salinity dropped, native fish populations returned, and commercial fishing has partially resumed around Aralsk, whereas the much larger South Aral Sea in Uzbekistan remains mostly dry desert.
