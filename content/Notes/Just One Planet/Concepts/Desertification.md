
Desertification means the loss of soil fertility in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from various factors, including climatic variations and human activities.

Globally, this phenomenon threatens the livelihoods of over 3.2 billion people and degrades roughly 100 million hectares of healthy land every year. It does not mean the natural expansion of existing deserts, but rather the severe deterioration of vulnerable ecosystems.

Land degradation is driven by a combination of unsustainable human practices and environmental shifts:

- **Overgrazing and Intensive Agriculture:** Excessive livestock grazing removes protective vegetation, while continuous monoculture depletes essential soil nutrients.

- **Deforestation:** Clearing forests for farmland, urbanization, or timber leaves the topsoil completely exposed to wind and water erosion.

- **Poor Irrigation Practices:** Inefficient water management causes aquifer depletion and drives up soil salinization, making land toxic to crops.

- **Climate Change:** Rising global temperatures fuel more frequent, severe, and prolonged droughts, accelerating the drying out of fragile soils.

The impacts of desertification extend far beyond agricultural losses, creating severe economic and humanitarian crises:

- **Food Insecurity:** Drastic drops in crop yields significantly reduce food availability and drive up prices.
- **Biodiversity Loss:** As natural habitats deteriorate, native plant and animal species face local or total extinction.
- **Climate Migration:** The United Nations estimates that water scarcity and land degradation could displace up to **700 million people** by 2030.
- **Water Scarcity:** Without vegetation to help absorb rainfall, groundwater aquifers fail to replenish, causing wetlands and wells to dry up.

According to the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), natural soil regeneration is heavily outpaced by human destruction. Geographically, areas like the Mediterranean are highly vulnerable.
