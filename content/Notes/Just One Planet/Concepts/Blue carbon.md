---
title: The scarcity of the blue carbon on the climate change international regime
---
Blue carbon is the carbon dioxide (CO₂) captured and naturally stored by the world's ocean and coastal ecosystems. Although these coastal environments cover less than 2% of the total ocean area, they are highly efficient powerhouses, capable of sequestering up to ten times more carbon per hectare annually than mature tropical rainforests. This makes them a critical weapon in the global fight against climate change.


- **Seagrass Meadows:** Underwater flowering plants that form dense, vast meadows in shallow coastal waters. They capture carbon rapidly through photosynthesis while stabilizing the seabed with their root networks.

- **Mangroves:** Salt-tolerant trees and shrubs that grow along tropical and subtropical coastlines. Their complex, tangled aerial roots trap organic debris, locking carbon into the ground.

- **Tidal Marshes:** Coastal wetlands flooded and drained by salt water brought in by the tides. They feature dense vegetation specifically adapted to saturated soils, continuously accumulating organic material.

The secret to blue carbon's efficiency lies in _where_ the carbon is kept. While terrestrial forests store most of their carbon in living wood (which is released back into the atmosphere if the trees die or burn), coastal ecosystems store up to 95% of their carbon directly in the soil and underwater sediment.

- **Anaerobic Environment:** Coastal soils are completely saturated with water, meaning they lack oxygen. This lack of oxygen drastically slows down the decomposition of organic matter by microorganisms.

- **Millennial Retention:** Because decomposition is nearly paused in these deep, muddy sediments, trapped carbon can remain locked away safely for **thousands of years** rather than just decades.
