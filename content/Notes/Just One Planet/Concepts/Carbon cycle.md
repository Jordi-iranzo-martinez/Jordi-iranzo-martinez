
The carbon cycle is the biogeochemical process by which carbon moves continuously between Earth's atmosphere, oceans, soil, rocks, and living organisms. Because carbon is the chemical backbone of all organic molecules (such as DNA, proteins, and carbohydrates) and regulates Earth's temperature through greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide (\(\text{CO}_{2}\)) and methane (\(\text{CH}_{4}\)), this cycle makes life on Earth possible.

Operating over days to decades, the fast cycle moves carbon through living ecosystems:

- **Photosynthesis (Carbon Uptake).** Land plants, trees, algae, and marine phytoplankton absorb \(\text{CO}_{2}\) from the air or water and use sunlight to convert it into glucose and organic tissue, releasing oxygen.

- **Food Webs and Respiration.** Animals obtain carbon by eating plants or other animals. Both plants and animals break down sugars for energy through **cellular respiration**, exhaling \(\text{CO}_{2}\) back into the atmosphere.

- **Decomposition.** When organisms die, bacteria, fungi, and detritivores break down their remains, returning carbon to the soil or releasing \(\text{CO}_{2}\) and methane back into the air.

- **Ocean–Atmosphere Exchange.** Gases constantly diffuse back and forth across the ocean's surface. Cold polar waters absorb \(\text{CO}_{2}\) readily, while warm tropical waters release it back into the air.

Operating over millions of years, the slow cycle acts as Earth's long-term thermostat:

- **Weathering and Sedimentation.** Rainwater combines with atmospheric \(\text{CO}_{2}\) to form weak carbonic acid, which slowly dissolves rocks on land. Rivers wash these calcium and bicarbonate ions into the ocean, where marine organisms (like corals, mollusks, and plankton) use them to build calcium carbonate shells. When they die, their shells sink and compress over millions of years into limestone.

- **Fossil Fuel Formation.** Ancient plants and marine plankton buried under oxygen-poor sediment layers are slowly transformed by heat and pressure into coal, oil, and natural gas.

- **Volcanic Outgassing.** As tectonic plates subduct and melt deep underground, volcanoes and hydrothermal vents release trapped \(\text{CO}_{2}\) back into the atmosphere, completing the geological loop.

For millennia, natural carbon emissions and absorption remained roughly balanced. By extracting and burning fossil fuels—which took millions of years to form—in just two centuries, alongside clearing forests and draining peatlands, human activities have shifted massive amounts of geological carbon directly into the atmosphere.

Excess atmospheric \(\text{CO}_{2}\) intensifies the greenhouse effect and drives global warming, while the roughly 25–30% of human \(\text{CO}_{2}\) emissions absorbed by the oceans reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering ocean pH and threatening shell-building marine life.
