
The North Sea is a marginal sea of the [[Atlantic Ocean]] located in northwestern Europe. Covering an area of approximately 570,000 square kilometers (220,000 square miles), it is one of the world's most heavily trafficked, resource-rich, and strategically important bodies of water.

Situated on the European continental shelf, it connects to the Atlantic Ocean via the English Channel in the south and the Norwegian Sea in the north, and links to the Baltic Sea through the Skagerrak strait.

It borders Great Britain to the west, Norway and Denmark to the east and northeast, and Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France to the south.

It is a relatively shallow sea with an average depth of about 95 meters (312 feet). Its shallowest areas lie in the south (such as the Dogger Bank, at just 15 to 30 meters deep), while it drops to over 700 meters (2,300 feet) in the Norwegian Trench.

The North Sea is notorious for severe storms, high winds, and steep, choppy waves, making it historically hazardous for maritime navigation.

It receives warm water from the North Atlantic Current alongside major freshwater inflows from European rivers like the Rhine, Elbe, and Thames, creating strong tidal ranges along its coastlines.

Beneath its seabed lie substantial reserves of oil and natural gas (primarily extracted by Norway and the UK), and it has become the world's leading center for offshore wind power.

It hosts some of the busiest shipping lanes on Earth—serving major ports like Rotterdam, Antwerp, and Hamburg—and remains a historic fishing ground now facing challenges from overfishing and ocean warming.
