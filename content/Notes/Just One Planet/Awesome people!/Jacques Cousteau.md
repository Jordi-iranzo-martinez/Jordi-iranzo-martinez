
Jacques-Yves Cousteau (1910–1997) was a renowned French naval officer, oceanographer, inventor, filmmaker, and conservationist who revolutionized humanity's understanding of the underwater world.

Instantly recognizable in his signature red knit beanie, he co-invented modern scuba diving gear and brought marine life into millions of living rooms through his landmark documentaries.

Alongside French engineer Émile Gagnan, Cousteau co-developed the first successful open-circuit, self-contained underwater breathing apparatus (SCUBA), the Aqua-Lung (1943). By automatically supplying compressed air on demand at ambient water pressure, it freed divers from heavy surface-tethered suits and hoses.

To film his dives, Cousteau designed specialized waterproof camera housings and lighting systems. He also pioneered deep-sea exploration vehicles, notably the agile, two-person SP-350 Denise diving saucer (1959), and led the Conshelf experiments (1962–1965), proving humans could live and work in underwater habitats for weeks.

Later in life, he co-invented the _Turbosail_, an aerodynamic wind-propulsion cylinder designed to reduce ship fuel consumption (tested aboard his experimental vessel _Alcyone_).

Aboard the Calypso. In 1950, Cousteau converted a former British Royal Navy minesweeper—the RV Calypso—into a floating oceanographic laboratory and film studio equipped with an underwater observation chamber at its bow, diving platforms, and a helicopter pad. For over four decades, he sailed the globe conducting marine research and underwater archaeology.

His 1953 bestselling book The Silent World was adapted into a 1956 documentary film (co-directed with Louis Malle) that won both the Palme d'Or at Cannes and an Academy Award—one of three Oscars Cousteau won over his career. From 1968 to 1976, his television series The Undersea World of Jacques Cousteau introduced global audiences to sharks, whales, coral reefs, and deep-sea ecosystems.

Witnessing first-hand the rapid degradation of reefs and marine wildlife during his expeditions, Cousteau became one of the 20th century's most influential environmental advocates.

He served as director of the Oceanographic Museum of Monaco (1957–1988), founded The Cousteau Society in 1973 to protect ocean ecosystems, successfully campaigned against nuclear waste dumping in the Mediterranean, and helped advocate for the 1991 Madrid Protocol protecting Antarctica from mineral exploitation. 

His honors include the U.S. Presidential Medal of Freedom (1985) and election to the Académie Française (1989).
