
Lynn Margulis (1938–2011) was an American evolutionary biologist and microbiologist who revolutionized modern biology by establishing endosymbiotic theory, demonstrating that cooperation and symbiosis—not solely competition and random mutation—are primary drivers of evolutionary innovation.

In 1967, after her landmark paper _"On the Origin of Mitosing Cells"_ was rejected by roughly fifteen scientific journals, Margulis published the theory explaining how complex eukaryotic cells (cells with nuclei, found in animals, plants, fungi, and protists) evolved from simpler prokaryotic bacteria:

- **Ancient cellular mergers**. She proposed that key organelles inside eukaryotic cells originated as free-living bacteria that were engulfed by a larger host cell without being digested, forming a mutually beneficial relationship.

- **Origins of mitochondria and chloroplasts**. Specifically, mitochondria (the energy powerhouses of cells) evolved from aerobic, oxygen-using bacteria, while chloroplasts (the photosynthetic structures in plants and algae) evolved from photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

- **Genetic proof**. Her once-controversial hypothesis became textbook biology in the 1970s and 1980s when genetic sequencing confirmed that both mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own circular DNA, distinct from the cell's nuclear DNA, closely matching bacterial genomes.

Beyond cell evolution, Margulis reshaped broader perspectives on life on Earth:

- **The Gaia Hypothesis**. Working closely with British chemist James Lovelock, she co-developed the [Gaia hypothesis](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESawHEIkXXft8w5qym1WdIWAJYYho1c0DH-hRfQ83w3WuXXNER0tCYvuhS8qaFntqTJe6EyzdUKC-29Da2qwvy_0W9g2jDCXHnXfTWpyh7FpLTW8IEUu3dtutQrS55x5p9K3oYKiF1VcqdE8Q4), proposing that Earth's living organisms interact with their inorganic surroundings (atmosphere, oceans, and rocks) to form a self-regulating, complex system that maintains conditions suitable for life.

	*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Planet/Books/Gaia]]*

- **Five Kingdoms classification**. Alongside ecologist Robert Whittaker, she helped champion and refine the Five Kingdoms system of biological classification (Monera, Protoctista, [[Fungi]], Plantae, and Animalia), elevating the evolutionary importance of microorganisms.

	*[[Plants]], [[Animals]]*

A longtime professor at Boston University and the University of Massachusetts Amherst, Margulis authored influential books for both scientists and general readers, including Symbiosis in Cell Evolution (1981), [[Microcosmos]] (1986), and Symbiotic Planet (1998). 

She was elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences (1983) and awarded the National Medal of Science (1999) and the Darwin–Wallace Medal (2008).
