
John D. Liu (born 1953) is a Chinese American filmmaker, researcher, and ecologist internationally renowned for documenting and championing large-scale ecosystem restoration. 

He is the founder of Ecosystem Restoration Communities (formerly Ecosystem Restoration Camps) and serves as Ecosystem Ambassador for the Commonland Foundation.

Born in Nashville, Tennessee, Liu spent over a decade as a television producer and cameraman for CBS News, RAI, and ZDF, helping establish the CBS News bureau in Beijing in 1981.

The Loess Plateau revelation (1995). While filming a World Bank project on China's Loess Plateau—an area roughly the size of France that had been degraded into barren desert over millennia—he witnessed local communities and scientists transform millions of hectares back into lush, productive land. This experience shifted his career toward ecology and land rehabilitation.

Through the Environmental Education Media Project (EEMP), Liu has produced, directed, and presented several award-winning films broadcast on BBC, National Geographic, Discovery, and PBS:

- **Lessons of the Loess Plateau** and **Hope in a Changing Climate** (2009). Documentaries illustrating how large-scale soil and water restoration in China, Ethiopia, and Rwanda can reverse desertification, sequester carbon, and lift communities out of poverty.

- **Green Gold** (2012). A widely viewed film exploring how restoring degraded ecosystems globally offers a practical, scalable solution to climate change and food insecurity.

In 2016–2017, Liu launched the **Ecosystem Restoration Camps** movement (now known as [Ecosystem Restoration Communities](https://ecosystemrestorationcommunities.org/about/)), starting with its first pilot project on the Altiplano in southeastern Spain. 

The movement creates living-learning centers where volunteers, farmers, and scientists work side by side to rebuild soil organic matter, restore watersheds, and revive biodiversity.

The network has grown to more than 80 restoration projects across six continents, restoring over 103,000 hectares of degraded land, with a goal of reaching 100 projects and 3.1 million hectares by 2030.
