
Dame Jane Goodall (1934–2025) was a pioneering English primatologist, ethologist, and conservationist renowned as the world's foremost authority on wild chimpanzees.

Her groundbreaking field research in Tanzania transformed science's understanding of animal intelligence and redefined the boundary between humans and the rest of the animal kingdom.

In July 1960, at age 26 and with no formal academic degree, Goodall began studying wild chimpanzees at Gombe Stream National Park in Tanzania under the mentorship of paleoanthropologist Louis Leakey.

In 1960, she observed a chimpanzee she named David Greybeard stripping leaves off twigs to "fish" for termites. Until then, tool-making was considered a uniquely human trait, prompting Leakey to famously remark that science had to redefine "tool," redefine "Man," or accept chimpanzees as humans.

Breaking from strict scientific conventions of the time, Goodall gave the chimpanzees names rather than numbers and documented their distinct personalities, family bonds, altruism, and capacity for grief, as well as darker behaviors like territorial warfare. 

Despite lacking an undergraduate degree, she was admitted to the University of Cambridge, earning her PhD in ethology in 1965. The Gombe study continues today as the longest-running wild chimpanzee research project in the world.

In the 1980s, alarmed by rapid deforestation and the decline of chimpanzee populations across Africa, Goodall transitioned from field researcher to full-time global advocate:

Founded in 1977, the [Jane Goodall Institute](https://janegoodall.org/) leads wildlife research, manages sanctuaries like Tchimpounga in the Republic of Congo, and pioneers community-centered conservation programs (such as TACARE) that link forest protection with sustainable livelihoods for local villages. 

Launched in 1991, Roots & Shoots is a global youth-led program active in nearly 75 countries that empowers young people to lead environmental, animal welfare, and humanitarian projects in their communities.

Goodall traveled around the world for decades—spending up to 300 days a year on tour—promoting hope, individual action, and environmental stewardship until her passing on October 1, 2025, at age 91.

Her honors included being named a UN Messenger of Peace (2002), a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire (2004), and receiving the Presidential Medal of Freedom in January 2025.
