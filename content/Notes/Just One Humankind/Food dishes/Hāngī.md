
Hāngī is a traditional Māori cooking method from [[New Zealand]] (Aotearoa) in which food is slow-cooked underground in an earth oven (umu) using heated volcanic stones and trapped steam. 

Reserved primarily for special occasions, community gatherings, and pōwhiri (welcoming ceremonies), it infuses meats and root vegetables with a tender texture and a distinctive smoky, earthy flavor.

How a Traditional Hāngī Works

1. **Digging & Heating the Stones**. A pit roughly 50 to 100 cm deep is dug into the earth, and a large wood fire (traditionally using dense native woods like _mānuka_ or _kānuka_) is burned over volcanic or basalt stones for 2 to 4 hours until the rocks are glowing white-hot. (River stones with trapped moisture are avoided because they can explode under extreme heat.)

2. **Preparing the Baskets**. Once the fire dies down and ash is cleared, food is arranged in metal wire baskets—historically woven flax baskets (_kono_)—lined with cabbage, banana, or native _puka_ leaves. Meats go on the bottom closer to the heat, with vegetables on top.

3. **Sealing in the Steam**. The baskets are lowered onto the hot stones, covered with wet hessian sacks, mutton cloth, or ferns to generate steam, and buried completely beneath a mound of damp earth so no steam can escape.

4. **Slow Cooking**. The sealed pit acts like a natural low-pressure steam oven, cooking the feast undisturbed for 3 to 4 hours before the soil is carefully dug away and the baskets are lifted out. 

Classic Hāngī Foods

- **Meats & Seafood** (*Kaimoana*). Pork, lamb, chicken, and mutton are standard today, alongside traditional seafood such as fish, mussels, or eels (_tuna_).

- **Vegetables**. Kūmara (Māori sweet potato), potatoes, pumpkin, taro, carrots, and cabbage.

- **Stuffing & Bread**. Savory herb-and-onion bread stuffing wrapped in foil, often served alongside *rēwena* bread (a traditional Māori fermented potato sourdough).

In geothermally active regions like Rotorua and Whakarewarewa, Māori communities have cooked hāngī for centuries using natural volcanic steam vents and boiling mineral pools instead of wood fires.

Because preparing a pit requires hours of collective digging, tending the fire, and prepping food, a hāngī embodies *manaakitanga* (hospitality, generosity, and care for guests) and strengthens *whānau* (family) and community bonds.
