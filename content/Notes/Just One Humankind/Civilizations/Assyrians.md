Assyrians are an indigenous Semitic ethnic group native to [[Mesopotamia]] who trace their ancestry back more than three millennia to the ancient Assyrian Empire. 

Distinct from neighboring Arab, Kurdish, Persian, and Turkish populations, they maintain a continuous linguistic, cultural, and religious heritage.

Ancient Assyria centered around the city-state of Aššur (on the Tigris River in modern-day northern Iraq) and grew into one of the ancient world's most powerful empires, reaching its peak during the Neo-Assyrian period (912–612 BCE).

The empire was renowned for pioneering iron weaponry, professional standing armies, and complex administration, as well as monumental art (such as the winged _Lamassu_ statues) and the famous Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh.


Assyrians speak modern dialects of Neo-Aramaic (such as Suret and Turoyo), directly descended from the ancient Aramaic lingua franca of Mesopotamia. Classical Syriac serves as their liturgical and literary language, written in the traditional Syriac alphabet.

Assyrians were among the earliest peoples to adopt [[Christianity]], beginning in the 1st century CE. Today, most belong to Eastern Christian denominations, including the Assyrian Church of the East, the Chaldean Catholic Church, the Syriac Orthodox Church, and the Syriac Catholic Church.

Estimates place the global Assyrian population between 3 and 5 million. Their ancestral homeland spans northern Iraq (particularly the Nineveh Plains), northeastern Syria, southeastern Turkey, and northwestern Iran.

Due to centuries of religious and ethnic persecution—most notably the Sayfo genocide during World War I—and modern regional conflicts, more than half of the Assyrian population now lives in diaspora communities across North America (with major hubs like Chicago and Detroit), Europe (such as Sweden and Germany), and Australia.
