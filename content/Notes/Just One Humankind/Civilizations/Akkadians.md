
The Akkadians were an ancient Semitic people of Mesopotamia who established the world's first multinational empire under Sargon of Akkad around 2334 BCE.

Named after their capital city of Akkad (Agade), they united the independent Sumerian city-states and Semitic-speaking populations into a single centralized state that laid the administrative, cultural, and linguistic foundations for later Mesopotamian civilizations—including the Assyrians and Babylonians.

Rising from humble origins, Sargon conquered the Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia and expanded his rule from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean Sea.

The Akkadians introduced standardized weights and measures, a unified calendar, and a regular postal system using clay envelopes sealed with cylinder seals to govern distant provinces.

Under Sargon’s grandson, Naram-Sin, the empire reached its greatest territorial extent, though it collapsed around 2154 BCE due to internal rebellions, severe climate-induced droughts, and invasions by the Gutian people.

he Akkadians adapted the Sumerian cuneiform writing system to their own East Semitic language. For over a millennium after the empire fell, Akkadian served as the primary language of diplomacy, trade, and literature across the ancient Near East—branching into the later Assyrian (northern) and Babylonian (southern) dialects.

Rather than erasing Sumerian traditions, the Akkadians merged their deities and myths with Sumerian ones, preserving classic works like the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]].

Sargon's daughter, Enheduanna, served as high priestess in Ur and composed hymns to the goddess Inanna, making her the earliest known named author in human history.
