
The Inuit are a group of culturally and linguistically related Indigenous peoples inhabiting the [[Arctic]] and subarctic regions of [[Web/content/Notes/Just One Humankind/Places/Greenland]], [[Canada]] (Nunavut, Nunavik, Nunatsiavut, and the Inuvialuit Settlement Region), and [[Alaska]] (where they are known as Iñupiat).

*The word literally means "the people" in Inuktitut, while the singular form for one person is Inuk.*

*Historically referred to by European colonizers as "Eskimos," that exonym is widely considered outdated or offensive in Canada and Greenland, though in Alaska it is still sometimes used collectively for both Iñupiat and Yupik peoples (a related but distinct Arctic group).*

They descend from the ancient Thule culture, which emerged in western Alaska around 1000 CE and expanded rapidly eastward across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland, replacing the earlier Dorset culture.

They speak closely related dialects within the Inuit branch of the Eskimo-Aleut language family, including Inuktitut (Canada), Kalaallisut (Greenland), and Iñupiaq (Alaska). Many Canadian dialects use a distinct syllabic writing system alongside the Latin alphabet.

Traditionally semi-nomadic hunters and fishers, the Inuit developed specialized techniques to thrive in extreme polar environments. Their diet relied heavily on marine mammals (seals, walruses, bowhead whales, and narwhals), caribou, polar bears, and fish, with every part of harvested animals used for food, clothing, tools, and lamp oil.

They engineered iconic survival technologies, including the **kayak** (a covered, single-person hunting boat), the **umiak** (a larger open skin boat for transport and whale hunting), the **igloo** (a temporary shelter built from wind-packed snow blocks), dog sleds (_qamutiik_), slit snow goggles to prevent snow blindness, and layered caribou- or sealskin parkas.

Traditional spirituality was animistic and mediated by shamans (_angakkuit_), featuring key figures such as Sedna, the ruler of marine animals. Inuit artistic traditions are renowned worldwide for carvings in soapstone, walrus ivory, and bone, as well as Inuit throat singing (_katajjaq_), traditionally performed as a rhythmic vocal game by women.

Following forced assimilation and sedentarization policies in the 20th century, Inuit have achieved major milestones in political autonomy. Key examples include the creation of the Canadian territory of Nunavut in 1999 (which has a majority Inuit population and public government) and Greenland's 2009 Self-Government Act (_Naalakkersuisut_).

Today, Inuit blend modern lifestyles with traditional harvesting and community values while navigating pressing issues such as rapid climate change melting sea ice, resource sovereignty, mental health initiatives, and language revitalization.
