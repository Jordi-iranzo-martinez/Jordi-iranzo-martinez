Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570 – c. 495 BCE) was an ancient Ionian Greek philosopher, mathematician, and religious thinker who founded Pythagoreanism, a secretive philosophical and mystical movement that deeply influenced [[Plato]], [[Aristotle]], and Western science.

*[[Ancient Greeks]]*

The Pythagorean Theorem (a² + b² = c²), most famously associated with his name, this geometric rule states that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides. (While Babylonian and Indian mathematicians knew the relationship earlier, the Pythagorean school is traditionally credited with its first formal deductive proof.)

Pythagoras taught that reality is fundamentally mathematical and that the cosmos is governed by numerical ratios and harmony ("All Is Number.")

By experimenting with vibrating strings (the monochord), he discovered that harmonious musical intervals correspond to simple whole-number ratios (such as 2:1 for an octave, 3:2 for a fifth, and 4:3 for a fourth).

The Pythagoreans were among the first Greek thinkers to propose that the Earth is a sphere and that the Morning Star and Evening Star are the same planet (Venus).

Around 530 BCE, Pythagoras settled in Croton (in southern Italy / _Magna Graecia_), where he established a communal, semi-monastic school open to both men and women.

He taught that the human soul is immortal and undergoes a cycle of rebirth into human, animal, or plant forms until purified through philosophy and ascetic living (*Metempsychosis*).

Followers practiced communal property ownership, silence, mathematical contemplation, and a strict meat-free diet (known for centuries as the "Pythagorean diet"), along with curious taboos such as avoiding fava beans.

Because Pythagoras left no surviving writings of his own and his followers attributed all their collective discoveries to him out of reverence, historians find it difficult to separate the historical man from later legend.
