Honda Tadakatsu (1548–1610), also known as Honda Heihachirō, was a legendary Japanese samurai general and daimyō who served Tokugawa Ieyasu during the late Sengoku and early Edo periods. 

Celebrated as one of the "Tokugawa Four Heavenly Kings" (Tokugawa Shitennō), he is famed across Japanese history for fighting in over 50 major battles without ever suffering a single serious wound.

The Tonbokiri ("Dragonfly Cutter"), his personal spear, forged by the master smith Fujiwara Masazane, is counted among the Three Great Spears of Japan. Legend holds that it was so razor-sharp that a dragonfly landing on the upright blade was instantly sliced in two.

Tadakatsu wore the Kaku-tsuki Kabuto: a lightweight, black-lacquered armor topped with a helmet featuring massive lacquered deer antlers and a large Buddhist rosary (juzu) draped across his chest. Designed to make him unmistakably visible rather than hidden, it let him draw enemy attention on the battlefield.

He rode a famous black steed warhorse named Mikuniguro until the horse died during the Odawara Campaign in 1590.

Alongside Ii Naomasa, Sakakibara Yasumasa, and Sakai Tadatsugu, Tadakatsu formed Ieyasu's elite inner circle of field commanders.

At Mikatagahara, he fought a fierce rearguard action to save Ieyasu from Takeda Shingen's forces in 1573. In 1584, at Komaki-Nagakute, Tadakatsu led a tiny force of around 500 men in a daring stand against Toyotomi Hideyoshi's massive army of tens of thousands, buying crucial time for Ieyasu to escape and impressing Hideyoshi so deeply that Hideyoshi forbade his troops from harming him.

He played pivotal frontline roles in nearly every decisive battle that brought Tokugawa Ieyasu to power as shōgun: Anegawa (1570), Nagashino (1575), & Sekigahara (1600)

Japan's great warlords admired him openly—Oda Nobunaga called him a "samurai among samurai," while Takeda Shingen's retainers remarked that Ieyasu had two treasures above all others: his helmet and Honda Tadakatsu.

Following the establishment of peace, Ieyasu rewarded him with the Ōtaki Domain (100,000 koku) in 1590 and later the Kuwana Domain (150,000 koku) in 1601.

After retiring in 1609, he died in Edo on December 3, 1610, at age 62. According to popular legend, the only time he ever shed his own blood was shortly before his death when he accidentally nicked his hand while carving wood.
