The real Oregon Trail was a historic 2,170-mile (3,490 km) east-to-west wagon route across the United States that connected the Missouri River to valleys in Oregon, serving as the primary overland migration artery for American pioneers between the 1840s and 1860s.

Starting primarily in Independence, Missouri (or nearby towns along the Missouri River), the trail followed the Platte River through Nebraska, crossed the Rocky Mountains via South Pass in Wyoming, wound through Idaho along the Snake River, and ended in the fertile Willamette Valley of Oregon.

Roughly 400,000 settlers, farmers, miners, and families traveled the route between 1841 and 1869, driven by economic depressions in the East, promises of free farmland (such as the Donation Land Claim Act of 1850), and Manifest Destiny.

A typical trip took 4 to 6 months by covered wagon (usually pulled by oxen rather than horses, as oxen were stronger and could survive on prairie grass), averaging about 15 miles (24 km) per day.

Despite popular Western movies, conflicts with Native Americans were relatively rare—and tribes frequently traded food, horses, and river-ferrying assistance with travelers.

Roughly 1 in 10 pioneers died along the way, overwhelmingly from infectious diseases (cholera, dysentery, typhoid fever), accidental gunshots, drowning at river crossings, and wagon accidents.

Usage dropped sharply after 1869, when the completion of the First Transcontinental Railroad cut the cross-country journey from months down to about a week.
