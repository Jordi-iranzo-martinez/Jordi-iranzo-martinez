The 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia (codenamed Operation Danube) was a massive joint military intervention on the night of August 20–21, 1968, led by the Soviet Union alongside Poland, Hungary, and Bulgaria, to halt the democratic reforms of the Prague Spring

In January 1968, reformist Alexander Dubček became First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia, launching a program to build "socialism with a human face."

His government abolished state censorship, allowed freedom of speech and travel, rehabilitated victims of past political purges, and began decentralizing the economy.

Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev and hardline leaders in East Germany and Poland feared that Czechoslovakia's liberalization would inspire similar uprisings across the Eastern Bloc and weaken Soviet hegemony.

Roughly 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops and 2,000 tanks crossed the border overnight, eventually swelling to around 500,000 soldiers. (Romania and Albania refused to participate).

Soviet paratroopers seized Prague's Ruzyně Airport, allowing heavy transport planes to land continuously. Special forces arrested Dubček and other top reformers, flying them to Moscow.

The Czechoslovak government ordered its armed forces to remain in their barracks to prevent a catastrophic war like the 1956 Hungarian Revolution.

Ordinary citizens resisted peacefully by blocking tanks with their bodies, painting over or removing street signs to disorient invading troops, and engaging bewildered young Soviet soldiers in political debates.

Journalists and technicians kept clandestine radio and television broadcasts running for days to coordinate peaceful resistance and inform the outside world.

Despite the lack of armed military resistance, over 130 Czechoslovak civilians were killed and hundreds were wounded during clashes and shootings in the first weeks of the occupation.

Under intense pressure in Moscow, Dubček and other leaders were forced to sign the Moscow Protocol, agreeing to dismantle their reforms and accept the permanent stationing of Soviet troops.

In April 1969, Dubček was replaced by the pro-Soviet Gustáv Husák, initiating twenty years of strict authoritarian control, censorship, and political purges that lasted until the Velvet Revolution of 1989.

To justify the invasion, Moscow declared the Brezhnev Doctrine, stating that the Soviet Union had the right to intervene militarily in any socialist country where communist rule was threatened.

The invasion deeply fractured the international communist movement, drawing condemnation from Western communist parties and inspiring tragic acts of protest, most notably the self-immolation of Czech student Jan Palach in January 1969.
