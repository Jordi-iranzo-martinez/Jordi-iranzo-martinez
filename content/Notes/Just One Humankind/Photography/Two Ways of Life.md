The theatrical tableau depicts a patriarchal sage guiding two young men into adulthood, presenting them with two contrasting moral paths:

- **Virtue** (Right). Represented by figures embodying religion, charity, industry, marriage, science, and the arts.

- **Vice** (Left). Depicts scenes of gambling, drunkenness, idleness, licentiousness, and despair.

At the center stands a veiled figure symbolizing repentance, bridging the gap between the two paths.

*Inspired by Renaissance frescoes such as Raphael's [[La scuola di Atene]].*

When exhibited at the 1857 Manchester Art Treasures Exhibition, the photograph sparked intense debate. Many Victorian critics considered the depiction of semi-nude figures in a photograph indecent—since photography was viewed as a literal record of real people rather than idealized painting—and some venues even draped a curtain over the left half of the image.

Despite the backlash, Queen Victoria admired the work and purchased a copy for 10 guineas as a gift for Prince Albert, helping legitimize photography as a fine art medium.

Two Ways of Life proved that photography could transcend mechanical documentation to construct complex symbolic narratives, laying the foundation for artistic photomontage and modern photo manipulation.
