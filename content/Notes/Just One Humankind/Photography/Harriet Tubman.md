Harriet Tubman (born Araminta Ross; c. 1822 – March 10, 1913) was an American abolitionist, social activist, and Union Army scout renowned for escaping slavery and leading dozens of enslaved people to freedom via the Underground Railroad. Her courage, strategic brilliance, and lifelong advocacy made her one of the most iconic figures in the struggle for freedom and civil rights in the United States.

Born into slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland, she endured harsh physical labor from a young age. As an adolescent, she suffered a severe head injury when an overseer threw a heavy metal weight meant for another enslaved person, leaving her with lifelong headaches, narcolepsy, and vivid spiritual visions.

In 1849, fearing she would be sold away from her family, Tubman escaped alone to Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. She traveled roughly 90 miles on foot with the assistance of the abolitionist network.

Rather than remaining safely in the North, Tubman returned to Maryland roughly 13 times over a decade. She personally guided around 70 enslaved people—including family members and friends—to free states and Canada, while providing instructions that helped dozens more escape independently.

Known as "Moses" by those she helped, Tubman never lost a single passenger. She traveled during winter nights, used disguises, secret safe houses, and coded spirituals, and carried a revolver both for self-defense against slave catchers and to ensure no one turned back and endangered the group.

During the Civil War (1861–1865), Tubman worked for the Union Army in South Carolina as a cook and nurse, and later as an armed scout and spy who gathered vital intelligence from local Black populations.

In June 1863, she became the first woman to lead an armed military expedition in U.S. history. Guiding Union gunboats up the Combahee River, the operation destroyed Confederate supply lines and liberated more than **700 enslaved people**.

After the war, Tubman settled in Auburn, New York, where she cared for her aging parents and established the Harriet Tubman Home for Aged and Indigent Negroes. She also campaigned actively alongside leaders like Susan B. Anthony for women's voting rights.

She died in 1913 and was buried with full military honors. Today, her legacy is preserved through national historical parks, museums, and the U.S. Treasury's initiative to feature her portrait on the $20 bill.
