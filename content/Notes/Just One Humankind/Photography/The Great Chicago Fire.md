The Great Chicago Fire was a catastrophic conflagration that burned across central Chicago, Illinois, from October 8 to October 10, 1871. Destroying roughly 3.3 square miles (9 km²) of the rapidly growing city, it killed an estimated 300 people, leveled more than 17,400 buildings, and left over 100,000 residents—a third of Chicago's population—homeless.

In 1871, nearly two-thirds of Chicago's buildings were constructed entirely of wood, including wooden roofs, wooden sidewalks, and miles of pine-block paved streets. Months of severe drought had left the city bone-dry.

The fire began around 8:30 PM on Sunday, October 8, in or near a small barn behind the home of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary at 137 DeKoven Street. A popular newspaper story claimed Mrs. O'Leary's cow knocked over a kerosene lantern, though the reporter later admitted he invented the tale; the Chicago City Council officially exonerated Catherine O'Leary and her cow in 1997.

Exhausted firefighters were initially sent to the wrong neighborhood, giving the blaze a fatal head start. Strong southwest winds carried burning embers across the Chicago River—twice—creating superheated convective updrafts ("fire devils") that ignited stone and iron buildings previously thought fireproof until steady rain finally helped extinguish the flames early on October 10.

Among the very few structures left standing in the burned district were the limestone Chicago Water Tower and Pumping Station (which became enduring symbols of the city's resilience), the St. Ignatius College Prep building, and Mahlon Ogden's wood-frame house.

Rather than abandoning the ruins, Chicago launched a massive reconstruction boom fueled by domestic and European investment. Strict new fire codes banned wooden buildings in the city center, while soaring downtown land values inspired architects of the Chicago School (such as William Le Baron Jenney, Louis Sullivan, and Daniel Burnham) to pioneer steel-frame construction, elevators, and fireproof terracotta cladding—giving birth to the world's first modern skyscrapers.

The second of the four red stars on the official Flag of Chicago commemorates the Great Chicago Fire of 1871.
