The Iron Age is the final epoch of the traditional Three-Age System (Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages), defined by the widespread adoption of iron and steel for tools, weapons, and agricultural equipment. 

Because iron ore is far more abundant than the copper and tin required to make bronze, ironworking democratized access to durable metal tools—fueling agricultural booms, population growth, larger armies, and the rise of major classical civilizations.

Unlike a single synchronized global date, the Iron Age began at different times depending on when societies mastered high-temperature smelting and forging:

- **Anatolia & the Near East** (~1200–1000 BCE). Following the Bronze Age Collapse and the fall of the Hittite Empire, ironworking spread rapidly across the Eastern Mediterranean and Levant.

- **India** (~1200–1000 BCE). Iron smelting developed across the Indo-Gangetic Plain alongside the Painted Grey Ware culture and early Vedic kingdoms.

- **Central & Western Europe** (~800–500 BCE). Characterized by the Hallstatt and La Tène cultures (often associated with Celtic peoples), ending locally as regions were absorbed into the Roman Empire or entered recorded history.

- **Sub-Saharan Africa** (~1000–500 BCE). Independent ironworking centers emerged early in regions like West and Central Africa (such as the Nok culture in Nigeria), spreading southward via the Bantu expansion without an intervening [[Bronze Age]].

- **East Asia** (~600–500 BCE). China adopted ironworking during the Zhou dynasty and rapidly pioneered cast iron production using blast furnaces centuries before Europe.

Key Technological & Social Changes

- **Superior Tools & Farming**. Iron plows, sickles, and axes allowed farmers to clear dense forests and cultivate heavy soils, dramatically increasing food surpluses.

- **Warfare & Empires**. Mass-produced iron swords, spearheads, and armor reshaped warfare, powering the expansion of empires like the Assyrians, Achaemenid Persians, Romans, and Mauryans.

- **Coinage & Trade**. The Iron Age saw the invention of standardized metal coins (first minted in Lydia around 600 BCE), accelerating long-distance commerce.

- **Alphabetic Writing**. Phonetic alphabets (pioneered by the Phoenicians and adapted by the Greeks) made literacy far easier to learn than complex Bronze Age scripts like cuneiform or hieroglyphics, marking the transition from prehistory into recorded history in many regions.
