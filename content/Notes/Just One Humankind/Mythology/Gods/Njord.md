Njörðr (anglicized as Njord) is the Norse god of the sea, wind, seafaring, fishing, and maritime wealth. 

A prominent member of the Vanir tribe of gods, he lived in [[Asgard]] alongside the [[Æsir]] as an honored hostage following the Æsir–Vanir War and fathered two of Norse mythology's most famous deities, [[Freyr]] and [[Freyja]].

Unlike the sea giant Ægir (who embodied the wild, dangerous ocean), Njörðr governed coastal waters, favorable winds, safe voyages, and the bounty of the sea. Vikings invoked him for calm seas, successful fishing hauls, and trading prosperity—giving rise to the Old Norse expression _"as rich as Njörðr."_

His dwelling sat right on the seashore, where he loved listening to the cry of gulls and swans and the sound of waves.

After the war between the Æsir and the Vanir ended in a truce, the Vanir sent Njörðr and his children to live among the Æsir in exchange for Hœnir and Mímir, integrating him into Asgard's ruling council.

Before coming to Asgard, Njörðr had his twin children—the fertility deities Freyr and Freyja—with his own unnamed sister, a practice permitted among the Vanir though forbidden among the Æsir.

When the giantess Skaði marched to Asgard seeking compensation for the gods killing her father (Þjazi), she was allowed to choose a husband from the gods—judging only by their bare feet. Hoping to pick the handsome Baldr, she chose the cleanest, fairest feet, which turned out to belong to Njörðr (washed spotless by the sea).

Their marriage quickly failed because neither could stand the other's home: Skaði hated the screeching seagulls and crashing waves at Nóatún, while Njörðr hated the howling wolves and freezing peaks of Skaði's mountain hall, Þrymheimr. After trying to split their time nine nights in each place, they separated amicably.

According to the _Prose Edda_, Njörðr is one of the few gods who will survive Ragnarök, returning home to the realm of the Vanir (_Vanaheimr_) when the world ends.

*Scholars widely connect Njörðr to Nerthus, an ancient Germanic Earth/fertility goddess described by the Roman historian Tacitus in the 1st century CE (Germania), whose name is the direct linguistic ancestor of Njörðr.*
