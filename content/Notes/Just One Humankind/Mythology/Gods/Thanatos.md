Thanatos (known as Mors or Letum in Roman mythology) is the ancient Greek personification (daimon) of death—specifically peaceful, non-violent death.

*[[Ancient Greeks]]*

Rather than ruling the Underworld like Hades, Thanatos acted as the relentless spirit who appeared when a mortal's thread of life was cut by the Fates, severing a lock of hair to consecrate them to the dead before escorting their soul toward the Underworld.

According to Hesiod's _Theogony_, Thanatos is the son of [[Nyx]] (Night) and [[Erebus]] (Darkness), and the twin brother of [[Hypnos]] (Sleep). His sisters include the Keres, bloodthirsty spirits who embodied violent death in battle or disease.

In ancient Greek vase paintings and sculpture (such as the famous carved column drum from the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus), he is depicted either as a bearded, winged older man or a handsome, winged youth holding a sword or an inverted, extinguished torch symbolizing a life snuffed out.

When Zeus's mortal son Sarpedon was killed during the Trojan War, Zeus ordered Apollo to cleanse the body and entrusted twin brothers Thanatos and Hypnos to carry him swiftly back to his homeland of Lycia for an honorable burial. 

When Zeus sent Thanatos to drag the cunning King Sisyphus of Corinth to the Underworld, Sisyphus flattered Thanatos and asked him to demonstrate how his chains worked—locking Death in his own fetters. While Thanatos was imprisoned, no one on Earth could die, enraging Ares (because soldiers wouldn't die in battle) until Ares rescued Thanatos and handed Sisyphus over.

When Queen Alcestis volunteered to die in place of her husband, King Admetus, Heracles ambushed Thanatos beside her tomb, wrestled him into submission, and forced him to release her back to the living.

*His name is the root of words like euthanasia ("good death"), thanatology (the scientific study of death), and thanatophobia (fear of death).*

Sigmund Freud used the term Thanatos to describe the "death drive"—an innate human impulse toward self-destruction and returning to an inanimate state, opposing Eros (the life drive).
