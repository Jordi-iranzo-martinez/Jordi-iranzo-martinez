A gargoyle is a carved stone grotesque—most famous in medieval Gothic architecture—designed with a built-in spout to channel rainwater away from a building's roof and walls.

The word comes from the Old French _gargouille_ ("throat" or "gullet," related to _gargle_). Before modern gutters and downspouts, gargoyles projected out from cathedral roofs to spit rainwater clear of the stone masonry so it wouldn't erode the mortar.

Technically, a statue is only a gargoyle if it has a water spout; purely decorative stone monsters perched on ledges (like the famous pondering _Stryge_ on Notre-Dame de Paris) are called grotesques or chimeras.

Medieval builders carved them as fierce beasts, demons, or mythical creatures both to ward off evil spirits from churches and to remind illiterate townspeople of the dangers of sin outside the sanctuary. Legend also ties them to La Gargouille, a fire-breathing river dragon in 7th-century France defeated by Saint Romanus whose fireproof head was mounted on the cathedral of Rouen.
