The Talmud (Hebrew for "instruction" or "learning") is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and theology. Compiled between the 3rd and 6th centuries CE, it is a vast, multi-volume record of centuries of rabbinic debates, legal rulings, ethics, philosophy, customs, and folklore passed down as the Oral Torah.

Every section of the Talmud is built around two interconnected layers:

- **The Mishnah** (c. 200 CE). Written primarily in Mishnaic Hebrew, this is the first major written redaction of Jewish oral law, compiled by Rabbi Judah the Prince (*Yehudah HaNasi*). It presents concise legal statements and differing early rabbinic opinions.

- **The Gemara** (c. 200–500 CE). Written mostly in Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, this is an expansive commentary and analysis of the Mishnah. Rather than acting as a simple rulebook, the Gemara preserves the back-and-forth dialectical arguments, questions, and minority opinions of later sages (*Amoraim*). 

Two distinct versions of the Talmud were produced by the major centers of Jewish scholarship in antiquity:

- **The Babylonian Talmud** (*Talmud Bavli*). Compiled in Babylonia (modern-day Iraq) around 500 CE. It is far more extensive, thoroughly edited, and serves as the authoritative standard in Jewish law and study.

- **The Jerusalem Talmud** (*Talmud Yerushalmi*). Compiled in the Land of Israel (specifically Galilee/Tiberias) around 400 CE. It is shorter, more concise, and focuses heavily on agricultural laws relevant to the Land of Israel.

Following the organization of the Mishnah, the Talmud is divided into six broad thematic orders (Sedarim): 

1. Zeraim (Seeds/Agriculture & Prayers)
2. Moed (Festivals & Shabbat)
3. Nashim (Women/Marriage & Divorce)
4. Nezikin (Damages/Civil & Criminal Law)
5. Kodashim (Holy Things/Temple Sacrifices)
6. Tohorot (Purities)

All of them span 63 tractates in total.
