Wabi-sabi (侘寂) is a traditional Japanese aesthetic philosophy and worldview centered on the acceptance and appreciation of imperfection, impermanence, and incompleteness. Rooted in Zen [[Buddhism]] and the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu), it finds quiet beauty in things that are modest, weathered, asymmetrical, and naturally aged rather than polished, uniform, or permanent.

*[[Web/content/Notes/Just One Humankind/Just One World/Japan|Japan]]*

Origins and Meaning of the Two Concepts

- **Wabi** (侘). Originally associated with solitude, simplicity, and living close to nature away from worldly extravagance. Over time, it came to describe understated elegance, humility, and contentment with little.

- **Sabi** (寂). Refers to the serene beauty and patina that come with age, wear, and the passage of time—such as tarnished bronze, weathered wood, moss-covered stone, or a repaired ceramic bowl.

Developed in 15th- and 16th-century Japan, the philosophy was famously championed by tea master Sen no Rikyū, who replaced ornate, imported Chinese tea utensils with rough, asymmetrical, locally crafted Japanese stoneware and rustic thatched teahouses.

Core Principles

- **Impermanence** (_Mujō_). Everything in nature is in a constant state of flux—growing, aging, and decaying. Rather than resisting time, _wabi-sabi_ honors the visible marks of history and use.

- **Imperfection and asymmetry**. Nature rarely produces exact symmetry or flawless uniformity. Handcrafted irregularities, cracks, rough textures, and organic shapes give objects soul and individuality.

- **Simplicity and restraint**. Stripping away unnecessary clutter and ostentation leaves space for quiet contemplation, authenticity, and a deeper connection to natural materials (such as raw wood, clay, linen, bamboo, paper, and stone).

Examples in Japanese Culture and Design

- **_[[Kintsugi]]_** (金継ぎ). The traditional art of repairing broken pottery with lacquer dusted with powdered gold, silver, or platinum—treating fractures as an integral part of the object's history rather than something to disguise.

- **Japanese rock gardens** (_Karesansui_). Minimalist arrangements of uncarved boulders, raked gravel, and moss that celebrate raw textures and seasonal change.

- **Interior design and architecture**. Neutral earthy tones, unfinished timber, handmade ceramics, soft diffused light through _shōji_ screens, and uncluttered living spaces that embrace natural aging.
