
Reggae is a music genre that originated in Jamaica in the late 1960s and grew into a global cultural phenomenon. Instantly recognizable by its relaxed, syncopated rhythm, the style is deeply intertwined with the Rastafari movement, spirituality, and social commentary.

Reggae emerged from blending traditional Jamaican folk styles (such as mento and nyabinghi drumming) with American rhythm and blues and soul broadcast from southern U.S. radio stations:

In the early 1960s, upbeat, horn-driven ska dominated Jamaica before slowing down around 1966 into rocksteady, which placed a heavier emphasis on smooth vocals and prominent basslines.

By slowing the tempo further and sharpening the off-beat guitar chop, reggae took shape. Toots and the Maytals' 1968 single "Do the Reggay" is widely credited as the first track to use the word in its title.

The rhythm guitar and keyboards play crisp, staccato chords on the off-beats (the second and fourth beats of a 4/4 bar), creating reggae's signature swaying groove.

The electric bass acts as a lead instrument, carrying deep, melodic, and repetitive riffs that anchor the entire song.

Shaped heavily by Rastafari philosophy, reggae lyrics frequently address social justice, resistance against systemic oppression ("Babylon"), unity, peace, and spiritual repatriation to Africa.

*Bob Marley & The Wailers propelled reggae to worldwide fame in the 1970s with landmark albums like Catch a Fire and Exodus. Other foundational artists include Peter Tosh, Bunny Wailer, Jimmy Cliff, Desmond Dekker, Burning Spear, and Gregory Isaacs.*

Reggae spawned dub (pioneered by producers Lee "Scratch" Perry and King Tubby, who treated the mixing console as an instrument using echo and reverb), lovers rock, and dancehall, directly influencing the birth of hip-hop, jungle, and reggaeton.

In 2018, [[UNESCO]] inscribed reggae on its Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity list.
