Graffiti is writing or drawings painted, scratched, or sprayed onto walls and other public surfaces, typically without permission.

Ranging from quick signatures to complex, large-scale murals, it serves both as an underground subculture and a major global art movement.

Core Styles & Forms

- **Tagging**. The simplest and oldest form of modern graffiti, consisting of a stylized signature or moniker written quickly with spray paint or a marker.

- **Throw-up** (or "Throwie"). A step above a tag, featuring bubbly, rounded letters painted quickly—usually with a single outline and one-color fill.

- **Piece** (Masterpiece). A large, intricate, multi-colored painting that takes hours to plan and execute, incorporating 3D effects, shading, and characters.

- **Wildstyle**. A highly complex, interlocking form of lettering filled with arrows, spikes, and curves that is often illegible to people outside the graffiti community.

- **Stencils & Wheatpaste**. Techniques popularized within broader street art (such as by Banksy or Shepard Fairey) that use pre-cut templates or poster paste-ups to apply detailed imagery quickly.

Carvings and inscriptions on public walls date back to Ancient Egypt, Greece, and the Roman Empire—most famously preserved in the ruins of Pompeii.

 Contemporary spray-paint graffiti (1960s–1980s) emerged in Philadelphia (pioneered by writers like Cornbread) and exploded in [[New York City]], where writers like TAKI 183 covered subway cars and walls alongside the rise of hip-hop culture.
 
By the 1980s and 1990s, graffiti spread worldwide, splitting into traditional illegal "bombing" and gallery-recognized street art and commissioned murals.

*Unauthorized graffiti on public or private property is illegal and prosecuted as vandalism in most jurisdictions.*

*Many cities now designate legal "free walls" and host international mural festivals—such as those tracked by Street Art Cities—turning once-blighted areas into open-air galleries.*
