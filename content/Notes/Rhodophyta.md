
Rhodophyta, commonly known as red algae, is a phylum of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms comprising around 7,000 mostly marine species, characterized by their reddish coloration and the complete absence of flagellated cells throughout their life cycle.

Unlike land plants and other algal groups, red algae possess distinct biochemical and cellular traits:

They contain chlorophyll _a_, but their characteristic color comes from phycobiliproteins—primarily red phycoerythrin and blue phycocyanin. These accessory pigments absorb blue and green wavelengths that penetrate deeper into the ocean, allowing them to photosynthesize at depths exceeding 200 meters.

No species of Rhodophyta possesses flagella or centrioles at any stage of development, including male gametes (spermatia), which rely passively on water currents to reach the female reproductive structures.

Their cell walls contain cellulose alongside complex mucilaginous polysaccharides such as agar and carrageenan. They store energy as floridean starch, a glycogen-like polymer stored in the cytoplasm rather than inside the chloroplasts.

While a few species are unicellular or inhabit freshwater environments, the vast majority thrive in temperate and tropical oceans:

They grow as filamentous, blade-like, branched, or crustose forms. Many species, known as coralline algae, deposit calcium carbonate in their cell walls, giving them a stony texture that plays a vital role in building and cementing coral reefs.

Most multicellular species exhibit a triphasic life cycle—alternating between three distinct generations (gametophyte, carposporophyte, and tetrasporophyte)—one of the most complex in the living world.

Red algae are foundational to coastal marine ecosystems and hold high economic value worldwide:

Species in the genera _Porphyra_ and _Pyropia_ are extensively farmed in East Asia to produce nori for sushi, while _Palmaria palmata_ (dulse) and _Chondrus crispus_ (Irish moss) are harvested in Europe and North America.

They are the sole commercial source of agar-agar (an essential culture medium in microbiology and food gelling agent) and carrageenan (widely used as a thickener and stabilizer in dairy products, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals).
