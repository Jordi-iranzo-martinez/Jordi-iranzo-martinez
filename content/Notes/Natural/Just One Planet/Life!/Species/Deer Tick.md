Deer Ticks (*Ixodes scapularis*), also known as the black-legged tick, is a small, hard-bodied parasite infamous for being the primary vector of Lyme disease in eastern and northern North America.


[[Arachnids]]

The deer tick has a complex, two-to-three-year life cycle that develops through four distinct phases:

1. **Egg**: Thousands are laid by adult females in moist leaf litter during the spring. They hatch uninfected.

2. **Larva**: Tiny, six-legged larvae hatch in the summer. They seek their first blood meal from small hosts, primarily the white-footed mouse. This is where the tick typically acquires the Lyme disease bacteria (Borrelia burgdorferi).

3. **Nymph**: After molting and overwintering, they emerge in spring as eight-legged, poppy-seed-sized nymphs. Nymphs are responsible for nearly all human Lyme disease cases because their miniature size allows them to feed undetected for days.

4. **Adult**: Adult ticks peak in the fall. Females feed primarily on white-tailed deer to secure the massive protein intake required to lay their eggs before dying.

The deer tick does not transmit pathogens immediately upon biting. The bacteria reside in the tick's gut and must migrate to its salivary glands. Because of this process, the tick must generally remain attached for at least 24 to 48 hours to successfully transmit Lyme disease.
