
Sea stars (class Asteroidea, phylum [[Echinodermata]]) inhabit every ocean on Earth, from tropical coral reefs to the freezing, pitch-black abyssal trenches.

[[Ocean]], [[Deep Ocean]], [[Coral Reef]]

The vast majority of species feature a central disc from which five arms radiate, though some specialized varieties (like the sunflower sea star) can grow up to 40 arms.

On the underside of each arm, they house rows of hundreds of tiny, suction-tipped tentacles called tube feet. These limbs are powered by a water vascular system—a sophisticated hydraulic network that pumps filtered seawater through the body, allowing the star to crawl, lock firmly onto crashing wave zones, or pry open stone-hard mollusk shells.

While they lack complex eyes, they possess a microscopic, light-sensitive eyespotted organ at the very tip of each arm. These ocelli cannot form sharp images, but they allow the star to detect light, dark, and navigate toward the shadows of protecting reef structures.
