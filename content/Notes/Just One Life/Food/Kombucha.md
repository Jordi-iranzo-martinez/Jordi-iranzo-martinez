
Kombucha is a lightly effervescent, tangy-sweet fermented drink made from sweetened tea (typically black or green tea) fermented by a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast known as a SCOBY.

Originating in Northeast [[China]] over 2,000 years ago, it has gained global popularity as a functional, lower-sugar alternative to traditional sodas.

Traditional kombucha requires just four ingredients:

- **Water**
- **Tea** (*Camellia sinensis*)
- **Sugar** (which serves as food for the microbes rather than a final sweetener)
- **Starter culture** (a gelatinous SCOBY disc plus a splash of mature kombucha).

Over 7 to 14 days at room temperature, yeasts break down the sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide, while acetic acid bacteria convert much of that alcohol into organic acids (such as acetic and gluconic acid), giving the drink its signature tartness.

 After the SCOBY is removed, the liquid is often sealed in airtight bottles for another 2 to 5 days with fruit juice, herbs, or spices (such as ginger, berries, lemon, or hibiscus) to build natural carbonation and flavor.

Nutritional Profile and Precautions

- **Beneficial Compounds**. Raw, unpasteurized kombucha contains live probiotics, organic acids, tea polyphenols (antioxidants), and trace amounts of B vitamins, with significantly less residual sugar than conventional soft drinks.

- **Trace Alcohol and Caffeine**. Because of natural fermentation, standard commercial kombucha contains trace amounts of alcohol (typically under 0.5% ABV to be sold as non-alcoholic) and small amounts of caffeine from the tea leaves.

- **Safety Considerations**. While often enjoyed to support gut health and digestion, moderation is recommended (around one glass a day). Pregnant or breastfeeding women and immunocompromised individuals are generally advised to avoid unpasteurized or home-brewed kombucha due to potential contamination risks if strict sanitation isn't maintained.
