Carthage (from Phoenician *Qart-ḥadašt*, meaning "New City") was an ancient Phoenician city-state and maritime superpower located on the coast of modern-day Tunisia, near present-day Tunis.

Founded around 814 BCE by settlers from the Phoenician city of Tyre (legendarily led by Queen Dido), Carthage grew into a wealthy commercial empire that dominated the western Mediterranean for centuries before its rivalry with the Roman Republic led to its destruction in 146 BCE.

Positioned on a peninsula overlooking the Gulf of Tunis and the narrow Strait of Sicily, Carthage controlled vital east–west Mediterranean trade routes and possessed two remarkable artificial harbors (the Cothon): a rectangular commercial harbor and a circular naval harbor capable of housing over 200 warships.

Building on Phoenician seafaring traditions, Carthaginian merchants traded metals (silver, tin, copper, and gold), purple Tyrian dye, glassware, ivory, and agricultural goods across North Africa, Iberia (Spain), Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and even along the Atlantic coasts of Africa and Europe (famous expeditions by Hanno the Navigator and Himilco).

Ruled as an oligarchic republic led by two annually elected magistrates called suffetes alongside a powerful Senate, Carthage practiced a polytheistic Phoenician religion centered on deities such as Baal Hammon and Tanit.

Carthage's expansion into Sicily and the Iberian Peninsula brought it into direct conflict with rising Rome across three devastating Punic Wars (derived from Punici, the Roman name for Phoenicians):

- **First Punic War** (264–241 BCE). Fought primarily at sea and on Sicily, ending with Carthage losing Sicily and paying heavy war indemnities, though its general Hamilcar Barca subsequently conquered rich territories in southern Spain (founding Qart Hadasht, modern Cartagena).

- **Second Punic War** (218–201 BCE). Hamilcar's son, Hannibal Barca, launched a legendary invasion of Italy by marching his army—including war elephants—across the Pyrenees and the Alps, inflicting crushing defeats on Rome at Trebia, Lake Trasimene, and Cannae (216 BCE). Rome eventually counterattacked in North Africa under Scipio Africanus, defeating Hannibal at the Battle of Zama (202 BCE) and stripping Carthage of its overseas empire.

- **Third Punic War** (149–146 BCE). Driven by Roman fear of Carthage's economic recovery (famously echoed by Cato the Elder's "Carthago delenda est"—"Carthage must be destroyed"), Rome besieged the city for three years. In 146 BCE, Roman forces breached the walls, burned the city to the ground, enslaved its surviving population, and annexed its territory as the Roman province of Africa.

Roughly a century later, Julius Caesar and Augustus refounded Carthage as a Roman colony (Colonia Iulia Concordia Carthago). It rapidly became one of the wealthiest and largest cities of the Roman Empire, a major grain supplier, and an influential center of early Christianity (home to thinkers like Tertullian and Augustine of Hippo).

After falling to the Vandals, Byzantines, and finally the Umayyad Caliphate in 698 CE, the city's ruins were preserved alongside modern Tunis and inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.
