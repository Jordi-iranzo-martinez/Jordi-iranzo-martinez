**The 'mother' of the Zero Waste Movement**

Bea Johnson is a French activist known worldwide as a pioneer of the "Zero Waste" movement by her blog [Zero Waste Home](https://zerowastehome.com/).

Bea Johnson is a French-American author and speaker, widely recognized as one of the pioneering and most influential figures in the modern **"zero waste"** lifestyle movement.

Johnson was born in France and moved to the United States, where she and her family (her husband and two sons) began drastically reducing the amount of waste they generated in their everyday life. She's known for popularizing the concept of the **"5 R's"** as a practical framework for the zero waste lifestyle: 

1. **Refuse**: what you don't need
2. **Reduce** what you do need
3. **Reuse**
4. **Recycle** — and only as a last resort
5. **Rot** (compost) the rest. 

This order of priorities is central to her approach: first avoid generating waste in the first place, and recycle only when there's no other option left.

In 2013, she published her best-known book, **"Zero Waste Home,"** in which she documents her family's experience reducing their waste generation to extremely low levels — her most frequently cited example is that, for years, her family of four reportedly fit an entire year's worth of non-recyclable, non-compostable trash into a single small mason jar.

The book became an international bestseller and has been translated into multiple languages, helping bring the zero waste concept from a niche environmental practice into mainstream popular awareness. Johnson is often credited as one of the key figures who helped launch the zero waste movement as a recognizable lifestyle trend, well before "zero waste" became a common term in sustainability and lifestyle media.

Beyond the book, Johnson has become a sought-after public speaker on sustainability and waste reduction, giving talks (including a well-known TEDx talk) and consulting with individuals, businesses, and even municipalities on reducing waste.

Like many figures who become the public face of a lifestyle movement, Johnson has faced some criticism — including questions about how replicable her specific lifestyle is for people with different economic means, family situations, or access to bulk-buying stores and zero-waste infrastructure, as well as broader debates about whether individual consumer choices can meaningfully address waste at the systemic level compared to industrial and corporate practices.
