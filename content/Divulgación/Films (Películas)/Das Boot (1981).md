During te World War I (1914–1918), Germany used U-boats to try to blockade Britain and cut off its supply lines. The campaign attacking merchant ships without warning was controversial and played a role in drawing the United States into the war, especially after incidents like the sinking of the RMS Lusitania in 1915.

During World War II (1939–1945) U-boats became most famous, largely through the Battle of the Atlantic. 

Admiral Karl Dönitz developed coordinated group attacks (_Rudeltaktik_), where multiple U-boats would swarm and wolfpack Allied convoys at night.

The most widely used U-boat class was Type VII.


In 1940–1942, U-boats sank enormous amounts of Allied shipping, threatening to cut off Britain's supply lines from North America.

But by mid-1943, Allied advances, including better radar, sonar (ASDIC), long-range aircraft, codebreaking (Ultra/Enigma), and escort carriers devastated the U-boat fleet ("Black May")

U-boat crews suffered roughly 75% of casualty rates: one of the highest of any branch of the German military.

U-boats sank thousands of ships and killed tens of thousands of Allied sailors, but ultimately the campaign failed to strangle Britain's supply lines. The struggle against them drove major advances in naval technology, codebreaking, and anti-submarine warfare.

The Kriegsmarine (German war marine) used the machine Enigma to code all their communications by radio, including the orders to U-boots: positions, objectives, *Rudeltaktik*...

U-boots used a more complex variant: the Naval Enigma of 4 rotors (M4): harder to break than the 3-rotors used by the Army and the Air Force.

The capture of some Enigma machine and code books in German U-booats (U-110, 1941; U-559, 1942) led Allies to crucial material to decode. In Bletchley Park (England) a team of cryptoanalists leaded by Alan Turin worked in Ultra Project to try to decode them (filmed in [[The Imitation Game (2014)]])
