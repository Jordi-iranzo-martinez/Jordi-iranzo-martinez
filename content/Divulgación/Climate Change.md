Key points to NAPs:
- Detailed adaptation measures are necessary and should include an estimate of costs and the necessary implementation arrangements.
- A good analysis of the costs should be complemented by plans on possible sources of necessary funds for implementation, either from domestic or international climate financing sources.
- The next step is to procure the materials and labour to implement the adaptation measures, generally involving the private sector and public procurement rules.
- To ensure a full integration of climate change adaptation measures, key stakeholders from different sector agencies should be involved in the process and clear in the NAP document
- .At each step, collect the necessary data on adaptation investments that will subsequently inform the evaluation of adaptation progress and effectiveness in the review stage.

1. **Vertical integration: connecting adaptation across all levels of government** : Effective adaptation requires collaboration between national, regional, and local governments. This vertical integration ensures that climate adaptation strategies are informed by both high-level policies and local needs, supported by institutional arrangements, capacity building, and financing channels.
    
2. **Horizontal integration: embedding adaptation across all sectors**  : Climate adaptation must break down silos between sectors, such as health, water, agriculture, and infrastructure. Horizontal integration mainstreams adaptation into sectoral planning, aligning climate resilience with broader sustainable development goals and ensuring coordinated, cross-sector responses.
    
3. **Global plans, local action: adaptation starts at home**  : Climate change affects each community differently. Local governments play a crucial role in bringing national adaptation plans to life by addressing the unique needs of their areas. Tailoring actions to the local context ensures that adaptation is both practical and impactful.
    
4. **Working together across levels: adaptation is a team effort**: For climate adaptation to work, national and local governments need to stay connected. By strengthening communication and creating flexible systems for planning, both levels can ensure that local priorities are reflected in national strategies, making adaptation more cohesive.
    
5. **Local governments face real barriers to climate action**: Despite their critical role, many local governments—especially in developing countries—struggle with limited awareness of climate risks, insufficient planning capacity, and inadequate budget support from national sources. Addressing these gaps is essential for effective adaptation at all levels.

### MRV system (previous)

In the MRV system, developed Parties presented annual GHG inventory submissions, comprosed of a National Inventory Report (NIRs) and Common Reporting Format (CRF) tables which were assessed by teams of technical experts reviews. 

They also submitted Biennial reports and National Communications (NC/BRs), which were scrutinized through the International Assessment and Review (IAR)

1. Technical review
2. Multilateral Assssment (MA)

In the case of developing Parties that submitted Biennial Update Reports (BURs), they underwent the International Consultation and Analysis (ICA) process. 

1. Technical review
2. Facilitate sharing of views (FSV)

### ETH

With the implementation of the ETF, the BUr and the BR are replaced by a Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) which will undergo a common technical expert review (TER) process that will supersede both the ICA and IAR processes including a Facilitative Multilateral Consideration of Progress, which will focus on the BTR. 

The BTR will cover information on NIR progress toawrds NDCs, policies and measures, climate change impacts and adaptation, levels of financial, technoology development and transfer and capacity-building support, capacity-building needs and areas of improvement. 

Moreover, Parties will submit additional information on education, training, public awareness and research and systematic observation. In addition, REDD+ information

Parties have also to submit Common Reporting Tables (CRTs) and Common Tabular Formats (CTFs). 


The BTRs will be subject to Technical Expert Review,

- **Waste Management** - Addresses methane emissions from landfills, waste treatment processes, recycling systems, and circular economy approaches to reduce waste generation.

- **Cancun Adaptation Framework**: Established at COP16 (2010) in Cancun, Mexico, this framework enhances action on adaptation by providing a structure for coherent consideration of adaptation actions. It aims to reduce vulnerability and build resilience to climate change impacts through better planning, implementation, and support for adaptation efforts.
- **Nairobi Work Programme**: Launched at COP12 (2006), this programme assists countries, particularly developing nations, in improving their understanding and assessment of climate change impacts, vulnerability and adaptation. It facilitates information sharing, promotes stakeholder engagement, and supports evidence-based decision-making on adaptation.

## The International Panel on Climate Change

The International Panel on Climate Change was created in 1988 by the [United Nations  Environmental Programme](https://www.unep.org/) (UNEP) and the [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/) (WMO) in order to better understand the climate change.

It is composed by thousands of expert scientists from around the world who voluntary work reviewing and synthesizing the most recent climate research. They do not conduct original research themselves, but rather assess and summarize existing scientific knowledge.

- Working Group I (WGI) "The Physical Science Basis" – focuses on understanding the climate system and observed changes.
- Working Group II (WGII): Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability – assesses how climate change affects ecosystems, society, and the economy.
- Working Group III (WGIII): Mitigation of Climate Change – analyzes options for reducing greenhouse gas emissions.
- Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories (TFI): develops methodologies for measuring and reporting emissions.

Their **Assessment Reports**, published approximately every 6–7 years, are considered the most authoritative scientific reference on climate change and guide global climate policy.

For its labor, the IPCC won the Nobel Prize in 2007, among with Al Gore, because its "*for their efforts to build up and disseminate greater knowledge about man-made climate change, and to lay the foundations for the measures that are needed to counteract such change*".

Its main goal of the UNFCCC is the stabilization of greenhouse gases to a sustainable level - one that prevents adverse effects like extreme meteorological events, while ensuring ecosystem adaptance, food security, and economic sustainable development.
