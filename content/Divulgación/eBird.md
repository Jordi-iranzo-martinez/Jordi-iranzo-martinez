
eBird is the world's largest citizen science project dedicated to ornithology and birdwatching. Created in 2002 by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and the National Audubon Society, it operates as a massive global database where anyone—from backyard birdwatchers to professional ornithologists—can log the birds they see.

In the context of wildlife conservation, eBird has completely transformed how scientists track and protect bird species.

- **Real-Time Checklists:** Using a free mobile app, users log the bird species they observe or hear in the field. The app automatically tracks the exact location (via GPS), the time spent, and the number of individual birds.

- **Scientific Verification:** To ensure data quality, eBird relies on a global network of **expert volunteer reviewers**. If a user logs a highly unusual species, a rare hybrid, or an unexpected bird outside its migration window, the system flags the entry. A local expert then reviews the submission, often requesting photos or audio recordings before validating it.

- **Open-Access & Dynamic Maps:** All data gathered is freely accessible to the public. Through their website, you can view interactive global distribution maps, check what birds are currently being seen at local birding destinations (known as _Hotspots_), and maintain your personal lifetime checklist (_Life List_).

Before platforms like eBird existed, scientists had to rely on official, heavily funded censuses that were only conducted every few years. Now, thanks to millions of active users worldwide, eBird provides:

- **Real-Time Decline Detection:** If a species begins to vanish from user checklists in a specific region, scientists can sound the alarm years before an official population collapse is formally documented.

- **Mapping Migration Corridors:** It has allowed researchers to map precise migration flyways, record exact arrival dates, and study how climate change is altering avian behavior globally.

- **Targeted Habitat Protection:** If eBird data proves that an endangered bird consistently utilizes a specific patch of agricultural land, conservation groups can use that legal evidence to advocate for the protection of that habitat against industrial development.
