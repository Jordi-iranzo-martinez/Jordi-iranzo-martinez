"Bloody Saturday" refers to a famous, harrowing photograph taken on August 28, 1937, during the Battle of Shanghai in the early stages of the Second Sino-Japanese War.

On that day, Japanese aircraft bombed Shanghai's South Railway Station, which was packed with civilian refugees trying to flee the fighting. The station had no military significance — it was crowded with women, children, and other civilians waiting for trains out of the city. The bombing killed and wounded hundreds of people.

Chinese cameraman H.S. "Newsreel" Wong (Wang Xiaoting), working for Hearst's Movietone News, was on the scene shortly after the attack. His most famous image shows a badly burned Chinese infant sitting alone and crying amid the wreckage of the bombed-out station, surrounded by twisted metal and debris.

The photo was published widely in the West, appearing in _Life_ magazine and elsewhere, and became one of the most viewed news images of the era — some estimates suggest hundreds of millions of people saw it. It played a major role in shaping international, especially American, public opinion against Japan's actions in China, well before World War II drew in the U.S. It's often cited as one of the most effective and widely circulated pieces of war photojournalism in history, galvanizing sympathy for China and outrage at Japanese military conduct.

Over the years, some historians and Japanese sources have questioned aspects of the photo's context — for instance, whether the scene was staged or how representative it was — though the mainstream historical view still treats it as a genuine and significant document of the bombing's civilian toll.
