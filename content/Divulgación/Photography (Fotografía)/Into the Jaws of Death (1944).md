
*"Into the Jaws of Death" is a famous photograph taken by U.S. Coast Guard Chief Petty Officer Robert F. Sargent on June 6, 1944 — D-Day, the Allied invasion of Normandy during World War II.*

![[photography_d-landing.png]]

The image shows American soldiers of the U.S. Army's 1st Infantry Division wading off the ramp of a Higgins boat (LCVP) into the surf at Omaha Beach, one of the two American landing zones during the invasion, under German fire. The photo captures the soldiers from behind, moving from the relative safety of the landing craft directly into chest-deep water and toward the beach — visually embodying the vulnerability and courage of the moment, hence the title.

Omaha Beach was by far the deadliest of the five D-Day landing beaches. American forces there faced heavily fortified German positions on the bluffs above the beach, including machine gun nests and artillery that had survived pre-invasion bombardment largely intact. Casualties at Omaha were severe, particularly in the first waves of troops, some of which suffered devastating losses within minutes of landing.

Sargent was one of very few photographers who captured the actual moment of landing on D-Day, largely because of the chaos and danger involved (unlike, for instance, Robert Capa's blurry Omaha Beach photos, which were taken slightly later and became famous partly due to a lab accident that destroyed most of the roll). "Into the Jaws of Death" became one of the most widely reproduced images of the Normandy invasion, and by extension of World War II as a whole, used for decades in books, documentaries, and commemorations of D-Day.

D-Day itself is remembered as the turning point that opened the Western Front in Europe, beginning the liberation of France and ultimately Nazi-occupied Western Europe. Roughly 156,000 Allied troops landed in Normandy that day across the five beaches (Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, and Sword), in what remains the largest seaborne invasion in history.
