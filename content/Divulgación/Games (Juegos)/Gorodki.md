Gorodki (literally meaning "little cities" or "townlets" in Russian) is a centuries-old traditional Russian folk sport. It shares conceptual similarities with bowling, skittles, or horseshoes, but instead of rolling a ball, players throw a heavy wooden bat to knock structures out of a square zone.

The main objective is to completely clear 15 specific formations made of wooden pins using the fewest possible number of throws.

- **The Gorod (City):** The playing area is a **2-meter by 2-meter marked square**, typically set up on flat ground like asphalt, gravel, or concrete.

- **The Gorodki (Pins):** Five cylindrical wooden pins, each about **20 centimeters long and 4.5–5 centimeters in diameter**.

- **The Bats:** Heavy wooden rods or bats measuring just under **1 meter in length**.

- **Formations:** The 5 pins are stacked at the front edge of the "city" square to form one of **15 predefined structural shapes**. These shapes have traditional names like _The Cannon_, _The Well_, _The Arrow_, _The Airplane_, and _The Letter_.

- **Throwing Distances:**
    - Players begin throwing from the furthest distance line, called the **Kon**, located **13 meters away**.
    - Once a player manages to knock at least one pin completely out of the square, they are allowed to advance and take their remaining throws from a closer line, called the **Polukon** (half-kon), located **6.5 meters away**.
    - _(Exception: The final formation, "The Letter", must always be cleared entirely from the 13-meter line).

- **Clearing the Pins:** A pin is only considered successfully cleared or "banished" when it is **knocked completely outside the boundaries** of the 2-meter square.

- **Winning:** Players take turns (usually getting 2 throws per turn). The winner is the individual or team that clears all 15 sequential formations using the **lowest total number of throws**.

The game originated hundreds of years ago as a popular folk activity among Russian peasants. It was celebrated for requiring physical strength, precision, and hand-eye coordination using easily accessible materials.

- **Famous Players:** Despite its humble rural roots, it grew to be deeply loved by Russia's elite. Historical figures like **Peter the Great, Alexander Suvorov, Vladimir Lenin, and Joseph Stalin** were avid players, as well as cultural icons like the author **Leo Tolstoy** and scientist **Ivan Pavlov**.

- **Soviet Codification:** For centuries, the game had no standardized rules. This changed on **August 19, 1923**, when the rules were officially codified for the first all-Union competition in Moscow. During the Soviet era, it became a massive institutionalized sport with hundreds of thousands of registered players.

- **Modern Era:** While its peak popularity declined after the fall of the USSR, Gorodki is still played in parks, celebrated through dedicated international tournaments, and has even been featured in modern popular culture—such as a challenge on the reality show _The Amazing Race_ or as a digital game on the Nintendo Wii.
