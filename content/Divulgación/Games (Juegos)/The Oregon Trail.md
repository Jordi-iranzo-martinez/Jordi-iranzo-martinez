"The Oregon Trail" is one of the most influential and beloved educational video games in history, first created in 1971 and best known through its widely distributed 1985 version.

![[{1F02A124-70A1-4F6C-AD1D-57C86CB3ED38}.png]]


The original game was created by three student teachers — Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann, and Paul Dillenberger — in Minnesota in 1971, as a teaching tool for an 8th-grade history class. It was written for a mainframe computer (a teletype terminal, with no graphics) and simulated the experience of a family traveling west along the historic Oregon Trail in a covered wagon during the 19th century.

The game was later acquired and developed by the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium (MECC), which released significantly upgraded versions with graphics — most famously the 1985 Apple II version, which became a fixture in American classrooms throughout the 1980s and 1990s, making it one of the most widely played educational games ever created for an entire generation of American schoolchildren.

Players manage a group of pioneers traveling from Missouri to Oregon's Willamette Valley, making decisions about supplies, pacing, hunting for food, fording rivers, and managing resources, all while facing various hazards along the way — including the game's most famous and often-memed feature: the frequent, sudden deaths of party members from dysentery, snakebites, drowning, or other ailments, delivered via the deadpan message "You have died of dysentery."

The game became a genuine cultural phenomenon and nostalgia touchstone for generations of Americans who grew up playing it in school computer labs. "You have died of dysentery" became a widely recognized internet meme and cultural reference point. The franchise has been remade and re-released multiple times over the decades, including modern mobile and browser versions.

The historical Oregon Trail was a roughly 2,000-mile overland route used by hundreds of thousands of American emigrants to travel from the Missouri River to the Oregon Territory (and connecting routes to California and Utah) during the mid-19th century.

The trail stretched from jumping-off towns like Independence, Westport, and later St. Joseph, Missouri, westward through what is now Kansas, Nebraska, Wyoming, Idaho, and finally into Oregon's Willamette Valley. It followed natural landmarks and river valleys — notably the Platte River, then the North Platte, and through South Pass in Wyoming, a relatively gentle crossing of the Continental Divide that made wagon travel possible. Famous landmarks along the way included Chimney Rock, Fort Laramie, Independence Rock, and Fort Bridger.

Portions of the route had long been used by Native American nations and by fur trappers and traders. The trail became passable for wagons in the 1830s, with missionaries like Marcus and Narcissa Whitman among the early travelers who helped prove wagons could make the journey. The first large wagon train migration is generally dated to 1836 or 1841-43, with the "Great Migration" of 1843 (about 1,000 settlers) often marking the start of mass overland travel.

Emigration peaked in the 1840s-1860s, with historians estimating somewhere between 300,000 and 400,000 people used the trail and its offshoots (including the California and Mormon trails, which shared much of the same route before splitting off). Motivations varied: farmland and economic opportunity in Oregon, gold in California after 1848, and religious refuge for Mormon settlers heading to Utah.

The trip typically took four to six months by wagon, usually starting in spring so travelers could cross mountain passes before winter snow. Wagons were pulled mostly by oxen (hardier and cheaper than horses or mules) and were often packed with only a few months of supplies, since space was limited and most goods had to be purchased along the way or hunted/foraged.

Contrary to popular imagination (and the video game's famous snakebite and drowning deaths), the leading cause of death on the trail was actually **disease**, especially cholera, which spread rapidly in the unsanitary conditions of large wagon trains and could kill within hours of the first symptoms. Accidents (drownings at river crossings, being run over by wagons, accidental gunshot wounds) were also significant causes of death. Conflict with Native American tribes did occur, but was statistically a far less common cause of death than disease and accidents — a fact that popular Western mythology has historically distorted.

The trail had a profound and often devastating impact on the Native American nations whose lands it crossed — including the Sioux, Cheyenne, Shoshone, and Pawnee, among others. The massive westward migration brought disease, depleted game and resources (especially bison) that Native communities depended on, and set the stage for escalating conflicts and treaty violations as U.S. expansion accelerated in subsequent decades.

The trail's importance declined rapidly after the completion of the First Transcontinental Railroad in 1869, which made the journey west dramatically faster, safer, and cheaper, effectively ending the era of mass wagon migration.
