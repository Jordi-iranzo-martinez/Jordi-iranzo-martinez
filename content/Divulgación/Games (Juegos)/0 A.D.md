15 different civilizations:

The **[[Athenians (0 AD)]]** (~508 BC – 322 BC): residents of the most influential democratic city-state in ancient Greece. They are globally celebrated for laying the foundation of Western philosophy, classical theater, and architecture.

	Greek city-state in Attica that pioneered direct democracy and naval power; center of classical philosophy, drama, and architecture (the Parthenon). Rivaled Sparta; lost independence to Macedon after the Lamian War.

The **[[Spartans (0 AD)]]** (~650 BC – 192 BC): residents of the most influential democratic city-state in ancient Greece. They are globally celebrated for laying the foundation of Western philosophy, classical theater, and architecture.

Militaristic Greek city-state in the Peloponnese, famous for rigorous warrior training (the _agoge_), hoplite infantry, and dual kingship. Dominated Greece after defeating Athens in the Peloponnesian War; declined after defeat at Leuctra (371 BC).

The **[[Persians (0 AD)]]** (550 a.C. – 330 a.C): creators of the vast Achaemenid Empire, famous for connecting the ancient world through advanced roads, decentralized local governors (satraps), and religious tolerance.

The Achaemenid Empire — the first great Near Eastern superpower, stretching from Egypt to the Indus under Cyrus the Great and Darius I. Known for royal roads and relatively tolerant rule over diverse peoples. Ended with Alexander's conquest.

The **[[Iberians (0 AD)]]** (~ VI a.C. – siglo I a.C.): the indigenous pre-Roman cultures of the eastern and southern Iberian Peninsula, recognized for their sophisticated metallurgy, urban settlements, and unique artistic styles.

Indigenous peoples of eastern/southern Iberia (Spain/Portugal), shaped by Phoenician and Greek trade contact. Known for fortified hilltop towns (_oppida_) and skilled warriors, some serving as mercenaries for Carthage. Gradually Romanized.

The **[[Carthaginians (0 AD)]]** (~814 a.C. – 146 a.C.): a Phoenician-descended maritime empire centered in North Africa. They dominated Mediterranean trade networks and fought Rome fiercely during the historic Punic Wars.

Phoenician-founded trading power based in North Africa (modern Tunisia), dominant across the western Mediterranean. Fought Rome in the Punic Wars, including Hannibal's invasion of Italy. Destroyed by Rome in 146 BC.

The **[[Romans (0 AD)]]** (753 a.C. – 476 d.C. (Rome) and 1453 d.C. (Bizancium)): an exceptionally enduring civilization that grew from an Italian city-state into a massive empire across three continents. They profoundly shaped Western law, military strategy, engineering, and romance languages.

Grew from a small city-state into the ancient world's largest empire, spanning from Britain to the Middle East. Known for law, engineering, and military organization. The Western Empire fell in 476 AD; the Byzantine East endured until 1453.

The **[[Gauls (0 AD)]]** (~siglo V a.C. – 51 a.C.): a diverse collection of Celtic tribes inhabiting western Europe. They were known for their expert metalworking, fierce resistance to Roman expansion, and distinct druidic culture.

Celtic-speaking peoples of modern France and Belgium, organized into tribal confederations. Skilled metalworkers and warriors who sacked Rome in 390 BC; conquered by Julius Caesar in the Gallic Wars.

The **[[Germans (0 AD)]]** (~ I a.C. – migraciones (siglos IV–VI d.C.): an ethno-linguistic group of tribes from northern and central Europe whose movements and eventual conflicts heavily accelerated the collapse of the Western Roman Empire.

Germanic-speaking tribes north of the Rhine and Danube, documented by Roman writers like Caesar and Tacitus. Alternately fought and allied with Rome; large-scale migrations helped bring down the Western Roman Empire.

The **[[Kushites (0 AD)]]** (~1070 a.C. – 350 d.C.): an ancient African kingdom based in Nubia (modern Sudan) that frequently competed with Egypt, famously ruling it as the 25th Dynasty, and flourishing as an iron-manufacturing hub.

Nubian civilization along the Nile (modern Sudan) that at one point ruled Egypt itself (the 25th Dynasty "Black Pharaohs"). Later centered at Meroë, known for ironworking and pyramid-building. Declined after conflict with Aksum.

The **[[Han Dynasty (0 AD)]]** (206 a.C. – 220 d.C.): the golden age empire of ancient China that consolidated imperial bureaucracy, unified Chinese culture, and formally opened the global Silk Road trading network.

Chinese imperial dynasty following the Qin, widely seen as a golden age that shaped Chinese identity ("Han Chinese"). Expanded imperial borders, opened the Silk Road, and standardized writing, currency, and administration.

The **[[Ptolemies (0 AD)]]** (305 a.C. – 30 a.C.): a Greek royal family that ruled Egypt following Alexander the Great's conquests. They famously blended Hellenistic and traditional Egyptian customs, ending with Queen Cleopatra.

Greek-Macedonian dynasty ruling Egypt after Alexander's death, founded by his general Ptolemy I. Blended Greek and Egyptian culture and built the Library of Alexandria. Ended with Cleopatra VII and Roman annexation.

The **[[Seleucids (0 AD)]]** (312 a.C. – 63 a.C.): a massive Hellenistic empire encompassing the Middle East and parts of Central Asia. They specialized in founding new Greek-style cities to integrate diverse territories.

The largest of Alexander's successor states, spanning from Anatolia to India at its peak. A Hellenistic empire ruling a highly diverse population; gradually lost ground to Parthia and Rome before annexation.

The **[[Mauryans (0 AD)]]** (322 a.C. – 185 a.C.): the first major empire to unify the vast majority of the Indian subcontinent. Their most famous ruler, Ashoka the Great, spread Buddhism and peaceful governance across Asia.

First empire to unify most of the Indian subcontinent, founded by Chandragupta Maurya. Peaked under Ashoka the Great, who promoted Buddhism after a brutal conquest. Known for centralized administration and trade networks.

The **[[Britons (0 AD)]]** (~ VIII a.C. – V d.C.): the Celtic peoples inhabiting Great Britain prior to the Anglo-Saxon migrations, characterized by tribal kingdoms, intricate metalwork, and prolonged resistance to Roman occupation.

Celtic-speaking Iron Age tribes of the island of Britain, partly conquered and Romanized as "Britannia." After Rome's withdrawal, Anglo-Saxon settlement pushed many Britons into Wales, Cornwall, and Brittany, where their culture persisted for centuries.
