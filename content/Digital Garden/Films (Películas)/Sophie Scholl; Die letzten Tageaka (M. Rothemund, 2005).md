
Sophie Scholl; Die letzten Tageaka (M. Rothemund, 2005) relates the story of Sophie Scholl, a 21-years-old student who formed a clandestine pacific resistance group colled *Die Weiße Rose* (The White Rose), in Münich during the Nazi regime.

Die Weiße Rose was a group was formed in 1942 by a small circle of friends at the University of Munich: Hans Scholl, his sister Sophie Scholl, Christoph Probst, Alexander Schmorell, and Willi Graf, and later philosophy professor Kurt Huber. 

Most of the male students were medical students and military veterans who had been deployed to the Eastern Front as medics. There, they witnessed firsthand the horrific massacres of Jewish civilians and the brutal reality of the Nazi war machine. This shattered any illusions they had about the regime and compelled them to act.

Realizing they could not fight the regime with weapons, they used ideas. They purchased a secret duplicating machine and began writing, printing, and mailing anonymous pamphlets called Leaflets of the White Rose.

They mailed these leaflets to random addresses across Germany, urging citizens to wake up, sabotage the war effort, and overthrow Hitler. In early 1943, as the German army suffered a catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Stalingrad, the group grew bolder, painting massive slogans like "Freedom!" and "Down with Hitler!" on the walls of Munich's streets.

On the morning of February 18, 1943, Hans and Sophie brought a suitcase packed with their 6th leaflet directly into the main atrium of the University of Munich while classes were in session. They left neat stacks outside lecture hall doors so students would find them when the bells rang.

As they were preparing to leave, Sophie noticed they had leaflets left over. She pushed the remaining stack off the top-floor balcony, sending hundreds of flyers fluttering down into the empty courtyard below. A pro-Nazi university janitor named Jakob Schmid saw her do it. He immediately locked the building's doors and called the Gestapo.

Sophie was handed over to Gestapo investigator Robert Mohr. The psychological battle depicted in the film is accurate. Mohr initially thought she was an innocent girl caught in the crossfire. He even offered her a legal loophole: if she blamed her brother Hans and claimed she was just following him blindly, she could walk free. Sophie refused. She insisted that she was equally responsible and proud of what she did.



Just four days after their arrest, on February 22, 1943, Hans, Sophie, and Christoph Probst were hauled before the fanatical Nazi "People's Court". The judge, Roland Freisler, screamed and insulted them for hours. Denied defense witnesses, Sophie calmly told the judge: 

>"Somebody, after all, had to make a start. What we wrote and said is also believed by many others. They just don't dare express themselves as we did."

>Einer musste ja schließlich damit anfangen"

(Somebody, after all, had to make a start)

They were sentenced to death for high treason and executed by guillotine just hours later at Stadelheim Prison. She was only 21 years old.

After the war, Mohr was arrested and interned by the French army. Because he had handled the Scholl interrogation without physical torture or violence, Sophie's father (who survived the war) actually wrote a letter confirming that Mohr had treated his children professionally. 

Thanks to this, Mohr was never tried for war crimes. He was released after a couple of years and spent the rest of his life working as an administrative worker and lifeguard at a public spa in a small German town, dying peacefully in 1977.

*Many of the intense debates between Sophie Scholl and Robert Mohr in the film are delivered using the exact, word-for-word sentences recorded by the Gestapo typist in 1943.*
