Octubre de 1993. Soldados americanos de élite son enviados a Mogadiscio (Somalia) en una misión de paz de las Naciones Unidas, pero su principal objetivo es capturar al caudillo Aidid y acabar de raíz con la guerra. La misión se complica cuando dos helicópteros Black Hawk son derribados. Entonces lo único que importa es rescatar a los soldados que han quedado atrapados o han resultado heridos en el accidente.


Somalia gained independence in 1960 and was ruled from 1969 by Siad Barre, a military dictator who took power in a coup. Barre played Cold War politics skillfully, switching alliances between the Soviet Union and the United States to secure military aid, while ruling through a mix of socialism, clan patronage, and repression. Clan identity had always mattered in Somali society, but Barre's regime deliberately manipulated and armed rival clans against each other to stay in power, which left deep, dangerous divisions once his government fell apart.

By the late 1980s, opposition movements — mostly organized along clan lines — had turned into armed insurgencies. Barre's regime responded with extreme brutality, including bombing his own city of Hargeisa. The state effectively disintegrated, and in January 1991 Barre was overthrown and fled the capital. But no single faction was strong enough to replace him, so Somalia split into competing clan militias fighting for control, with no functioning central government at all — a total state collapse, not just a change of ruler.

The fighting devastated agriculture and trade routes, and militias routinely looted food and blocked aid convoys as a weapon of war. By 1992, a severe famine killed an estimated 300,000 Somalis, with images of mass starvation shocking the world and putting pressure on the international community to act.

- **UNOSOM I (1992)** — A small UN peacekeeping force was sent to protect aid shipments, but it lacked the mandate and manpower to stop militias from hijacking food convoys.

- **Operation Restore Hope / UNITAF (Dec 1992)** — The US led a much larger, US-dominated coalition force to secure aid distribution by force if necessary. This phase was relatively successful at getting food moving again.

- **UNOSOM II (1993)** — Command transferred to the UN, with a broader and more ambitious mandate: not just protecting aid, but disarming militias and helping rebuild Somali state institutions. This is where the mission shifted from humanitarian protection toward nation-building and, eventually, direct confrontation with Aidid's faction after his forces ambushed and killed 24 Pakistani peacekeepers in June 1993.

After that ambush, the UN Security Council authorized action against those responsible, and the US deployed Task Force Ranger specifically to hunt Aidid and his lieutenants. Several raids were conducted through the summer of 1993, escalating tensions with Aidid's Habr Gidr clan and much of the local population, who increasingly saw the mission as a foreign occupation rather than humanitarian help. That escalation set the stage for the October 3–4 battle.





The Battle of Mogadishu was fought October 3–4, 1993.

Somalia had collapsed into civil war and famine after the fall of dictator Siad Barre in 1991.

The United Nations and the United States of America intervened in Operation Restore Hope (later UNOSOM II), initially to protect humanitarian aid deliveries.

Warlord Mohamed Farrah Aidid, one of the most powerful militia leaders in Mogadishu, became a direct target after his forces killed 24 Pakistani UN peacekeepers in June 1993.

The United States deployed Task Force Ranger (Army Rangers and Delta Force operators) specifically to capture Aidid and his top lieutenants.

On October 3th 1993 Task Force Ranger launched a daylight raid into the Bakara Market district, a hostile stronghold, to capture two of Aidid's senior officials attending a meeting. The plan was for a fast helicopter insertion, a quick snatch operation, and extraction by ground convoy, expected to take under an hour.

The operation devolved into one of the fiercest urban firefights US forces had faced since Vietnam.

Somali militia and armed civilians converged on the area in huge numbers, far more than anticipated.

A UH-60 Black Hawk helicopter (callsign Super Six-One) was shot down by a rocket-propelled grenade, and a second one (Super Six-Four) shortly after.

Ground convoys trying to reach the crash sites got lost and pinned down in a maze of streets under constant fire.

At the second crash site, two Delta Force snipers, Master Sergeant Gary Gordon and Sergeant First Class Randy Shughart, volunteered to protect the wounded pilot, Michael Durant, despite being told the situation was likely unsurvivable. Both were killed defending the crash site.

Durant was captured and held for 11 days before being released. Gordon and Shughart were posthumously awarded the Medal of Honor.



The American forces where surrounded and fought through the night, until a relief convoy of US, Pakistani, and Malaysian troops fought their way in the next morning to extract them.

18 American soldiers were killed and around 73 wounded. Somali casualties were estimated from several hundred to over a thousand killed, including many civilians.

Images of a dead American soldier's body being dragged through the streets by a crowd, broadcast on US television, caused a political firestorm.

The backlash led President Clinton to withdraw US forces from Somalia within months, and the battle became a defining case study in the risks of urban combat and "mission creep" — it strongly influenced US reluctance to commit ground troops to Rwanda the following year ([[Hotel Rwanda (T. George, 2004)]]), and shaped military doctrine on urban warfare for years afterward.

- The US withdrew all forces from Somalia by March 1994; the broader UN mission wound down by 1995 without having restored a functioning government.

- Somalia remained without a stable central government for roughly two decades afterward, becoming a textbook example of a "failed state" — a period marked by continued clan warfare, the rise of Islamist factions including al-Shabaab, and piracy off its coast in the 2000s.

- Aidid himself was never captured; he died in 1996 from wounds sustained in factional fighting, still contesting for power.

The "Mogadishu effect" made US policymakers in Washington deeply wary of humanitarian military interventions with open-ended mandates. It is widely cited as a factor in the US and UN's reluctance to intervene during the Rwandan genocide ([[Hotel Rwanda (T. George, 2004)]]) just six months later, despite clear warning signs.
