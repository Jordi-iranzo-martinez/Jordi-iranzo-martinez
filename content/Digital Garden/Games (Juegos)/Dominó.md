Dominoes (known as Dominó in Spanish) is a globally celebrated tile-matching game. It belongs to the family of "edge-matching" games, where players alternate placing rectangular tiles end-to-end, ensuring that adjacent values match.

The primary objective in a standard match is to be the first player to discard all your tiles, or to score points based on the tiles remaining in your opponents' hands.

A domino tile (traditionally nicknamed a "bone") is a rectangular tile split down the center into two square ends. Each end is marked with a number of dots—called pips—or left completely blank.

Double-Six Set (Standard): The most common set internationally. It features 28 tiles ranging from double-blank (0-0) up to double-six (6-6).

**Double-Nine Set (Cuban Variant):** Highly popular in Central and Western Cuba, this version uses an expanded set of **55 tiles** going up to 9-9. It dramatically spikes the strategic depth and calculation required.

While dozens of regional rule sets exist, the foundational style is the Block Game played by 2 to 4 players:

- **The Deal (Shuffling):** All tiles are placed face down and mixed together (an action traditionally called _"dar agua"_ or "giving water" in Spanish-speaking cultures). Each player draws **7 tiles**, keeping them hidden from the rest of the table.

- **The Opening:** The player holding the highest double—traditionally the **double-six (6-6)**—places it in the center of the table to start the game.

- **Matching Ends:** Play moves clockwise (or counter-clockwise in some Latin American traditions). On your turn, you must place a tile against one of the two open ends of the line, ensuring the numbers match exactly. Doubles are usually played crosswise.

- **Passing or Drawing:** If you do not possess a matching tile on your turn:
    - In a **Draw Game**, you must pull new tiles from the leftover pile (the boneyard) until you find a playable one.
    - In a strict **Block Game**, you simply pass your turn.

- **End of the Round:** A round ends immediately when one player runs out of tiles (known as _Domino_). Alternatively, a round can become **"blocked" or "locked" (_trancado_)**, meaning no player has a valid matching move left.

- **Round Winner:** If someone goes out, they win the round. If the board is blocked, players count the total number of pips left in their hands; the person or team with the **lowest total score wins the round.**
- **Points Calculation:** The round winner receives points equal to the **sum total of all pips remaining** in the losers' hands.
- **The Match:** Rounds are repeated until a player or a team reaches a predetermined threshold, usually **100 points**. The earliest written accounts of domino-like tiles appear in China during the **Song Dynasty**. These early layouts represented all the possible outcomes of rolling two six-sided dice simultaneously. Curiously, some of the earliest imperial sets were carved out of **donkey hooves** that were boiled down, flattened, and hand-drilled!

The Italian Transformation (1700s): The game arrived in Europe in the 18th century through Italy. European players altered the Chinese system by introducing blank faces and scaling it down to a 28-tile matrix.

Etymology: The name "domino" likely comes from the French word for a black-hooded carnival robe worn with a white mask in Venice, mirroring the contrast of dark ivory pips on a stark white tile face.
