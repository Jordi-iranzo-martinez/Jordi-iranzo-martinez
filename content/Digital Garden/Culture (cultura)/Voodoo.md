
Voodoo is a syncretic religion with deep roots in West Africa that developed in the Americas, particularly in Haiti, as a result of the transatlantic slave trade. While popular culture often misrepresents it as black magic or a horror movie trope, it is actually a rich, complex spiritual tradition focused on healing, community, and ancestral connection.

It blends the traditional religions of West Africa (specifically the Fon and Yoruba peoples) with elements of Roman Catholicism. Practitioners believe in a supreme creator God (Bondye) and communicate with intermediate spirits called Loa through ritual, music, and dance.

Louisiana Voodoo (New Orleans Voodoo) is an adaptation evolved in United States ([[Gabriel Knight; Sins of the Fathers (Sierra On-Line, 1993)]]) which incorporated local folklore, herbalism (such as gris-gris bags), and was shaped by historical figures like Voodoo Queen Marie Laveau.

 Voodoo is fundamentally a religion centered on healing, protecting the living, and honoring ancestors, rather than a system designed to harm others.
 
*In traditional Haitian Vodou, sticking pins into dolls to cause harm is not a standard practice. This concept was heavily sensationalized by Hollywood and actually stems from European folk magic and American Hoodoo (a separate system of folk magic), rather than the formal Voodoo religion.*
