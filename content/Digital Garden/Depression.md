Depression is a serious and common mood disorder that deeply affects how a person thinks, feels, and handles daily activities. Unlike temporary sadness, depression involves a persistent low mood or loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia) lasting for at least two consecutive weeks, impairing a person's ability to function.

Common Clinical Symptoms

Symptoms can range from mild to severe and include both emotional and physical manifestations:

- **Depressed Mood:** Feelings of sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, or outbursts of irritability.
- **Anhedonia:** A markedly diminished interest or pleasure in almost all activities once enjoyed.
- **Sleep and Energy Changes:** Insomnia, hypersomnia (sleeping too much), and fatigue or extreme loss of energy nearly every day.
- **Physical Shifts:** Drastic changes in appetite or weight, unexplained physical aches (headaches, digestive issues), and psychomotor slowing.
- **Impaired Cognition:** Difficulties concentrating, indecisiveness, and pathological feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt.
- **Suicidal Ideation:** Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal thoughts, or self-harm attempts.

Clinical Treatment Options

Depression management is tailored based on the severity of the diagnosis determined by a healthcare professional:

- **Mild**: Psychological interventions (such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy or CBT) are prioritized before turning to medication, accompanied by structured lifestyle changes (guided exercise, sleep hygiene).

- **Moderate to Severe**: A combination of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy (antidepressives like SSRIs or SNRIs) is standard to maximize remission rates and mitigate relapses.

- **Treatment-Resistant**: If there is no remission after trying two or more adequate medications, current medical guidelines consider advanced options like nasal esketamine, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), or electroconvulsive therapy (ECT).


If you or a loved one are experiencing thoughts of self-harm, severe difficulties caring for yourselves, or a mental health crisis, it is crucial to seek professional assistance immediately. Local emergency services and suicide prevention lifelines are available free of charge and confidentially to provide crisis support.

Depression is not caused by a single factor. In the medical community, it is understood as a complex biopsychosocial disorder, meaning it arises from the interaction of three major categories: biological, psychological, and environmental factors.

A common way to explain this is the "vulnerability-stress model": our biology provides a certain level of vulnerability, and life events act as the trigger.

In a depressed brain, the transmission of neurotransmitters like serotonina, dopamine, and norepinephrine is often altered or depleted. Genetics influence how your brain physically processes stress and adapts to the world. Alterations in cortisol (the stress hormone) or hormonal fluctuations during postpartum, menopause, or thyroid issues deeply impact brain chemistry.



2. Environmental & Social Factors (The Triggers)

- **Chronic Stress & Trauma:** 

Living under prolonged pressure—such as financial hardship or a toxic workplace—drains the brain's cognitive resources. High-impact events like losing a loved one, a painful breakup, or relocating (moving away) often serve as acute triggers. Experiencing emotional or physical abuse, neglect, or instability during early years can physically alter the brain's developing stress-response system, making a person more vulnerable to depression in adulthood. Lacking a solid support system (friends, family, or community) greatly increases the risk of onset and makes recovery harder.

Certain personality traits, such as chronic low self-esteem, deep pessimism, extreme perfectionism, or a tendency to constantly ruminate (overthink) on negative thoughts, make it easier for daily challenges to spiral into clinical depression.
