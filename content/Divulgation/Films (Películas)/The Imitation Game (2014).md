**Cryptanalysis of the Enigma** — Turing's work breaking the German Enigma cipher at Bletchley Park during WWII, using the electromechanical Bombe he designed (building on earlier work by Polish cryptologists).

**Turing's proof** — His 1936 demonstration (in "On Computable Numbers") that Hilbert's _Entscheidungsproblem_ (decision problem) has no general solution — meaning no algorithm can exist that determines, for every mathematical statement, whether it's provable or not. He proved this by formally defining what "computable" means, via what we now call the Turing machine.

**Turing machine** — The abstract mathematical model he invented to formally define computation. It's the theoretical foundation of all modern computing — every computer today is, in essence, a physical realization of this concept.

**Turing test** — His 1950 proposal for evaluating machine intelligence: a human evaluator has blind conversations with both a machine and a human, without knowing which is which; if the evaluator can't reliably tell them apart, the machine "passes."

**Unorganised machine** — A concept he introduced in 1948, describing networks of simple, randomly interconnected elements that could "learn" through a training process — considered an early conceptual precursor to artificial neural networks.

**Turing pattern** — Patterns that emerge from a system of two interacting, diffusing chemical substances (reaction-diffusion), which he proposed in 1952 to explain how natural patterns arise — like leopard spots or zebra stripes — from an initially uniform state.

**Turing reduction** — A concept in computability theory: problem A is "reducible" to problem B if a hypothetical algorithm solving B (an "oracle") could be used to solve A. It's a core tool for comparing the relative difficulty of computational problems.

**"The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis"** — The title of his 1952 paper introducing the reaction-diffusion equations behind Turing patterns, mentioned above. Considered a foundational work in mathematical biology.

**Turing paradox** — Related to the halting problem: he proved no general algorithm can exist that determines, for any given program and input, whether that program will finish running or continue forever. It's a direct consequence of his 1936 proof.
