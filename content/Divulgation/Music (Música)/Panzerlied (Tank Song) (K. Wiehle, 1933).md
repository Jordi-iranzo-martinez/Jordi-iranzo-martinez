Panzerlied was the anthem for Nazi Germany's armored forces (_Panzerwaffe_) during World War II.

It was composed by Oberleutnant Kurt Wiehle in 1933, using the tune of Luiska-Lied, an old German sailor's song.



German Panzers were absolutely monumental during World War II, not just because of their mechanical design, but because they completely revolutionized modern military doctrine and strategy.

Before WWII, most nations viewed tanks merely as slow, mobile shields meant to support infantry forces. The German military flipped this concept upside down, turning armored divisions into the literal tip of the spear.

During the invasions of Poland and France, the Panzers became the core element of Blitzkrieg ("Lightning War").

Instead of spreading tanks thinly among infantry units, Germany massed them into standalone Panzer Divisions. Every Panzer was equipped with a wireless radio operator. This allowed crews, tank platoons, and aircraft (like Stuka dive-bombers) to communicate in real-time, executing fast, synchronized flanking maneuvers that paralyzed enemy armies.

Panzer I and Panzer II were light, thinly-armored vehicles, but Germans had better coordination and centralized command.

When Germany launched Operation Barbarossa against the Soviet Union in 1941, the Panzers met a terrifying surprise: the Soviet T-34 and KV-1 tanks.

The standard German anti-tank weapons and the 37mm guns on the main workhorse, the Panzer III, completely bounced off the sloped armor of the T-34.

This forced Germany into a rapid engineering arms race. They extended the chassis of the Panzer IV to fit a long, high-velocity 75mm gun, which became the true, reliable backbone of the Wehrmacht for the rest of the war.

In response to the Soviet threat, Germany shifted its philosophy toward heavy, technologically superior engineering, giving birth to the legendary Panzer V (Panther) and Panzer VI (Tiger I & Tiger II).

While late-war Panzers like the Tiger and Panther could easily destroy Allied tanks (like the American M4 Sherman) from long distances, Germany lost the armored war due to industrial logistics and over-engineering.

While Germany meticulously hand-crafted around 1,300 Tiger I tanks, the United States mass-produced nearly 50,000 Shermans, and the Soviets built over 80,000 T-34s.

Late-war Panzers were notoriously prone to transmission breakdowns, required immense amounts of scarce fuel, and were incredibly difficult to repair in the field.

Panzers changed how every single modern army handles ground combat operations to this day
