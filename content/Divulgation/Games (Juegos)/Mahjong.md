Mahjong (or májiàng in Chinese) is a centuries-old, four-player tile-based game of skill, strategy, and calculation. Often compared to the Western card game rummy, it involves drawing and discarding tiles to assemble specific combinations.

*This traditional multiplayer strategy game should not be confused with Mahjong Solitaire, a popular single-player computer tile-matching puzzle.*

A standard traditional Mahjong set contains 144 tiles, featuring distinct suits and special honor symbols:

- **The Three Suits (108 tiles):** Numbered 1 through 9, with 4 copies of each tile.
    - **Bamboos (_Bams_):** Sticks resembling bamboo green stalks. The "1-Bam" is traditionally decorated as a bird.
    - **Characters (_Craks_):** Traditional Chinese numerical characters, often printed in red.
    - **Dots:** Circles originally representing ancient coins.
- **Honor Tiles (28 tiles):**
    - **Winds:** East, South, West, and North (4 of each).
    - **Dragons:** Red, Green, and White (often blank or depicted as a border).
- **Bonus Tiles (8 tiles):** Flowers and Seasons, which award luck-based extra points.

While rules differ by region, the foundational flow of traditional Mahjong remains consistent:

- **The Setup:** Tiles are shuffled face down and stacked into four protective walls. Players draw their starting hands (usually **13 tiles each**; the dealer starts with 14).

- **Turn Cycle:** Play proceeds in turns. On your turn, you draw a tile from the wall and then discard a tile from your hand face-up into the center of the table.

- **Claiming Discards:** Instead of drawing from the wall, a player can claim an opponent's _just-discarded_ tile to immediately complete a set. This requires revealing the completed set to the table.

- **Forming Melds (Sets):** Players strive to organize their hand into combinations:
    - **Pung:** Three identical tiles.
    - **Chow:** A numerical sequence of three tiles from the same suit (e.g., 4-5-6 Dots).
    - **Kong:** Four identical tiles.

- **Winning Hand:** The round ends when a player completes a legal 14-tile hand, consisting of **four sets (melds) and one identical pair**. The winning player shouts _"Mahjong!"_ to claim the round.

Over the past century, Mahjong has adapted into distinct localized versions:

- **Hong Kong / Cantonese Style:** The global baseline. It features balanced rules and quick scoring, making it highly dynamic and beginner-friendly.

- **Japanese Riichi Mahjong:** A highly complex, competitive variant. It introduces defensive rules, a points-betting phase when declaring a ready hand (_Riichi_), and hidden bonus tiles (_Dora_).

- **American Mahjong:** Standardized in the 1930s by the National Mahjong League. It adds **8 Wild Jokers** (making 152 tiles total) and changes its legal winning patterns every year via a mandatory rule card. It also features a pre-game tile-swapping ritual called "The Charleston".

The history of Mahjong is a mix of rapid evolutionary changes and clever marketing. While popular folklore often attributes the game to the ancient philosopher Confucius around 500 BCE, historians have proven that Mahjong is actually a relatively modern creation.

After World War I, American expats living in Shanghai discovered the game.

An American businessman named **Joseph P. Babcock** began translating the rules and importing standard sets to the United States in the early 1920s.

To make the game feel alluring and exotic, marketing campaigns falsely advertised Mahjong as a "mystical, thousands-of-years-old secret of the royal Forbidden Chinese courts".

The marketing worked flawlessly. It became an overnight obsession across America and Europe. High-society elites, Hollywood celebrities, and even President Warren G. Harding played it regularly. Retailers like Abercrombie & Fitch sold thousands of luxury imported sets.

While the initial 1920s "exotic fad" eventually cooled down among the general public, it found a deep, permanent home within the Jewish-American community.

- **Standardization (1937):** In New York, a group of Jewish women established the National Mah Jongg League (NMJL) to streamline the chaotic, conflicting rules brought over from China.

- **Suburban Culture:** In the 1950s and 1960s, as middle-class families moved to the suburbs, weekly Mahjong nights became an essential social ritual for young mothers to build community, raise charity funds, and establish lifelong friendships. This tradition continues to be passed down through generations today.

During China's Cultural Revolution in the mid-20th century, the game was temporarily banned as a symbol of gambling and old society. However, its popularity returned in full force in the late 1980s.

Today, Mahjong is experiencing a massive global resurgence. Online platforms have digitized the traditional 4-player rules, while highly competitive versions like Japanese Riichi Mahjong have turned it into an international mind sport with formal world championships.
