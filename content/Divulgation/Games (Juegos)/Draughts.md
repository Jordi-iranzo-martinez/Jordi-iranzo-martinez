It is essentially a game created by merging an ancient Middle Eastern strategy game with a European chessboard

Before there were squares, there were lines. The grandfather of Checkers is an ancient game called **Alquerque** (originally _El-Quirkat_ in Arabic). [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESawHrOzAVyM4xSqb8GRMi5r3lhApJriaKWJSYOh606N4fF9kXfvaU5qo-aLmg3fbgbJ81pdsi6ODV1y5w7c80AAvQUfHOViB8S0Mh1HnWd1FiPDcZhLy6o_QrxY9dWK3Lk--dEjFD9W4hblHp)]

- It was played in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia on a grid of lines and intersecting points.
- Just like in modern Checkers, players had 12 pieces each, moved them across the board, and **captured enemy pieces by jumping over them** into an empty space.

Around the 12th century, somewhere in southern France or Spain, an unknown inventor had a brilliant idea: why not play Alquerque using the pieces and grid of a standard chessboard ([[Chess]])?

- The pieces were placed only on the dark squares of the 8x8 grid.
- The game was originally called _Fierges_ or _Ferses_ (the medieval name for the chess Queen).
- Because the game was highly popular as a social pastime among noblewomen, it eventually became known across Europe as **Le Jeu de Dames** (The Game of Ladies)—which is why it is called **Damas** in Spanish, Portuguese, and Italian today. 

Spain played a massive role in cementing the game's legacy.

- In **1547**, in [[Valencia]], a Spaniard named **Antonio de Torquemada** published the **very first book in the world about Damas**.

- Historians note that the rise of the game's popularity closely matched the rise of the ultra-powerful Queen (Dama) in Spanish chess under Queen Isabella of Castile. It is during this era that the concept of a promoted piece becoming a powerful **"Flying King"** (or _Dama_) became a core mechanic in the Spanish variant.

Originally, capturing an opponent's piece was optional—you could choose a normal move instead. Around 1535, French players introduced a rule that completely changed the tactical depth: capturing became mandatory.

If you failed to see a jump or refused to take it, your opponent could penalize you by "huffing" (blowing on your piece and removing it from the board). This forced-capture rule turned Damas into a deep mathematical game of sacrifices and traps.

As the game spread, different countries formalized their own localized rule sets:

British Draughts / American Checkers: Brought to North America by European settlers. It kept the traditional 8x8 board but made the Kings "slow" (they can only move one square backward or forward).

**International Draughts:** Emerged in Paris around 1720. The board was expanded to a larger **10x10 grid with 20 pieces per side**, introducing long-range "flying kings" and backward capturing for regular pieces. This remains the dominant competitive version in Europe and Africa today.
