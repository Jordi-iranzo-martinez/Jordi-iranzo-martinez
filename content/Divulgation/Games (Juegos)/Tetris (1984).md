Tetris is one of the most iconic, best-selling, and culturally influential video games in history. It was originally designed and programmed by Soviet software engineer Alexey Pajitnov on June 6, 1984. Its name is a portmanteau blending the Greek prefix tetra- (meaning four, as every piece has four segments) with tennis, Pajitnov's favorite sport.

The fundamental principle of Tetris stands out for its absolute simplicity, making it a masterpiece of mathematical logic:

- **The Tetrominoes:** The geometric pieces falling from the top of the screen are called tetrominoes. There are exactly 7 distinct shapes in the standard game (identified by the letters I, J, L, O, S, T, and Z), and every single one is composed of **4 identical squares** arranged in different configurations.

- **Clearing Lines:** The player must rotate and slide the pieces horizontally as they drop to fit them perfectly into the grid below. When a full horizontal row is filled with solid blocks and has no empty gaps, that line **vanishes from the screen**, awarding points and opening up space to keep playing.

- **The "Tetris":** Clearing **four lines simultaneously** using the long, straight "I" piece is the ultimate move in the game, traditionally known as scoring a _"Tetris"_.

- **Game Over (Topping Out):** As lines are cleared, the game automatically steps up in level, increasing the gravity and dropping speed of the pieces. The game ends when blocks stack up messily all the way to the top boundary of the screen.


- **Born in the USSR:** Pajitnov developed the initial prototype on a Soviet terminal computer called the _Electronika 60_ while working at the Academy of Sciences in Moscow. Because the machine had no advanced graphics capabilities, he had to use ordinary text brackets `[ ]` to form the blocks.

- **The Battle for Rights:** Because it was programmed under the communist regime, the legal rights to the game initially belonged to the Soviet government via a state-run agency called _Elorg_. This triggered a thrilling sequence of corporate espionage and intellectual property legal battles throughout the late 1980s as Western companies raced to secure the global distribution rights.

- **The Game Boy Phenomenon (1989):** Entrepreneur Henk Rogers successfully brokered the handheld console rights for Nintendo. By bundling a copy of Tetris with every single original **Game Boy** console sold worldwide, the game became an overnight international phenomenon and defined the success of portable gaming.

Tetris has transcended screen boundaries to deeply embed itself into popular culture. It gave rise to scientific case studies like the "Tetris Effect" (the psychological phenomenon where players who play for hours begin involuntarily visualizing how real-world objects fit together), and it has inspired high-budget biopic films and indie documentaries.


- **Classic Tetris World Championship (CTWC):** Every year, players gather globally to compete on the original 1989 NES version of the game, creating a massive competitive esport scene.

- **"Crashing" the Game:** In December 2023, a 13-year-old American player named Willis Gibson (_Blue Scuti_) made gaming history by becoming the first human ever to trigger the game's functional "True Kill Screen," causing the code to freeze on Level 157—a feat that inspired the popular documentary [_Blue Scuti: Tetris Crasher_](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESVwHrOzAVB-jk-nXUxfspAy2FYQNjTrXKcilsA-teyRB9I7yOlVlHwHRlol_vmorXXhpcZKjfS44cOYFjnSl6g4l75LkkDRuBAVUROeD5FnE7up4EB63Lvg).
