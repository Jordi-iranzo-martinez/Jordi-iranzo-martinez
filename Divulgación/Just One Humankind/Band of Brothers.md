E Company, from the 2nd Battalion of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment of the 101st Airborne Division, the "Screaming Eagles", is a [company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_\(military_unit\) "Company (military unit)") in the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army).

Herbert Maxwell Sobel was their commissioned officer. Known as a tough, strict, and divisive training commander, Sobel was ultimately replaced as company commander prior to D-Day due to his inadequacy in the field.

By the time Easy Company had transferred to Camp Mackall, North Carolina, in February 1943, Sobel's shortcomings as a field commander became apparent. During exercises, his lack of spatial awareness, physicality, and smart decision making made his men concerned about his ability to lead them in battle. "I am going into combat with this man. Screaming Eagles


The company formed in 1942 at Camp Toccoa, Georgia, under the notoriously demanding Captain Herbert Sobel.

Sobel's harsh, sometimes petty discipline was miserable to endure, but it forged a tightly bonded, physically tough unit — even as it drove a rift between him and his officers. That tension came to a head in what's known as the "Toccoa mutiny," when several NCOs refused promotions and demanded Sobel's removal; he was eventually reassigned, and First Lieutenant Richard Winters took over as one of the company's key leaders.

Easy Company jumped into Normandy in the early hours of D-Day, June 6, 1944. Right after, Winters gathered about a dozen men and used them to knock out a German battery of four 105mm guns dug in behind hedgerows, that were raking Utah Beach.

Using flanking maneuvers and small fire teams to make the attacking force seem larger than it was, they took out all four guns and inflicted heavy casualties despite being badly outnumbered. It's still taught at West Point as a model of small-unit tactics.

Winters had roughly 12–13 men against a fortified German position of more than 50 troops dug in with four 105mm howitzers connected by trenches, plus machine gun emplacements.

The tactic was first reconnaissancce to map out the trench line and gun positions, then put a machine gun team to pin the Germans down, while assault teams moved along covered a hedgerow and ditch to hit the flank.

His men cleared one gun emplacement, and then used it as a new base of fire to support the assault on the next one. Each captured position became the launch point for the next attack.

By moving aggressively and attacking from an unexpected angle, the small force made the Germans believe they faced a much larger unit, which helped break their will to hold.

By the end, all four guns were destroyed, and Easy Company had inflicted disproportionate casualties on the German garrison for very light losses. It's taught at West Point specifically as a model of the "reconnaissance, fire and maneuver, defeat in detail" approach against a fixed position with a badly outnumbered force.

Days after Normandy, Easy Company took part in the fight to secure the town of Carentan, a critical road junction linking the D-Day beaches. The advance down a long, exposed causeway toward the town (nicknamed "Purple Heart Lane") was brutal, with the company taking heavy fire in the open before finally pushing the Germans out.

Winters was awarded with the Distinguished Service Cross (DSC) for his command of the assault on Brécourt Manor. It is the second highest military decoration for soldiers who displayed extraordinary heroism in combat with an armed enemy force, only after the Medal of Honor.

He stood out because of his discipline and preparation, calm under pressure, moral seriousness and a well execution of textbook application.

Easy Company jumped into Holland as part of the massive airborne operation to seize a corridor of bridges into Germany. They helped liberate Eindhoven, and fought a rough engagement near the village of Nuenen against German armor, one of the tougher fights of the campaign for the unit, where they took real losses.

They also held defensive positions along the Rhine floodplain in the Netherlands ("the Island"), enduring weeks of static, miserable fighting in flooded terrain, artillery, and patrols. It was some of their hardest, most exhausting duty.

Rushed into Bastogne (Belgium) without proper winter gear, ammunition, or supplies, dug into the snowy Bois Jacques woods and held the line under relentless artillery bombardment during the German Ardennes offensive. Conditions were brutal (frostbite, trench foot, shortages...). The fighting culminated in the assault on the town of Foy (January 1945): a costly, chaotic attack that became a defining test of leadership within the company (and a low point for one particular officer, Lt. Dike, whose hesitation under fire is a major plot point in the series).

Easy Company held a defensive line along the Moder River near Haguenau, France, conducting risky night patrols across the river into German-held territory, then advanced into Germany itself as the war wound down.

As the war ended, Easy Company reached Hitler's Bavarian retreat a *Berchtesgaden*, Hitler's mountain retreat in Bavaria, and the nearby *Kehlsteinhaus* (the "Eagle's Nest").
