Le Chat Noir opened in Montmartre in 1881, founded by Rodolphe Salis, and it set the template: a bohemian crowd, satirical songs, shadow puppet shows, and Salis himself acting as a kind of proto-emcee, mocking his own customers as they walked in.

Montmartre at the time was full of cheap rent and cheap wine, which drew poets, painters, and anarchists together in the same rooms.

The Moulin Rouge (1889) leaned more toward can-can and spectacle than the intimate satire of Chat Noir, showing how quickly the form started to branch.

Cabaret is a style of entertainment—and the type of venue it happens in—that mixes music, song, dance, comedy, and sometimes theater into a single intimate show.

Cabaret traditionally happens in a small, intimate space —a nightclub, restaurant, or bar— rather than a formal theater. Audiences usually sit at tables, often eating or drinking during the performance, and there's little separation between performers and crowd: personal and spontaneous rather than polished and distant.

The show is typically a varieties spectacle: a sequence of different acts (a singer, a comedian, a dancer, maybe a magician or drag performer...) stitched together, often by an emcee (a "compère") who banters with the audience between numbers.

It is flexible: the same venue might host wildly different lineups night to night.

Cabaret emerged in late 19th-century Paris (Le Chat Noir, 1881, is often cited as the first true cabaret) as a place where artists, writers, and performers could be irreverent, satirical, and sexually frank in ways more formal theater wouldn't allow.

That spirit carried into Weimar-era Berlin in the 1920s and early 30s, where cabaret became a vehicle for political satire and social commentary.


Ïn Weimar Berlin cabarets like Schall und Rauch mixed sharp political sketches with music from composers like Kurt Weill and Friedrich Hollaender.

It was a genuinely dangerous kind of comedy, mocking politicians and social hypocrisy at a moment when Germany's politics were curdling badly.

When the Nazis took power in 1933, cabaret was an early target: many performers were Jewish, queer, leftist, or all three, and the genre's whole ethos was incompatible with the regime.

Vienna had its own literary cabaret scene (Nachtlicht, Fledermaus) with a more absurdist, dadaist bent.

New York developed supper clubs and piano bars in the 20th century: a lineage that runs through Cole Porter–style songbook performance into today's cabaret rooms.

Modern cabaret is genuinely fragmented in burlesque revivales (tease-and-spectacle), Drag cabaret (gender-bending irreverence), piano bar and jazz cabaret (intimate singer-songwriter tradition) and dinner theater (variety-show structure).


The Weimar Constitution created a semi-presidential system: a Chancellor led the government day-to-day, but the President held significant emergency powers under Article 48, which let him rule by decree "to restore public order" without needing parliamentary approval. This clause was meant as a safety valve for crises, but it became a loophole that Hitler exploited once in power — it's a big part of how democratic institutions got dismantled from the inside rather than overthrown outright.

The *Reichstag* (German Parliament) was elected by proportional representation. That fragmented politics into dozens of small parties. Coalition governments were constantly forming and collapsing: the republic went through over 20 different Chancellors in 14 years.

By the Treaty of Versailles (1919) was a peace treaty signed on 28 June 1919. As the most important treaty of World War I, it ended the state of war between Germany and most of the Allied Powers. 

It was signed in the Palace of Versailles, exactly five years after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the proximate cause of the war. The other Central Powers on the German side signed separate treaties.[iii] Although the armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the actual fighting, and agreed certain principles and conditions including the payment of reparations, it took six months of Allied negotiations at the Paris Peace Conference to conclude the peace treaty. After the Versailles Peace (1919), the Republic was founded. In 1920 right-wing paramilitaries did The Kapp Putsch: an attempt to seize Berlin which was defeated by a general strike. Between 1924 and 1929 there was an stabilization period under Gustav Stresemann, a new currency, American loans (Dawes Plans), and relative prosperity.

However, during the Wall Street Crash in 1929, American loans get recalled and the economy collapsed again. Unemployment soars past 6 million by 1932.

- **Burlesque revivals** (Dita Von Teese being the most visible figure) lean into the tease-and-spectacle side.
- **Drag cabaret** carries forward the gender-bending irreverence that Weimar cabaret also had, especially through emcee/host figures.
- **Piano bar / jazz cabaret** keeps the more intimate, singer-songwriter tradition alive — this is closest to what people mean by "cabaret singer" as a vocal style.
- **Dinner theater** versions strip out most of the edge and keep the variety-show structure and audience proximity.

- **Weimar's legacy in musical theater** shows up directly in Kander and Ebb's _Cabaret_ (1966) and Weill/Brecht's _The Threepenny Opera_, which borrows cabaret's cynicism and directness even though it's staged as book theater.


Many cabarets of that era were shut down or performers persecuted once the Nazis came to power, because of their genre's boundary-pushing nature.

Today "cabaret" covers a lot of ground: burlesque shows, drag cabaret, jazz or piano bar performances, comedy cabaret, and dinner-theater variety shows. What ties them together is usually that mix of intimacy, variety, a bit of irreverence, and direct engagement with the audience.
